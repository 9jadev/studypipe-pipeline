Practical analysis questions

Answer these five questions, then compare with the model answers.

  1. Why does the artery wall need so much elastic tissue?
  2. Why do veins need valves but arteries (beyond the heart) do not?
  3. At which end of the capillary does fluid leave, and why?
  4. What would happen to NFP at the venous end if Pc there rose to 30 mmHg?
  5. Why are red cells and most plasma proteins kept inside?
Model answers unlocks after you try
  1. It takes the surge of blood from the ventricle and then springs back, which smooths the flow and keeps blood moving between beats (felt as the pulse).
  2. Veins carry blood at low pressure, often against gravity, so pocket valves stop it falling back. Arteries carry high-pressure blood pushed by the heart, so they need none.
  3. The arterial end, because Pc (36 mmHg) is greater than πc (25 mmHg), so NFP is positive and fluid is pressed out.
  4. NFP would be 30 − 25 = +5 mmHg: still outward, so fluid would no longer be drawn back at that end.
  5. They are too big to squeeze through the gaps between the wall cells, so only water and small dissolved substances leave.

Deoxygenated blood is dark red. Veins only look blue through the skin.

Three adaptations

Arteries: elastic, muscular walls take the surge from the ventricle and spring back, which keeps blood moving (felt as the pulse). Veins: pocket valves let blood move only towards the heart, and contracting leg muscles squeeze the veins between them (the muscle pump). Capillaries: walls one cell thick give a short diffusion path; their huge number gives a vast surface; slow flow gives time for exchange.

How tissue fluid forms and returns

Tissue fluid: near the start of a capillary (its arterial end), the pressure of the blood (Pc) is greater than the inward pull of plasma proteins (πc), so water with dissolved oxygen, glucose, amino acids and salts is squeezed out between the cells as tissue fluid; red cells and large proteins stay behind. Cells absorb what they need and add carbon dioxide and wastes. At the venous end Pc has fallen below πc, so most of the fluid is drawn back in by osmosis. The rest seeps into lymph capillaries, which are closed at one end; once inside, it is known as lymph.

NFP = Pc − πc   tissue-side pressures treated as near zero
Q = A × v, so v = Q ÷ A   the same flow over a far larger total area moves far more slowly

4Apply · Worked examples

Worked examples, step by step

Attempt each problem on paper first, then check the working one line at a time.

EXAMPLE 1

NFP at both ends of a skeletal-muscle capillary

Pc is 36 mmHg at the arterial end and 16 mmHg at the venous end; πc is 25 mmHg along the whole length. Find NFP at each end and where it becomes zero.

  1. Formula: NFP = Pc − πc.
  2. Arterial end: 36 − 25 = +11 mmHg (outward: filtration).
  3. Venous end: 16 − 25 = −9 mmHg (inward: reabsorption).
  4. Zero point: Pc must equal 25, a fall of 36 − 25 = 11 out of 36 − 16 = 20; 11 ÷ 20 = 0.55.
+11 mmHg out, −9 mmHg in; filtration turns to reabsorption 55% of the way along
EXAMPLE 2

Why capillary blood is so slow

Blood leaves the aorta (cross-section 2.4 cm²) at 35 cm s⁻¹. All capillaries together have a cross-section of about 2400 cm². Find the speed in the capillaries.

  1. Flow: Q = A₁ × v₁ = 2.4 × 35 = 84 cm³ s⁻¹.
  2. Check it is sensible: 84 × 60 = 5040 cm³ min⁻¹ ≈ 5.0 dm³ min⁻¹, a resting cardiac output.
  3. Capillary speed: v₂ = Q ÷ A₂ = 84 ÷ 2400 = 0.035 cm s⁻¹ (0.35 mm s⁻¹).
  4. Compare: 35 ÷ 0.035 = 1000.
About 0.035 cm s⁻¹, about a thousand times slower than in the aorta, which gives time for exchange (textbooks quote about 0.03 cm s⁻¹)
EXAMPLE 3

Mama Bisi's leg

After hours of standing, with faulty vein valves, Pc at the venous end of her ankle capillaries rises to 30 mmHg (arterial end still 36). πc is 25 mmHg.

  1. Arterial end: 36 − 25 = +11 mmHg (out).
  2. Venous end: 30 − 25 = +5 mmHg (still out).
  3. Conclusion: NFP is positive along the whole capillary, so there is no reabsorption zone; all the extra fluid must leave by lymph, which cannot keep up.
Fluid collects in the tissues: oedema
EXAMPLE 4

Lymph flow

About 20 dm³ of fluid is filtered from capillaries each day (outside the kidneys) and about 17 dm³ is reabsorbed (approximate textbook values). Find the lymph flow in cm³ per hour.

  1. Left over: 20 − 17 = 3 dm³ per day.
  2. Convert: 3 dm³ = 3000 cm³.
  3. Per hour: 3000 ÷ 24 = 125 cm³ h⁻¹.
About 125 cm³ h⁻¹ (15% of what is filtered)

Part A · Home investigation: Which way do the valves in your veins let blood go?

Which way do the valves in your veins let blood go?

Home investigation

You will use the veins on the back of your hand and forearm to show that vein valves let blood flow only towards the heart, and you will time how fast a vein refills.

You need: your own arm (or a family member's), a phone stopwatch, a ballpoint pen that writes on skin (or a small piece of masking tape), a chair, your notebook and pencil.

Safety: Press gently: you only need enough pressure to flatten the vein. Stop if anything hurts or tingles. Do not tie anything tightly round the arm. Wash off any pen marks afterwards.

Steps:

  1. Sit down and let one arm hang straight down beside the chair for 1 minute, so the veins on the back of the hand and the forearm stand out. Look along a straight vein for small knots or bulges; mark each one lightly with the pen. These are often the sites of valves.
  2. Press one fingertip firmly on the vein just below a bulge (the hand side). Keep pressing, and with a second finger stroke the blood in the vein away from the first finger towards the elbow (towards the heart), then lift the second finger only. Note whether the emptied stretch of vein stays flat.
  3. Now lift the first finger and start the stopwatch. Time how long the flat stretch takes to fill again. Repeat three times and take the mean.
  4. Repeat Step 2, but this time try to stroke the blood the other way, from the elbow side towards the hand (away from the heart). Note what you feel and see.
  5. Repeat Steps 2–3 with the arm raised above the shoulder for 1 minute first. Record the refill time.

What a good answer shows: the learner concludes that the valves are pocket valves that open only towards the heart and shut when blood tries to flow back, which is why the stroked stretch stays empty and why blood cannot be pushed towards the hand; the bulges are explained as valve sites; the slower refill with the arm raised is linked to blood returning against gravity, and then to Mama Bisi's legs: when she stands still for hours there is no muscle pump, and if her valves leak, blood pools in her lower legs; the learner suggests at least one practical remedy (raising the feet, walking about to work the calf muscles); the results are in a ruled table with units and means.

Part B · Written paper (30 marks)

1. Definitions

3 marks

Define each of the following: (a) tissue fluid; (b) lymph; (c) oedema.

Model solution and mark scheme unlocks after you try

Model solution: (a) Tissue fluid is the watery liquid, pressed out of the blood in capillaries, that surrounds and bathes body cells; it carries dissolved food and oxygen to them and wastes away from them, but holds no red cells and very little protein (1). (b) Lymph is the surplus tissue fluid that has drained into the lymph capillaries and is carried in the lymphatic vessels back towards the blood (1). (c) Oedema is the swelling of a body part caused by tissue fluid building up between the cells faster than it is removed (1). Equivalent wordings that keep the key ideas are credited.

Mark scheme note: "Tissue fluid is blood outside the vessels" scores zero: it has no red cells. For (b), the answer must say the fluid is inside lymph vessels. For (c), "swelling" alone, without the cause, is not credited.

3. Capillary adaptations

3 marks

State three ways in which capillaries are suited to the exchange of materials with body cells, explaining each.

Model solution and mark scheme unlocks after you try

Model solution: Any three (1 each, feature and reason together): the wall is only one cell thick, so the diffusion path between blood and cells is very short · there are huge numbers of capillaries, giving a very large total surface area for exchange · blood flows very slowly through them, giving time for exchange · they are so narrow that red cells pass in single file close to the wall, which shortens the path for oxygen · they reach close to every living cell, so no cell is far from a supply.

Mark scheme note: A feature without its reason scores zero ("they are thin" alone). Do not credit "capillaries have valves" or "capillaries have thick walls to withstand pressure".

4. Formation and return of tissue fluid

4 marks

Describe how tissue fluid is formed at a capillary bed and how it returns to the blood.

Model solution and mark scheme unlocks after you try

Model solution: At the arterial end, the blood pressure is greater than the osmotic pull of the plasma proteins, so water and small dissolved substances such as glucose, amino acids, oxygen and salts are forced out between the wall cells (1). Red cells and most plasma proteins are too large to pass and stay in the blood (1). At the venous end, the blood pressure has fallen below the protein pull, so most of the fluid, now carrying carbon dioxide and wastes from the cells, is drawn back into the capillary by osmosis (1). The fluid left over seeps into lymph capillaries, which are closed at one end, and is returned to the blood as lymph (1).

Mark scheme note: Each mark point is separate: a candidate who only says "pressure forces fluid out and osmosis brings it back" scores 2 at most. "Red cells leave the capillary" loses the second mark.

5. Calculation: net filtration pressure

6 marks

In a capillary of the gut wall, Pc falls evenly from 32 mmHg at the arterial end to 14 mmHg at the venous end. The osmotic pressure of the plasma proteins (πc) is 26 mmHg all along. Take NFP = Pc − πc.
(a) Calculate NFP at the arterial end and state the direction of fluid movement. (1 mark)
(b) Calculate NFP at the venous end and state the direction of fluid movement. (1 mark)
(c) Calculate what fraction of the way along the capillary NFP becomes zero. (2 marks)
(d) In someone whose vein valves leak, like Mama Bisi, the venous-end pressure rises to 28 mmHg. Calculate the new venous-end NFP and explain the effect on the tissues. (2 marks)

Model solution and mark scheme unlocks after you try

Model solution: (a) 32 − 26 = +6 mmHg; fluid moves out of the capillary (1). (b) 14 − 26 = −12 mmHg; fluid moves into the capillary (1). (c) NFP is zero when Pc = 26 mmHg, a fall of 32 − 26 = 6 mmHg out of a total fall of 32 − 14 = 18 mmHg (1); 6/18 = 1/3, so one third (0.33) of the way along (1). (d) 28 − 26 = +2 mmHg (1). NFP is now positive at both ends, so fluid leaves along the whole capillary and none is reabsorbed; the extra fluid builds up faster than lymph can drain it, so the tissue swells (oedema) (1).

Mark scheme note: A value without its direction loses the mark in (a) and (b). In (c), the first mark is for the two pressure falls, the second for the fraction; "18 out of 32" is a common slip and scores zero. In (d), the second mark needs the link to no reabsorption, not just "the leg swells".

Model solution and mark scheme unlocks after you try

Model solution: (a) Artery: wall 32 × 0.025 = 0.80 mm, lumen 96 × 0.025 = 2.4 mm (1). Vein: wall 14 × 0.025 = 0.35 mm, lumen 152 × 0.025 = 3.8 mm (1). (b) Artery 0.80/2.4 = 0.33; vein 0.35/3.8 = 0.092; the artery has a far thicker wall for its size, to withstand and smooth out the high, pulsing pressure from the ventricle, while the vein's wide lumen and thin wall suit slow, low-pressure flow back to the heart (1). (c) The lumen of the vein read too low, because the collapsed walls had moved closer together (1).

Mark scheme note: In (a), each mark needs both readings for that vessel, with mm units. In (b), the ratios alone without an explanation score zero. In (c), "the reading was wrong" with no direction scores zero.

7. The lymphatic system

3 marks

(a) Trace the path of lymph from a lymph capillary until it rejoins the blood. (1 mark)
(b) Explain how lymph keeps moving although the lymphatic system has no pump. (1 mark)
(c) State one function of the lymphatic system other than returning tissue fluid to the blood. (1 mark)

Model solution and mark scheme unlocks after you try

Model solution: (a) Lymph capillary → larger lymph vessels with valves, passing through lymph nodes → thoracic duct (or right lymphatic duct) → subclavian vein near the neck (1). (b) Contracting skeletal muscles and breathing movements squeeze the lymph vessels, and their valves let lymph move only one way, towards the neck (1). (c) Any one: carries fats absorbed by the lacteals of the ileum · lymph nodes filter out bacteria and debris · lymph nodes make lymphocytes, which help fight infection (1).

Mark scheme note: In (a), ending the route "in the heart" or "in the kidney" scores zero. In (b), "the heart pumps it" is the commonest wrong answer.
