Precautions and sources of error

Precautions taken (write them in the past tense): the pulse was felt with two fingers, not the thumb, which has its own pulse · the subject rested for five minutes before the resting count · each resting count was repeated three times and averaged · the stopwatch was started on a beat counted as “zero”.

Sources of error (always give the direction): counting the first beat as “one” when the watch started added a beat, so the rate was too high by 4 beats per minute · a short delay before the first post-exercise count let the heart slow, so the peak was too low · multiplying a 15 s count by 4 turns one missed or extra beat into ±4 beats per minute, so a reading may be too high or too low (random) · pressing too hard on the artery blocked some beats, so the rate read too low.

3Make sense · 15 min

One heartbeat, start to finish

Now we connect what you counted to the names and timings WAEC, NECO and JAMB expect.

Movement break (45 min mark, 5 minutes): stand up and find your own radial pulse with two fingers. Count for 15 seconds, then jog on the spot for 30 seconds and count again. Multiply each by 4. Did your rate rise? Say “lub” as you tap your chest on every beat and “dub” on the short gap between. Sit down slowly and breathe until your pulse feels calm again.

The cardiac cycle (at about 75 beats per minute, one cycle ≈ 0.8 s):

  1. All chambers relaxed (about 0.4 s): blood flows in from the venae cavae and pulmonary veins, fills the atria and runs on through the open AV valves into the ventricles. The semilunar valves are shut.
  2. Atria contract (about 0.1 s): both atria squeeze together and top up the ventricles.
  3. Ventricles contract (about 0.3 s): pressure in the ventricles rises above that in the atria and the AV valves slam shut (lub). It then passes the pressure in the arteries, the semilunar valves open and blood surges into the aorta and pulmonary artery.
  4. Ventricles relax: pressure inside them falls below the arteries, blood starts to fall back, and the semilunar pockets fill and snap shut (dub). The cycle repeats.

Systole means contraction; diastole means relaxation.

Heart sounds

SoundCauseCharacter
Lub (first)AV valves (tricuspid, bicuspid) closing as the ventricles start to squeezedeeper and longer
Dub (second)Semilunar valves closing as the ventricles relaxshorter and sharper

A murmur, an extra swishing sound, hints at a leaky or narrowed valve.

Control of the beat

SAN (pacemaker, right atrium wall) → both atria contract → AVN (short delay) → bundle of His (down the septum) → Purkinje fibres (up both ventricle walls, from the apex)

Cardiac muscle is myogenic: the beat begins inside the heart. The cardiac centre in the medulla oblongata only adjusts the rate. Sympathetic nerves and the hormone adrenaline raise the rate, while impulses along the vagus nerve lower it. The short delay at the AVN gives the atria time to finish emptying before the ventricles contract.

Pulse and blood pressure: a pulse is the rhythmic bulge and spring-back of an artery's wall after every push of the left ventricle into the aorta, so the pulse rate equals the heart rate. It is felt where an artery crosses a bone near the skin: wrist (radial), neck (carotid), temple, groin. Blood pressure is the push of blood against vessel walls, measured with a sphygmomanometer. A typical young adult reads about 120/80 mmHg (ventricles contracting / relaxed). It peaks in the aorta and drops steadily all the way back to the heart.

Four equations to know

heart rate = beats in t × 60 ÷ t
T = 60 ÷ heart rate
cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume
% change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100

Factors that change the heart rate

Faster: exercise, fear or excitement (adrenaline), fever, high carbon dioxide in the blood, caffeine. Slower: sleep, rest, fitness (resting rate), vagus nerve impulses, lying calm.

Worked examples, step by step

Try each one on paper first, then check the working. Write the units in every line.

EXAMPLE 1

Heart rate and cycle time

At rest, Chidi's pulse gives 18 beats in 15 s. Find his heart rate and the length of one cycle.

  1. Scale to one minute: 18 × 60 ÷ 15 = 18 × 4 = 72 beats min⁻¹
  2. Cycle time: T = 60 s ÷ 72
  3. Evaluate: T = 0.833… ≈ 0.83 s
72 beats min⁻¹; about 0.83 s per cycle (close to the textbook 0.8 s).
EXAMPLE 2

Cardiac output at rest and after the sprint

Stroke volume is 70 cm³ at rest (68 bpm) and 95 cm³ just after the sprint (132 bpm). Find each cardiac output in dm³ min⁻¹ and how many times it rose.

  1. Rest: 68 × 70 = 4760 cm³ min⁻¹
  2. Convert: 4760 ÷ 1000 = 4.76 dm³ min⁻¹
  3. After the sprint: 132 × 95 = 12540 cm³ min⁻¹ = 12.54 dm³ min⁻¹
  4. Ratio: 12.54 ÷ 4.76 = 2.63 ≈ 2.6
4.76 → 12.5 dm³ min⁻¹: about 2.6 times more blood each minute.
EXAMPLE 3

Percentage rise

By what percentage did Chidi's heart rate rise from 68 to 132 bpm?

  1. Rise: 132 − 68 = 64 beats min⁻¹
  2. Fraction of the old value: 64 ÷ 68 = 0.941
  3. Percentage: 0.941 × 100 = 94.1 %
A 94.1% rise (almost double).
EXAMPLE 4

How long the ventricles rest

At 75 bpm, ventricular systole lasts 0.30 s. What fraction of each cycle do the ventricles spend relaxing?

  1. Cycle time: 60 ÷ 75 = 0.80 s
  2. Relaxing time: 0.80 − 0.30 = 0.50 s
  3. Fraction: 0.50 ÷ 0.80 × 100 = 62.5 %
62.5% of every cycle is rest, which is why heart muscle does not tire.

Part A · Home investigation: does posture or a short climb change your pulse?

Measure your pulse lying, sitting, standing and after a short climb, then see how quickly it settles.

You need: a phone or wall clock with a seconds hand (or the phone stopwatch), a mat or bed, a flight of stairs or a sturdy low step, a pencil and your notebook.

Safety: anyone with asthma, sickle cell disease or a known heart condition should only take the resting readings and must not do the climb. Climb at a steady pace, hold the rail, and stop at once if you feel dizzy or short of breath. Do not climb straight after a heavy meal.

  1. Lie still for 3 minutes. Place your index and middle fingers (not the thumb) on the thumb side of the inside of the wrist and count the beats for 15 s. Repeat twice more and take the mean.
  2. Sit up and wait 1 minute; take three 15 s counts again. Then stand still for 1 minute and repeat.
  3. Climb up and down the stairs (or step up and down the low step) steadily for 1 minute.
  4. Count for 15 s at once, then again at 1, 2, 3 and 4 minutes, sitting quietly between counts.
  5. Multiply each count by 4 to get beats per minute. Draw a ruled table, then plot heart rate (y) against time after the climb (x) and join the points with a smooth curve.

A good answer shows that the rate is lowest lying down and rises a little on sitting and standing, because on standing blood tends to pool in the legs and the heart beats faster to keep enough blood reaching the brain. The climb raises the rate sharply because working leg muscles need more oxygen and glucose and produce more carbon dioxide, and adrenaline and sympathetic nerves speed up the SAN. Recovery is fast at first and then slows, so the points lie on a curve, not a straight line. The learner states the recovery time (where the curve meets the resting rate), notes at least one source of error with its direction (for example, a delay before the first count makes the peak too low; one miscounted beat becomes an error of 4 beats min⁻¹ after multiplying), and uses a ruled table with units in the column headings.

Part B · Written paper (30 marks)

1. Definitions

3 marks

Define each of the following: (a) pulse; (b) blood pressure; (c) cardiac output.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) A pulse is the regular throb felt in an artery as its wall stretches and then springs back each time the left ventricle forces blood into the aorta. (1) (b) Blood pressure is the force with which blood pushes against the walls of the vessels it flows through, measured in mmHg. (1) (c) Cardiac output is the volume of blood pumped out by one ventricle in one minute; it equals heart rate × stroke volume. (1)

Mark scheme note: For (a), “the beating of the heart” scores zero: the pulse is felt in an artery, not in the heart. For (c), “the blood in the heart” or a volume with no time (“per minute”) is not credited.

2. The cardiac cycle

5 marks

At a resting rate of about 75 beats per minute, one heartbeat lasts about 0.8 s. Describe the stages of one cardiac cycle, stating what happens to the valves at each stage.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(1) While all four chambers are relaxed (about 0.4 s), blood from the venae cavae and pulmonary veins fills the atria and flows on through the open AV valves into the ventricles; the semilunar valves are closed. (2) The two atria then contract together (about 0.1 s) and push the last of their blood into the ventricles. (3) Next the ventricles contract (about 0.3 s); as their pressure rises above that in the atria, the tricuspid and bicuspid valves close. (4) When ventricular pressure passes the pressure in the arteries, the semilunar valves open and blood is driven into the aorta and pulmonary artery. (5) The ventricles then relax; their pressure falls below that in the arteries and the semilunar valves close, stopping blood from flowing back. One mark for each numbered point.

Mark scheme note: The stages must be in the right order. Candidates who say the AV valves open during ventricular contraction, or that the atria and ventricles contract at the same time, lose that point. Timings help but no extra mark is given for timings alone.

3. Heart sounds

3 marks

Through a stethoscope Chidi hears “lub-dub, lub-dub”. (a) State the cause of the “lub”. (1 mark) (b) State the cause of the “dub”. (1 mark) (c) Dr Aisha hears an extra swishing sound in another patient's chest. Suggest what this indicates. (1 mark)

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) The closing of the AV valves (tricuspid and bicuspid) as the ventricles begin to contract. (1) (b) The closing of the semilunar valves at the bases of the aorta and pulmonary artery as the ventricles begin to relax. (1) (c) A murmur, which suggests a faulty valve that is leaking or narrowed, so blood is flowing the wrong way or through too small an opening. (1)

Mark scheme note: Swapping the two sounds scores zero for both (a) and (b). “The heart contracting” or “the ventricles squeezing” is not credited: the sounds come from valves closing.

4. Control of the heartbeat

4 marks

(a) Describe how an impulse starting in the heart makes the whole heart contract in the correct order. (3 marks) (b) Explain why the impulse is delayed at the atrioventricular node. (1 mark)

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) The sinoatrial node (pacemaker), set in the right atrium's wall, fires an impulse that spreads across both atria, so they contract. (1) The impulse reaches the atrioventricular node and, after a short pause, travels along the bundle of His running down the septum. (1) The Purkinje fibres carry it through the ventricle walls, so both ventricles contract from the apex upwards, squeezing blood up towards the arteries. (1) (b) The delay gives the atria time to finish emptying into the ventricles before the ventricles start to contract. (1)

Mark scheme note: Candidates who write “the brain sends the impulse” lose the first mark; the beat is myogenic. In (b), “to rest the heart” is not credited.

5. Calculation: Baba Sule's heart

6 marks

Dr Aisha checks Baba Sule at rest. She counts 22 beats in 15 s. His stroke volume is 65 cm³. (a) Calculate his heart rate. (1 mark) (b) Calculate the length of one cardiac cycle, to 2 decimal places. (1 mark) (c) Calculate his cardiac output in dm³ min⁻¹. (2 marks) (d) After carrying a goat carcass to his stall, his heart rate is 121 beats min⁻¹. Calculate the percentage rise in his heart rate. (2 marks)

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) Heart rate = 22 × 60/15 = 22 × 4 = 88 beats min⁻¹. (1) (b) T = 60 s ÷ 88 = 0.6818… ≈ 0.68 s. (1) (c) Cardiac output = 88 × 65 cm³ = 5720 cm³ min⁻¹ (1); 5720 ÷ 1000 = 5.72 dm³ min⁻¹. (1) (d) Rise = 121 − 88 = 33 beats min⁻¹; % rise = 33/88 × 100 (1) = 37.5%. (1)

Mark scheme note: In (c), the first mark is for the product with its cm³ unit and the second for the correct conversion; 5720 dm³ scores only the first mark. In (d), dividing by 121 instead of 88 (giving 27.3%) loses both marks. Answers without units lose the answer mark in (a) to (c).

7. Precautions

2 marks

State two precautions that were taken to obtain a reliable resting pulse rate in the practical.

Model solution unlocks after you try

Any two (1 each): the pulse was felt with the index and middle fingers, not the thumb · the subject sat quietly for five minutes before the resting count · each resting count was repeated three times and the mean was taken · the stopwatch was started on a beat counted as zero.

Mark scheme note: Precautions must be written in the past tense. “Use two fingers” (an instruction) loses the mark. “I was careful” is too vague to score.

8. Baba Sule's claim

2 marks

Baba Sule says, “The brain sends every single heartbeat.” Explain why he is wrong, and state what the brain actually does to the heart.

Model solution unlocks after you try

Heart muscle is myogenic: each beat starts in the heart itself, at the sinoatrial node, so the heart would keep beating even if its nerves were cut. (1) The cardiac centre in the medulla oblongata only changes the rate: sympathetic nerves (with the hormone adrenaline) speed it up, and impulses down the vagus nerve slow it down. (1)

Mark scheme note: The second mark needs both directions, one route that speeds the heart and one that slows it. “The brain controls the heart” with no detail scores zero.
