Precautions and sources of error

Precautions (past tense): a separate clean stirrer was used for each well · wells were labelled before any serum was added · (teacher only) the lancet was sterile, used once and dropped into a sharps bin · gloves were worn and the tile was disinfected after use · every result was read after the full 2 minutes.

Sources of error (with direction): reading before 2 minutes missed weak clumping, so a positive well was read as negative (a false negative) · reusing one stirrer carried cells and serum between wells and could cause clumping that does not belong there (a false positive) · a drying drop at the edge of a well can look grainy and be mistaken for clumping (a false positive).

Practical analysis questions

Answer these five questions, then compare with the model answers.

  1. Why does group B blood clump in anti-B serum?
  2. Why is Donor 4 unsafe though it contains the B antigen the rider already has?
  3. Which donor is the universal ABO donor, and why?
  4. Why is Rh+ blood avoided for an Rh− patient?
  5. Why are fresh stirrers needed?
Model answers unlocks after you try
  1. Group B red cells carry antigen B. The antibody in anti-B serum fits that antigen, and each Y-shaped antibody links neighbouring cells, so the cells clump.
  2. Donor 4 (AB) carries antigen A as well as B. The rider's plasma holds anti-a, which would clump the donor's cells, so it is unsafe.
  3. Donor 1 (group O) is the universal ABO donor: its red cells carry neither antigen A nor B, so no antibody in any patient's plasma can clump them.
  4. Rh+ cells carry the D antigen. An Rh− patient has no anti-D at first, but would start making it after meeting Rh+ cells, and a later transfusion could cause a reaction.
  5. A used stirrer carries serum and cells from one well to the next, which can cause clumping that does not belong there (a false positive).
3Make sense · 15 min

Groups, clumps, Rhesus and disorders

Now we connect what the tile showed to the rules WAEC, NECO and JAMB expect, then to the disorders of the circulation.

Movement break (45 min mark, 5 minutes): stand up and march on the spot for one minute, swinging your arms, then shake out your legs and take five slow breaths. Moving the leg muscles squeezes the leg veins and pushes blood back to the heart, which is why you keep moving on long journeys. Sit, find your pulse and count it for 15 seconds.

1 · The ABO groups

Agglutination: if donor red cells carry an antigen that matches an antibody in the patient's plasma, the antibodies link the cells into clumps. The clumps can block small vessels and the burst cells can damage the kidneys, which can be fatal. The rule: look at the donor's antigens and the patient's antibodies.

Hospitals therefore test the group of both people and mix samples to check for clumping before any transfusion. Donated blood is also screened for syphilis, HIV and hepatitis B and C, and stored with an anticoagulant such as sodium citrate.

2 · The Rhesus factor

People whose red cells carry the Rhesus (D) antigen are Rh+ (the majority); those without it are Rh−. An Rh− person makes anti-D only after meeting Rh+ cells. If an Rh− mother carries an Rh+ baby, baby cells can enter her blood at birth and sensitise her. In a later Rh+ pregnancy her anti-D crosses the placenta and attacks the red cells of the unborn child, a condition called haemolytic disease of the newborn. An anti-D injection given to the mother soon after each delivery prevents this.

4Apply · Worked examples · 15 min

Worked examples, step by step

Try each problem on paper first, then check the working.

EXAMPLE 1

Reading a tile

A sample clumps with anti-A and anti-D but not with anti-B. Find the group.

  1. Anti-A: it clumps, so antigen A is present.
  2. Anti-B: no clumping, so antigen B is absent; the ABO group is A.
  3. Anti-D: it clumps, so the Rhesus antigen is present.
The sample is group A and Rhesus positive.
EXAMPLE 2

Choosing bags for the rider

The patient is B, Rh−. The bags are O+, O−, A−, B+, B− and AB−. Which can he safely receive?

  1. ABO: his plasma holds anti-a, so reject bags with antigen A (A− and AB−). B and O cells are acceptable.
  2. Rhesus: he is Rh−, so reject Rh+ bags (O+ and B+) so that he is not sensitised.
  3. Left: B− (exact match, first choice) and O−.
B negative first, O negative as the alternative.
EXAMPLE 3

Mama Bisi's reading

Her pressure is 160/100 mmHg. Hypertension is diagnosed when readings on two different days are 140 mmHg or more systolic, or 90 mmHg or more diastolic. What do her numbers say?

  1. Systolic: 160 ≥ 140, above the line.
  2. Diastolic: 100 ≥ 90, above the line.
  3. Pulse pressure: 160 − 100 = 60 mmHg, compared with 120 − 80 = 40 mmHg normally: a stiffer, harder-worked arterial system.
Both numbers are high; she needs repeat checks and treatment, not rest alone.
EXAMPLE 4

A Rhesus pregnancy

An Rh− woman's first baby is Rh+. Explain the risk to her second Rh+ baby in steps, and say how it is prevented.

  1. First birth: baby cells enter her blood.
  2. Her response: she makes anti-D.
  3. Second pregnancy: her anti-D crosses the placenta.
  4. Result: the baby's red cells are destroyed.
Prevention: an anti D injection soon after the first delivery.

Part A · Home investigation: the paper cross-match and the family salt check

Build a paper model of clumping, then check the salt in your home.

You will build a paper model that shows why some donor–patient pairs clump, and then look at one everyday habit that affects blood pressure in your home. You will not use any blood or needles.

You need: thin card or an old exercise-book cover, scissors, a pencil and ruler, a teaspoon, and a notebook.

Safety and respect: Do not prick anyone's finger or handle anyone's blood. Asking relatives about their blood group, genotype or blood pressure is optional: ask politely, accept "I don't know" or "I'd rather not say", and keep their answers private.

  1. The paper cells. Cut out 8 card circles as red cells. Draw a small triangle on the edge of two of them (antigen A), a small square on two (antigen B), both shapes on two (group AB) and nothing on the last two (group O). Label each with its group.
  2. The paper antibodies. Cut out Y-shaped cards. On the arms of some, cut a triangle-shaped notch (anti-a); on others, a square notch (anti-b). A group A patient gets anti-b cards, group B gets anti-a, group O gets both, group AB gets none.
  3. Draw a 4 × 4 grid with donors down the side and patients across the top. For each square, test whether any of the patient's antibody notches fits a shape on the donor's cell. If one fits, write "clumps"; if none fits, write "safe".
  4. Add a Rhesus row: mark a dot on cells that are Rh+, and decide which of them an Rh− patient should not receive.
  5. The salt check. For one day, ask whoever cooks to let you note how many level teaspoons of salt and how many seasoning cubes go into the family's meals. Divide by the number of people who ate.
  6. Write a short heart-health plan for your home with three changes, one of which uses your salt result.

2. Agglutination

3 marks

(a) State what is meant by agglutination. (1 mark)
(b) Explain why giving group A blood to a group B patient is dangerous. (2 marks)

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) Agglutination is the clumping together of red cells when the antigen on them meets the antibody that matches it (1). Equivalent wordings that keep the key ideas are credited. (b) The donor's red cells carry antigen A, and the patient's plasma contains anti-a, so the donor cells are clumped (1). The clumps can block small vessels such as capillaries and the burst cells can damage the kidneys, which may kill the patient (1).

Mark scheme note: Do not credit "the blood clots" for (a): clumping is not clotting. In (b) the first mark needs both the donor's antigen and the patient's antibody; naming only one does not earn it.

3. Choosing the rider's bag

5 marks

The injured okada rider is group B, Rh−. The cool box holds O+, O−, A−, B+, B− and AB−. State which bags are safe for him and explain your choice.

Model solution unlocks after you try

A− and AB− are rejected, because their cells carry antigen A, which the anti-a in his plasma would clump (1). O+ and B+ are rejected, because he is Rh− and Rh+ cells could make him produce anti-D, so a later transfusion could cause a reaction (1). B− is the first choice, as an exact match for both ABO and Rhesus (1). O− is the alternative if B− runs out (1), because its cells carry no A, B or D antigen for his antibodies to react with (1).

Mark scheme note: Each mark is for a separate decision; a candidate who simply lists "B− and O−" without reasons earns only the two marks for naming them. Choosing AB− because "AB receives from everybody" confuses the universal recipient with a universal donor and scores zero for that line.

4. Haemolytic disease of the newborn

5 marks

(a) Explain how an Rh− mother's second Rh+ baby can be harmed. (4 marks)
(b) State how this is prevented. (1 mark)

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) At the birth of her first Rh+ baby, some of the baby's red cells, which carry the Rhesus (D) antigen, enter the mother's blood (1). Her immune system responds by making anti-D antibodies (1). In a later pregnancy with an Rh+ baby, her anti-D crosses the placenta into the baby's blood (1). The anti-D destroys the baby's red cells, so the baby becomes anaemic and may die (1). (b) The mother is given an anti-D injection soon after each Rh+ delivery (1).

Mark scheme note: Reversing the Rhesus groups (an Rh+ mother with an Rh− baby) scores zero for the whole of (a). The first baby is not usually harmed; candidates who say it is lose the first mark. "Anti-D is given to the baby" does not earn (b).

6. Practical: precautions and errors

4 marks

(a) State two precautions that were taken during the blood-typing practical. (2 marks)
(b) For two sources of error, state the effect on the result and why. (2 marks)

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) Any two, in the past tense: a separate clean stirrer was used for each well (1); the wells were labelled before any serum was added (1); the lancet was sterile, used once and dropped into a sharps bin (1); gloves were worn and the tile was disinfected after use (1); each well was read only after the full 2 minutes (1). (b) Any two: reading before 2 minutes missed weak clumping, so a positive well was recorded as negative, a false negative (1); reusing one stirrer carried cells and serum between wells, giving clumping that did not belong there, a false positive (1); a drop drying at the edge of a well looked grainy and was mistaken for clumping, a false positive (1).

Mark scheme note: Precautions must be in the past tense; "use a new stirrer" loses the mark. An error needs both the effect (false positive or false negative) and the reason. "Use clean apparatus" with no detail is not credited.

7. Circulatory disorders

3 marks

For each disorder, state its cause and one way to prevent or control it: (a) atherosclerosis, (b) sickle cell anaemia, (c) varicose veins.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) Fatty deposits build up in artery walls, narrowing and hardening them; prevented by eating less saturated fat, not smoking and keeping active (1). (b) An inherited abnormal haemoglobin (HbS) makes red cells bend into sickle shapes that jam in capillaries; reduced by genotype testing and counselling before marriage (1). (c) The valves in leg veins fail, so blood pools and the veins swell and twist; reduced by avoiding standing still for many hours and by regular exercise (1).

Mark scheme note: Each mark needs both the cause and a matching prevention. "Eat a good diet" alone is too vague. Do not credit "sickle cell is caught from an infected person": it is inherited.

8. Mama Bisi's follow-up

3 marks

At health day Mama Bisi's pressure was 160/100 mmHg. Three months after cutting down on salt, her reading is 146/94 mmHg. Take 140/90 mmHg as the warning line.
(a) Is her new reading still above the warning line? Give a reason. (1 mark)
(b) Calculate her pulse pressure on each visit. (1 mark)
(c) Suggest one further change, other than eating less salt, that would help her. (1 mark)

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) Yes: 146 is above 140 and 94 is above 90, so both numbers are still high (1). (b) First visit: 160 − 100 = 60 mmHg; now: 146 − 94 = 52 mmHg (1). (c) Any one: regular exercise such as a daily walk; losing excess weight; not smoking; little or no alcohol; managing stress; taking the drugs her doctor prescribes and having her pressure checked regularly (1).

Mark scheme note: In (a) the answer must compare both numbers with the line; "yes, it is high" with no comparison scores zero. In (b) both values are needed for the mark; adding the numbers (260 and 240) instead of subtracting scores zero. "Rest" alone is not credited in (c).
