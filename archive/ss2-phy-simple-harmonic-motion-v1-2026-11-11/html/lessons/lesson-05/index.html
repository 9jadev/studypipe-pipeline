3Make sense · 15 min

What makes a motion Simple Harmonic?

You've seen the pendulum swing and timed its period. Now let's connect what you observed to the exact laws and equations WAEC, NECO and JAMB test.

The defining law of SHM

A body performs simple harmonic motion (SHM) if it moves to and fro about a fixed equilibrium point such that its acceleration is directly proportional to its displacement from equilibrium, and is always directed towards that equilibrium point:

a = −ω2x     and     F = −kx = −mω2x

Why is there a minus sign (−)? Because displacement x (measured outward from the centre) and acceleration a (pointing back towards the centre) are always in opposite directions. When the bob is to the right (x > 0), the restoring pull accelerates it to the left (a < 0).

The four core quantities

Every harmonic oscillator is described by four numbers you must define and use fluently:

  • Displacement (x): distance in a specified direction from the equilibrium centre at any instant (unit: m).
  • Amplitude (A): the maximum displacement from the equilibrium position on either side (unit: m).
  • Period (T): the time taken to complete one full to-and-fro oscillation (unit: s). If N oscillations take time t, then T = t ÷ N.
  • Frequency (f): the number of complete oscillations per second (unit: hertz, Hz or s⁻¹), where f = 1 ÷ T.
ω = 2πf = 2π ÷ T   (angular frequency in rad s−1)
v = ω√(A2 − x2)   →   vmax = ωA (at x = 0)  ·  amax = ω2A (at x = ±A)
Simple pendulum: T = 2π√(L ÷ g)    ·    Loaded spring: T = 2π√(m ÷ k)

All simple harmonic motions are periodic, but not all periodic motions are simple harmonic. The Earth orbits the Sun every 365.25 days, so its motion is periodic — but it does not move back and forth through a central equilibrium point with a = −ω²x, so it is not SHM.

Three oscillator truths examiners love

1 · Isochronism (Small angles)

For angles up to about 10°, sin θ ≈ θ (in radians). A slightly wider swing travels faster through the middle, taking the same time T.

2 · Mass cancels out

In a pendulum, both gravity and inertia are proportional to mass m. They cancel out in ma = −mg sin θ, so mass never appears in T = 2π√(L/g).

3 · Damping vs Resonance

Air drag causes damping (amplitude fades while period stays steady). Pushing at the natural frequency f₀ causes resonance (huge amplitude).

Worked examples, step by step

Cover the numbered steps with your hand, attempt each calculation on paper first, and then check how every mark is earned.

EXAMPLE 1

Period, frequency, ω and length from 20 swings

In a school physics laboratory, a simple pendulum takes 36.0 s to complete 20 oscillations. Taking g = 9.8 m s−2 and π = 3.142, calculate: (a) the period T, (b) the frequency f, (c) the angular frequency ω, and (d) the effective length L of the pendulum.

  1. Period T: divide the total time t = 36.0 s by the number of oscillations N = 20: T = 36.0 ÷ 20 = 1.80 s.
  2. Frequency f: take the reciprocal of the period: f = 1 ÷ T = 1 ÷ 1.80 = 0.556 Hz.
  3. Angular frequency ω: use ω = 2π ÷ T = (2 × 3.142) ÷ 1.80 = 3.49 rad s⁻¹.
  4. Length L: square both sides of T = 2π√(L/g) to get L = gT² ÷ (4π²) = (9.8 × 1.80²) ÷ (4 × 3.142²) = 31.752 ÷ 39.489 = 0.804 m (80.4 cm).
(a) T = 1.80 s  ·  (b) f = 0.556 Hz  ·  (c) ω = 3.49 rad s−1  ·  (d) L = 0.804 m
EXAMPLE 2

Maximum speed, maximum acceleration & speed at x

A particle executes simple harmonic motion with amplitude A = 0.10 m and angular frequency ω = 4.0 rad s−1. Find: (a) its maximum speed vmax, (b) its maximum acceleration amax, and (c) its speed when it is at displacement x = 0.06 m from the equilibrium centre.

  1. Maximum speed (at x = 0): vmax = ωA = 4.0 × 0.10 = 0.40 m s⁻¹.
  2. Maximum acceleration (at x = ±A): amax = ω²A = 4.0² × 0.10 = 16 × 0.10 = 1.60 m s⁻².
  3. Speed at x = 0.06 m: substitute into v = ω√(A² − x²) = 4.0 × √(0.10² − 0.06²) = 4.0 × √(0.0100 − 0.0036) = 4.0 × √0.0064 = 4.0 × 0.08 = 0.32 m s⁻¹.
(a) vmax = 0.40 m s−1  ·  (b) amax = 1.60 m s−2  ·  (c) v = 0.32 m s−1
EXAMPLE 3

Finding g from the slope of a WAEC practical graph

During a WAEC Paper 3 practical, a student plots length L (m) on the vertical axis against T2 (s2) on the horizontal axis. Two widely separated points on the line of best fit are (1.60 s2, 0.40 m) and (4.44 s2, 1.10 m). Determine: (a) the slope S of the graph, and (b) the acceleration due to gravity g.

  1. Slope S: S = ΔL ÷ ΔT² = (1.10 − 0.40) ÷ (4.44 − 1.60) = 0.70 ÷ 2.84 = 0.2465 m s⁻².
  2. Relate slope to g: since L = (g ÷ 4π²)T², the slope of L against T² equals g ÷ 4π².
  3. Calculate g: g = 4π² × S = 4 × (3.142)² × 0.2465 = 39.489 × 0.2465 = 9.73 m s⁻².
(a) Slope S = 0.247 m s−2  ·  (b) g = 9.73 m s−2
EXAMPLE 4

JAMB scaling trap: quadrupling the length

A simple pendulum has a period of 1.50 s at a certain location. What becomes its new period and frequency if its length is quadrupled (multiplied by 4) at the same location?

  1. From T = 2π√(L/g), when g is constant, T ∝ √L.
  2. Therefore T₂ ÷ T₁ = √(L₂ ÷ L₁) = √4 = 2.
  3. New period: T₂ = 2 × 1.50 s = 3.00 s. New frequency: f₂ = 1 ÷ 3.00 = 0.333 Hz (half the original frequency).
New period T2 = 3.00 s (doubled)  ·  New frequency f2 = 0.333 Hz (halved)

Back to Mama Ngozi: how do we fix the school clock?

Now you have the physics to solve Kunle's complaint! Test Mrs Adeyemi's idea first (making Clock B's bob heavier), then time 10 swings of both clocks, and finally turn the regulating nut to adjust Clock B's length.

Clock A · Accurate reference · L = 0.994 m

2.00 speriod T (target: 2.00 s)

Clock B · School hall clock · L = 1.150 m

2.15 speriod T (loses 4.5 min/hr!)

Clock A ticks with T = 2.00 s, while Clock B is dragging at T = 2.15 s. Start with Step 1.

The big idea of this whole topic: a simple pendulum is a natural timekeeper because its period T = 2π√(L/g) depends only on its effective length L and local gravity g — never on the mass of the bob or small changes in swing width. To make a slow pendulum clock tick faster, you shorten L by turning the regulating nut upward; to measure g anywhere on Earth, you measure L and T!

Part B · Written questions (30 marks)

1. Define each term and state its SI unit: (a) simple harmonic motion (b) amplitude (c) period (d) frequency.

4 marks
Solution unlocks after you try
  1. Simple harmonic motion is a periodic to-and-fro motion in which the acceleration of the body is directly proportional to its displacement from a fixed equilibrium point and is always directed towards that point (a = −ω²x). (1)
  2. Amplitude is the maximum displacement of an oscillating body from its equilibrium position (SI unit: metre, m). (1)
  3. Period is the time taken to complete one full oscillation (SI unit: second, s). (1)
  4. Frequency is the number of complete oscillations made in one second (SI unit: hertz, Hz or s⁻¹). (1)

2. “All simple harmonic motions are periodic, but not all periodic motions are simple harmonic.” Explain this statement with one example of each.

3 marks
Solution unlocks after you try

Every simple harmonic motion repeats at regular time intervals T, so it is periodic (1). However, to be simple harmonic, the motion must also have a restoring acceleration proportional to displacement towards a central equilibrium point (a = −ω²x) (1). Example of SHM: a simple pendulum swinging through a small angle (or a mass on a spiral spring). Example of periodic motion that is not SHM: the uniform rotation of ceiling fan blades (or the Earth orbiting the Sun) (1).

3. A body of mass 0.20 kg executes simple harmonic motion with amplitude A = 0.12 m and frequency f = 2.5 Hz. Calculate: (a) its angular frequency ω, (b) its maximum speed vmax, (c) its maximum acceleration amax, and (d) its speed when displaced x = 0.09 m from equilibrium. (Take π = 3.142)

5 marks
Solution unlocks after you try
(a) ω = 2πf = 2 × 3.142 × 2.5 = 15.71 rad s−1 (1)
(b) vmax = ωA = 15.71 × 0.12 = 1.89 m s−1 (1)
(c) amax = ω2A = (15.71)2 × 0.12 = 246.8 × 0.12 = 29.6 m s−2 (1)
(d) v = ω√(A2 − x2) = 15.71 × √(0.122 − 0.092) = 15.71 × √0.0063 = 15.71 × 0.07937 = 1.25 m s−1 (2)

4. For an undamped simple pendulum swinging between −A and +A, state the position(s) where each quantity is maximum: (a) kinetic energy, (b) magnitude of restoring force, (c) linear velocity, (d) gravitational potential energy.

4 marks
Solution unlocks after you try

(a) Kinetic energy is maximum at the equilibrium centre (x = 0) (1).
(b) Restoring force is maximum at the two extreme ends (x = −A and x = +A) (1).
(c) Linear velocity is maximum at the equilibrium centre (x = 0) (1).
(d) Gravitational potential energy is maximum at the two extreme ends (x = −A and x = +A) (1).

5. In a simple pendulum practical (N = 20 oscillations), a candidate recorded t1 = 28.40 s for L1 = 0.50 m, and t2 = 40.16 s for L2 = 1.00 m. (a) Find T and T2 for both lengths. (b) Calculate the slope S = ΔL ÷ ΔT2. (c) Hence determine the acceleration due to gravity g.

5 marks
Solution unlocks after you try

(a) For L₁ = 0.50 m: T₁ = 28.40 ÷ 20 = 1.420 s; T₁² = 2.016 s² (1).
For L₂ = 1.00 m: T₂ = 40.16 ÷ 20 = 2.008 s; T₂² = 4.032 s² (1).

(b) Slope S = (1.00 − 0.50) ÷ (4.032 − 2.016) = 0.50 ÷ 2.016 = 0.2480 m s⁻² (1).

(c) g = 4π² × S = 39.489 × 0.2480 = 9.79 m s⁻² (2).

6. State and explain how the period T of a simple pendulum changes if: (a) the mass of the bob is tripled, (b) the length of the thread is multiplied by 9, (c) the pendulum is taken to the Moon where gMoon = ⅙ gEarth.

3 marks
Solution unlocks after you try

(a) Unchanged, because period T = 2π√(L/g) is independent of mass (1).
(b) Triples (3T), because T ∝ √L and √9 = 3 (1).
(c) Increases by a factor of √6 (≈ 2.45T), because T ∝ 1/√g (1).

7. Kunle's school hall pendulum clock has a period of 2.15 s instead of 2.00 s, so it loses time. (a) Why did attaching a heavier lead weight to the bob fail to speed it up? (b) By how many centimetres must the effective length be shortened to bring the period from 2.15 s back to 2.00 s? (Take g = 9.81 m s−2)

4 marks
Solution unlocks after you try

(a) Mass does not affect the period of a simple pendulum because gravitational force and inertia both scale equally with mass (1).

(b) Using L = gT² ÷ 4π²:
At T₁ = 2.15 s: L₁ = 9.81 × (2.15)² ÷ 39.478 = 1.149 m (1).
At T₂ = 2.00 s: L₂ = 9.81 × (2.00)² ÷ 39.478 = 0.994 m (1).
Shorten by ΔL = 1.149 − 0.994 = 0.155 m = 15.5 cm (1).

8. State two precautions, written in the standard WAEC past-tense format, that you took during the simple pendulum experiment.
