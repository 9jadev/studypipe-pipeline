Practical analysis questions

Answer these five questions, then compare with the model answers.

  1. Why is no current drawn from the test cell at balance?
  2. Why does that let the potentiometer read the true EMF when a voltmeter cannot?
  3. Why must the bridge wire be uniform?
  4. In the driver circuit, why isn't the whole driver EMF across the wire?
  5. Why tap, not drag, the jockey?
Model answers unlocks after you try
  1. At the null point the galvanometer reads zero, so the current through the test cell is zero.
  2. With no current there are no lost volts (Ir = 0), so the p.d. balanced on the wire equals the EMF itself; a loaded voltmeter always reads E − Ir, which is smaller.
  3. Only a uniform cross-section gives the same resistance per centimetre everywhere, so the two parts of the wire are in the ratio of their lengths, l/(100 − l).
  4. The wire shares the driver's EMF with the series resistor (and any internal resistance): V_wire = E₀ × R_wire/(R_wire + R_series), so only part of E₀ sits across the wire.
  5. Tapping avoids scraping and thinning the wire; a dragged jockey would change the wire's cross-section and spoil the uniform resistance per centimetre.

3Make sense · 15 min

Two null methods, one idea

Now we connect the balances you found to the names and formulae WAEC, NECO and JAMB expect.

Metre bridge: a Wheatstone bridge built from a uniform 100 cm wire. It balances (galvanometer current zero) when R1/R2 = R3/R4. With a balance length l from the left, Rleft/Rright = l/(100 − l), so an unknown R = Rstandard(100 − l)/l. For two wires of the same material and length, a plot of R₂ against R₁ is a straight line whose slope is (d1/d2)², so √slope = d1/d2.

Potentiometer: a steady current in a uniform wire makes the p.d. across any length proportional to that length, V = kl. Because the balanced cell passes no current, no lost volts arise inside it and its full EMF is read: E1/E2 = l1/l2. Internal resistance from two lengths: r = R(l1 − l2)/l2, where l₁ is the open-circuit balance and l₂ the loaded one.

Key equations

R/S = l/(100 − l) · V = kl · E₁/E₂ = l₁/l₂ · r = R(l₁ − l₂)/l₂ · V_wire = E₀ R_wire/(R_wire + R_series)

The driver trap: the whole driver EMF is not across the wire. Part of it drops across the series rheostat (and the driver's own internal resistance). Always work out V_wire = E₀ × R_wire/(R_wire + R_series) first, then the gradient k = V_wire/100, before you turn a balance length into a voltage.

EXAMPLE 1

Metre bridge, then resistivity from a measured diameter

Chinedu puts a 7.0 Ω standard in the left gap and Emeka's spare coil in the right gap; the galvanometer reads zero at l = 56.0 cm. (a) Find the coil's resistance. (b) The coil is 1.25 m of wire of diameter 0.36 mm. Find the resistivity.

  1. Balance condition: Rleft/R = l/(100 − l), so 7.0/R = 56.0/44.0.
  2. Resistance of the coil: R = 7.0 × 44.0/56.0 = 5.50 Ω.
  3. Area from the diameter: A = πd²/4 = π × (3.6 × 10⁻⁴)²/4 = 1.018 × 10⁻⁷ m².
  4. Resistivity: ρ = RA/L = (5.50 × 1.018 × 10⁻⁷)/1.25 = 4.48 × 10⁻⁷ Ω m.
R = 5.50 Ω; ρ = 4.48 × 10⁻⁷ Ω m (≈ 4.5 × 10⁻⁷ Ω m to 2 s.f.).
EXAMPLE 2

The potentiometer driver circuit

A 100 cm potentiometer wire of resistance 5.0 Ω is in series with a 2.0 V driver cell (negligible internal resistance) and a 15.0 Ω resistor. (a) Find the current in the wire. (b) Find the p.d. across the whole wire. (c) At what length does a 0.040 V source balance?

  1. Current: I = E₀/(Rwire + Rseries) = 2.0/(5.0 + 15.0) = 0.10 A.
  2. P.d. across the wire: Vwire = I × Rwire = 0.10 × 5.0 = 0.50 V, so k = 0.50/100 = 0.0050 V cm⁻¹.
  3. Balance length: l = V/k = 0.040/0.0050 = 8.0 cm.
I = 0.10 A; V_wire = 0.50 V; balance at l = 8.0 cm. Trap: the full 2.0 V is not across the wire — only 0.50 V is.
EXAMPLE 3

Internal resistance by potentiometer

A cell balances at l1 = 75.0 cm on open circuit. With a 3.0 Ω resistor across it, the balance falls to l2 = 60.0 cm. Find the internal resistance.

  1. Formula: open circuit gives E ∝ l1, on load V ∝ l2, so r = R(l1 − l2)/l2.
  2. Substitute: r = 3.0 × (75.0 − 60.0)/60.0.
  3. Evaluate: r = 3.0 × 15.0/60.0 = 3.0 × 0.25 = 0.75 Ω.
r = 0.75 Ω.

Part A · Home investigation: a balance point without a meter

Find the mass of an eraser in "bottle-cap units" using lengths only.

You need: a 30 cm ruler, a round pencil, about 8 identical plastic bottle caps, an eraser, a flat table and your notebook.

  1. Rest the empty ruler across the pencil and move it until it balances level. Note the pivot position (your "zero", like checking a meter's zero).
  2. Place the eraser 10.0 cm from the pivot on one side and keep it there throughout.
  3. Stack 3 caps and slide them on the other side until the ruler balances; record the distance d to the stack's centre.
  4. Repeat with 4, 5, 6 and 8 caps, each balance twice; take the mean d. Work out n × d and M = n × d/10.0.
  5. Plot d (vertical) against 1/n and draw the best line.

A good answer sees that d against n is a curve but d against 1/n is a straight line through the origin, because n × d stays constant (about 60 cap cm), and finds the eraser's mass as about 6.0 caps. It explains that this is a null method — the ruler was judged only by whether it tipped, so no scale or spring balance was needed, just as a metre bridge needs only a galvanometer zero and the ratio l/(100 − l). Precautions are in the past tense (the empty ruler was balanced first; each distance was measured to the centre of the stack; each balance was repeated), and one error is named with its direction.

Part B · Written paper (30 marks)

1. Definitions and statements

3 marks

(a) State the principle behind the potentiometer. (b) State the condition for a Wheatstone bridge to be balanced. (c) What is meant by the potential gradient of a potentiometer wire?

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) If a steady current flows along a wire of uniform cross-section and material, the p.d. between any two points is directly proportional to the length between them. (1) (b) The bridge is balanced when the ratio of the two resistances on one side equals the ratio of the two on the other, P/Q = R/S, so that the galvanometer carries no current. (1) (c) It is the p.d. per unit length along the wire, k = V/l, in V cm⁻¹ or V m⁻¹. (1)

Mark scheme note: In (a), both "steady current" and "uniform wire" are needed. In (b), the ratio with no mention of zero galvanometer current is not credited. In (c), the unit is part of the mark.

2. Why the null method wins

4 marks

Chinedu's voltmeter read 8.4 V across Emeka's battery while the starter ran, but the potentiometer showed its EMF as 12.0 V. (a) Explain why the voltmeter reading is lower. (b) Explain why the potentiometer reading is the true EMF. (c) State one other advantage of the potentiometer over a voltmeter.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) While the battery delivers current, part of its EMF drives that current through its own internal resistance (1), so the terminals (and the voltmeter) show only V = E − Ir = 12.0 − 3.6 = 8.4 V. (1) (b) At balance the galvanometer reads zero, so no current is drawn from the battery; with I = 0 there are no lost volts, so the balanced p.d. equals the EMF. (1) (c) Any one: its accuracy does not depend on a pointer scale, only on a zero reading · it is more sensitive, as a small p.d. is spread over a long length · its range can be set by the driver current. (1)

Mark scheme note: In (a), "the voltmeter is faulty" scores zero; the two marks are the cause and the equation. In (b), the answer must link zero current to zero lost volts. In (c), do not credit a repeat of (b).

3. Derivation

4 marks

A cell balances at length l₁ on open circuit, and at l₂ when a resistor R is connected across it. Show that r = R(l₁ − l₂)/l₂.

Model solution unlocks after you try

On open circuit the balance gives the EMF, on load the terminal p.d., both with the same gradient k: E = kl₁ and V = kl₂, so E/V = l₁/l₂. (1) With current I, E = I(R + r) and V = IR, so E/V = (R + r)/R. (1) Equating: l₁/l₂ = (R + r)/R = 1 + r/R. (1) So r/R = (l₁ − l₂)/l₂, giving r = R(l₁ − l₂)/l₂. (1)

Mark scheme note: The first mark needs l₁ ↔ E and l₂ ↔ V; swapping loses it. The second is for the circuit equations. Quoting the result with no working scores zero.

4. Metre bridge and resistivity

4 marks

A metre bridge has a 6.0 Ω standard in the left gap and a constantan coil in the right gap; the balance is 37.5 cm from the left. (a) Calculate the coil's resistance. (b) The wire has diameter 0.40 mm; taking ρ = 4.9 × 10⁻⁷ Ω m, find the length of wire in the coil.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) 6.0/R = 37.5/(100 − 37.5) = 37.5/62.5 (1); R = 6.0 × 62.5/37.5 = 10.0 Ω. (1) (b) A = πd²/4 = π × (4.0 × 10⁻⁴)²/4 = 1.26 × 10⁻⁷ m² (1); L = RA/ρ = (10.0 × 1.257 × 10⁻⁷)/(4.9 × 10⁻⁷) = 2.6 m. (1)

Mark scheme note: In (a), the 6.0 Ω must pair with the 37.5 cm length; inverting the ratio scores 0 for the answer. In (b), using d for r in πr², or leaving d in mm, loses the area mark; the length needs a unit.

5. The driver circuit trap

6 marks

A 100 cm potentiometer wire of resistance 8.0 Ω is in series with a rheostat set to 11.5 Ω and a driver of EMF 4.0 V and internal resistance 0.50 Ω. (a) Find the current in the wire. (b) Find the p.d. across the wire and the potential gradient. (c) A cell balances at 70.0 cm; find its EMF. (d) Tobe assumed the whole 4.0 V was across the wire; state the EMF he would have found, and whether it is too high or too low.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) Total R = 8.0 + 11.5 + 0.50 = 20.0 Ω (1); I = 4.0/20.0 = 0.20 A. (1) (b) V_wire = 0.20 × 8.0 = 1.6 V (1); k = 1.6/100 = 0.016 V cm⁻¹. (1) (c) E = kl = 0.016 × 70.0 = 1.12 V. (1) (d) Tobe's gradient is 4.0/100 = 0.040 V cm⁻¹, giving 0.040 × 70.0 = 2.8 V, which is too high, because only 1.6 V of the 4.0 V is really across the wire. (1)

Mark scheme note: In (a), leaving out the 0.50 Ω loses the first mark; the second can be earned on error carried forward. In (b), the gradient needs its unit. In (d), the value without "too high" scores zero.

6. Internal resistance by potentiometer

4 marks

A cell balances on open circuit at l₁ = 88.0 cm. Different resistors R were connected across it, giving: R = 1.0 Ω → l₂ = 55.0 cm; R = 2.0 Ω → l₂ = 67.7 cm; R = 4.0 Ω → l₂ = 76.5 cm. (a) Calculate r for each row to 2 d.p. (b) Find the mean r. (c) Explain why l₂ was always shorter than l₁. (d) The driver ran down slightly after l₁ was found but before the l₂ readings; state the effect on r and explain.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) 1.0 × 33.0/55.0 = 0.60 Ω; 2.0 × 20.3/67.7 = 0.60 Ω; 4.0 × 11.5/76.5 = 0.60 Ω. (1) (b) Mean r = 0.60 Ω. (1) (c) With R connected, current flows through the cell, so a p.d. Ir is lost inside it; its terminal p.d. is less than its EMF, so it balances against a shorter length. (1) (d) r came out too low: the weaker driver lowered the gradient, so each l₂ was longer than it should have been, making (l₁ − l₂) too small. (1)

Mark scheme note: In (a), all three values are needed. In (b), the unit is required. In (c), "because R was added" without the lost-volts idea scores zero. In (d), a direction without a reason, or the wrong direction, scores zero.

7. Good bridge practice

3 marks

(a) Why must a metre-bridge wire be of uniform cross-section? (b) Why is a balance near the middle of the wire preferred? (c) Why does a slightly mis-calibrated galvanometer scale not spoil a metre-bridge result?

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) Only then is the resistance per centimetre the same all along, so the two parts of the wire are in the ratio of their lengths, l/(100 − l). (1) (b) Near the middle neither l nor (100 − l) is small, so the fixed uncertainty in reading the rule (and any small end-resistance) is a small percentage of each length, giving a more accurate ratio. (1) (c) The method uses only the zero (null) reading, not the size of a deflection, so the scale's calibration does not enter the result. (1)

Mark scheme note: In (a), "so that it is straight" scores zero. In (b), "it is easier to read" without the percentage-error idea is not credited. In (c), the key word is the null or zero reading.

8. Precautions

2 marks

State two precautions taken during the potentiometer or metre-bridge experiment to get reliable readings.

Model solution unlocks after you try

Any two (1 each): the key was removed after each reading so the wire did not warm up · the jockey was tapped lightly and not dragged along the wire · all connections were tight and the terminals clean · the balance was approached from both sides and the readings repeated · parallax was avoided by reading the metre rule with the eye directly above the jockey.

Mark scheme note: Precautions must be in the past tense; "remove the key after each reading" is an instruction and loses the mark. "I was careful" or a precaution from another experiment scores zero.
