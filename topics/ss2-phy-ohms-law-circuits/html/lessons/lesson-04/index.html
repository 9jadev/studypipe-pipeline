Practical analysis questions

Answer these five questions, then compare with the model answers.

  1. Why must the shunt resistance be very small?
  2. Why must the multiplier resistance be very large?
  3. What fraction of the line current flows through the coil in the 3 A ammeter above?
  4. Why does adding a series multiplier not change the current the coil needs for full scale?
  5. What goes wrong if you use Rtotal as the multiplier?
Model answers unlocks after you try
  1. The ammeter is in series with the circuit, so a small shunt keeps the whole meter's resistance tiny and barely changes the current being measured.
  2. The voltmeter is in parallel across a component, so a large multiplier keeps the whole meter's resistance huge and lets it draw almost no current, leaving the p.d. undisturbed.
  3. Ig ÷ I = 0.015 ÷ 3.0 = 0.005, so only 0.5 % of the line current passes through the coil; the shunt carries the other 99.5 %.
  4. The multiplier is in series with the coil, so both carry the same current; it only shares the voltage, so the coil still reaches full scale at the same 15 mA.
  5. Rtotal = V ÷ Ig is the whole meter's resistance; using it as the added multiplier makes the meter's total resistance too large, so at full-scale voltage the coil carries less than Ig and every reading comes out too low.
3Make sense · 15 min

Shunt and multiplier, from Ohm's law

Now we connect the bench to the formulae WAEC, NECO and JAMB expect — and the one trap that costs marks.

Movement break (45 min mark, 5 minutes): stand up. Clap your hands side by side in parallel and say, “Small shunt in parallel makes an ammeter — placed in series!” Then stretch both arms one above the other in series and say, “Big multiplier in series makes a voltmeter — placed in parallel!” Do it twice, shake out your hands and sit down.

Converting to an ammeter: put a small shunt Rs in parallel with the coil so most of the current bypasses it. The parallel pair has equal p.d., so IgRg = (I − Ig)Rs. The finished ammeter has a very low resistance and goes in series with the load.

Converting to a voltmeter: put a large multiplier Rm in series with the coil so that at full-scale voltage the current is still only Ig. Then V = Ig(Rg + Rm). The finished voltmeter has a very high resistance and goes in parallel. The resistor you add is Rm = Rtotal − Rg, not the whole Rtotal.

The equations
R_s = I_g R_g / (I − I_g)
R_m = V / I_g − R_g
R_total(voltmeter) = V / I_g = R_g + R_m
current split I_g : (I − I_g)

Worked examples, step by step

Try each on paper first, then check the working. Watch the units and the Rm-vs-Rtotal trap.

EXAMPLE 1

Nkechi's coil as a 3.0 A ammeter

A galvanometer of coil resistance 18.0 Ω gives full-scale deflection at 15 mA. What shunt turns it into a 3.0 A ammeter?

  1. Convert units: I_g = 15 mA = 0.015 A; target I = 3.0 A.
  2. Apply the shunt formula: R_s = I_g R_g / (I − I_g) = (0.015 × 18.0) / (3.0 − 0.015).
  3. Evaluate: R_s = 0.270 / 2.985 = 0.0905 Ω (3 s.f.), connected in parallel.
R_s = 0.0905 Ω — the coil takes 15 mA and the shunt the other 2.985 A.
EXAMPLE 2

A different coil (8 mA, 25 Ω) as a 40 V voltmeter

A different galvanometer from the drawer (not Nkechi's 15 mA one) has coil resistance 25 Ω and full-scale current 8 mA, and is to read up to 40 V. Find the multiplier.

  1. Convert units: I_g = 8 mA = 0.008 A.
  2. Total resistance needed: R_total = V / I_g = 40.0 / 0.008 = 5 000 Ω.
  3. Subtract R_g for the resistor you add: R_m = R_total − R_g = 5 000 − 25 = 4 975 Ω, in series.
R_m = 4 975 Ω — the 5 000 Ω is the whole meter's resistance, not the resistor you add.
EXAMPLE 3

Find I_g first, then the multiplier

A galvanometer of coil resistance 40 Ω reaches full-scale deflection when the p.d. across its coil is 0.10 V. Convert it into a 25 V voltmeter.

  1. Full-scale current: I_g = V_g / R_g = 0.10 / 40 = 0.0025 A (2.5 mA).
  2. Total resistance: R_total = V / I_g = 25.0 / 0.0025 = 10 000 Ω.
  3. Multiplier: R_m = 10 000 − 40 = 9 960 Ω, in series.
R_m = 9 960 Ω.

Part A · Home investigation: the water shunt

How much flow takes the easy path?

You need: an empty 1.5 L plastic bottle, a narrow coffee stirrer or thin straw (~3 mm), a wide drinking straw (~6–8 mm), a nail (an adult makes the holes), tape or candle wax, two identical cups, a measuring cup or 20 mL syringe, water and a clock.

  1. Have an adult make two holes on opposite sides, ~3 cm above the base and at the same height. Push a 4 cm piece of the narrow straw into one and a 4 cm piece of the wide straw into the other, and seal round each.
  2. Cover both straw ends, fill the bottle to a marked line, and hold one cup under each straw.
  3. Release both straws together, collect for 20 seconds, then cover both ends again.
  4. Measure the volume in each cup and work out the percentage of the total flow through the narrow straw (the “coil”) and the wide straw (the “shunt”). Repeat twice more and average.
  5. Replace the wide straw with a second narrow straw and repeat, to see what happens when the “shunt” is no longer an easy path.
  6. Pour the water onto a plant and dry the area.

A good answer shows that both straws feel the same “push” (the same water depth, like the same p.d. across parallel branches), but the wide straw offers much less opposition, so it carries most of the flow — just as a low-resistance shunt carries most of the line current and protects the coil. When the two paths are alike, the flow splits roughly equally, like equal resistors in parallel. It links the result to Nkechi's 3 A starter test (the thinner the coil's path compared with the shunt, the smaller its share), states one limitation (water flow through a straw is not exactly ohmic) and one source of error with its direction (the water level fell during the 20 s, so both flows came out a little low, though the percentage split changed much less).

Part B · Written paper (30 marks)

1. Definitions

3 marks

Define each term: (a) shunt; (b) multiplier; (c) the full-scale deflection current of a galvanometer.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) A shunt is a resistor of very low value connected in parallel with a galvanometer coil, so that most of the current to be measured bypasses the coil, turning it into an ammeter. (1) (b) A multiplier is a resistor of very high value connected in series with the coil, so the coil current stays within its limit when a large p.d. is applied, turning it into a voltmeter. (1) (c) The full-scale deflection current is the smallest current through the coil that moves the pointer to the end of its scale. (1)

Mark scheme note: In (a) and (b) both the size (low / high) and the position (parallel / series) are needed. In (c) the idea of the pointer reaching the end of the scale is required.

2. Why Convert at All?

3 marks

(a) Explain why a galvanometer of full-scale current 1 mA (a more sensitive one than Nkechi's 15 mA coil) cannot be used directly to measure the 3 A current to Emeka's starter. (b) Explain why an ideal ammeter has a very low resistance. (c) Explain why an ideal voltmeter has a very high resistance.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) A current thousands of times larger than its full-scale value would drive the pointer far off the scale, and the fine coil would overheat and burn out. (1) (b) It is placed in series, so a very low resistance adds almost nothing to the circuit and leaves the current practically unchanged. (1) (c) It is placed in parallel across a component, so a very high resistance draws almost no current and leaves the p.d. practically unchanged. (1)

Mark scheme note: In (b) and (c) the position (series / parallel) must be linked to its effect; saying an ammeter needs a high resistance “so the current does not damage it” is a recognised misconception and scores zero.

3. Deriving the Formulae

5 marks

A galvanometer has coil resistance Rg and full-scale current Ig. (a) Derive Rs for an ammeter of full-scale reading I. (b) Derive Rm for a voltmeter of full-scale reading V.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) The coil and the shunt are in parallel, so the p.d. across each is the same. (1) At full scale the coil carries Ig, so the shunt carries I − Ig. (1) Equating the p.d.s, IgRg = (I − Ig)Rs, so Rs = IgRg ÷ (I − Ig). (1) (b) The coil and the multiplier are in series and carry the same current, Ig at full scale, so V = Ig(Rg + Rm). (1) Rearranging, Rm = V ÷ Ig − Rg. (1)

Mark scheme note: Writing the shunt current as I instead of I − Ig loses a mark. Stopping at R = V ÷ Ig and calling it the multiplier loses the last mark: that is the whole meter's resistance.

4. Building an Ammeter

5 marks

A galvanometer of coil resistance 45 Ω gives full-scale deflection at 2.0 mA, to become a 1.5 A ammeter. (a) Coil p.d. at full scale. (b) Shunt current at full scale. (c) Shunt resistance. (d) Total resistance of the finished ammeter and how it is connected.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) Ig = 0.0020 A; Vg = IgRg = 0.0020 × 45 = 0.090 V. (1) (b) I − Ig = 1.5 − 0.0020 = 1.498 A. (1) (c) Rs = Vg ÷ (I − Ig) = 0.090 ÷ 1.498 = 0.0601 Ω, in parallel. (1) (d) R = (45 × 0.0601) ÷ (45 + 0.0601) = 0.0600 Ω, so the ammeter is connected in series with the circuit. (1) (1 mark for correct units/placement throughout.)

Mark scheme note: Using 2.0 instead of 0.0020 is the mA-as-A slip and loses marks, with error carried forward afterwards. In (d) both the value and “in series” are needed.

5. Building a Voltmeter

5 marks

The same galvanometer (45 Ω, full scale 2.0 mA) becomes a voltmeter. (a) Total resistance for a 0–30 V range. (b) The multiplier and its connection. (c) Extra resistance to extend 30 V to 75 V. (d) The current drawn when it reads 12 V on the 0–30 V range.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) Rtotal = V ÷ Ig = 30 ÷ 0.0020 = 15 000 Ω. (1) (b) Rm = 15 000 − 45 = 14 955 Ω, in series with the coil. (1) (1) (c) New total = 75 ÷ 0.0020 = 37 500 Ω, so the extra series resistance is 37 500 − 15 000 = 22 500 Ω. (1) (d) I = V ÷ Rtotal = 12 ÷ 15 000 = 8.0 × 10⁻⁴ A = 0.80 mA. (1)

Mark scheme note: Giving 15 000 Ω as the multiplier in (b) scores zero for the value. In (c), subtracting only 45 Ω from 37 500 Ω gives the whole new multiplier, not the extra resistance added.

6. Practical: Shunt Against Range

4 marks

For a galvanometer with Rg = 20 Ω and Ig = 10 mA, the shunt Rs was found for ranges I = 0.05, 0.10, 0.20, 0.50, 1.00 A (Rs = 5.00, 2.22, ?, 0.408, 0.202 Ω). (a) Find the missing Rs. (b) Describe the graph of Rs against I. (c) What should go on the horizontal axis for a straight line through the origin? (d) State its gradient and meaning.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) Rs = (0.010 × 20) ÷ (0.20 − 0.010) = 0.20 ÷ 0.19 = 1.05 Ω. (1) (b) A smooth curve falling steeply at first, then levelling towards zero as I rises; it is not a straight line. (1) (c) 1 ÷ (I − Ig). (1) (d) Gradient = IgRg = 0.010 × 20 = 0.20 V, the p.d. across the coil at full-scale deflection. (1)

Mark scheme note: Using I instead of I − Ig in (a) gives 1.00 Ω and loses the mark. In (d) the unit (V) and the meaning are both needed.

7. Practical: Precautions and Errors

3 marks

(a) State two precautions taken when the converted meters were built and tested. (b) The shunt of a home-made ammeter was joined to the coil with poor, high-resistance contacts. State and explain the effect on the ammeter's readings.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) Any two, in the past tense: the shunt and multiplier values were calculated before any connection was made, so the coil was never overloaded (1); the shunt was joined with short, thick, tight connections of negligible resistance (1); the correct terminals and polarity were used (1); parallax error was avoided by reading the pointer square to the scale (1). (Max 2.) (b) The readings were too high: the contact resistance made the shunt branch effectively larger, so a bigger share of the line current passed through the coil and it deflected further than it should for that current. (1)

Mark scheme note: Precautions given as commands or in the future tense lose the marks. In (b) the direction (too high) and the reason (more current diverted through the coil) are both needed.

8. The Starter-Test Mistake

2 marks

Tobe connects a finished ammeter of total resistance 0.050 Ω directly across Emeka's battery (EMF 12.0 V, internal resistance 1.2 Ω) instead of in series with the starter. (a) Calculate the current that flows. (b) Explain why this is a serious mistake.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) I = E ÷ (r + RA) = 12.0 ÷ (1.2 + 0.050) = 12.0 ÷ 1.25 = 9.6 A. (1) (b) A low-resistance ammeter across the battery acts almost as a short circuit, so a current far above the meter's range flows, which can overheat and burn out the shunt and coil and heat the battery; an ammeter must always be connected in series. (1)

Mark scheme note: Leaving out the internal resistance in (a) gives 240 A and loses the mark. In (b) the idea of a near short circuit with a dangerously large current is required.
