Practical analysis questions

Answer these five questions, then compare with the model answers.

  1. For a low-resistance pump, which cell arrangement gives more current, and why?
  2. Why is a parallel combination's resistance smaller than any one branch?
  3. At what load is the output power greatest?
  4. Why does maximum power not mean maximum efficiency?
  5. Why does a worn (high-r) battery run hot and waste power?
Model answers unlocks after you try
  1. Parallel. For a low-resistance load the internal resistance dominates, and identical cells in parallel give rₜ = r/n, so the current I = E/(R + r/n) is larger than the series current E_t/(R + nr).
  2. Adding a branch gives the charge an extra path, so for the same p.d. more total current flows; since R = V/I, a larger current at the same p.d. means a smaller combined resistance — smaller than any single branch.
  3. At R = r, where the load power P = E²R/(R + r)² reaches its single peak (here 12.5 W at R = 0.5 Ω).
  4. At R = r the efficiency η = R/(R + r) is only 50 %, so half the power is wasted inside the cell. High efficiency needs R ≫ r, where the output power is small. The two are not maximised together.
  5. A worn battery has a large internal resistance r, so under load it dissipates a big I²r inside itself as heat, which both warms it and lowers its efficiency R/(R + r).
3Make sense · 15 min

Networks, cell arrays, power & efficiency

Now connect what you measured to the equations WAEC, NECO and JAMB expect.

Movement break (45 min mark, 5 minutes): stand up. Walk slowly in a single file loop around your seat — that is a series path, one route for everyone. Now split into two lanes side by side and walk — that is parallel, the current sharing between branches. Do it twice, shake out your hands and sit down.

Series: one path, so the current is the same everywhere; the p.d.s add; R = R₁ + R₂ + …. Parallel: the branches share the same p.d.; the currents add (I = I₁ + I₂ + …); ¹⁄R = ¹⁄R₁ + ¹⁄R₂ + …, and for two branches R = R₁R₂/(R₁ + R₂).

Cell arrays: n identical cells in series give Eₜ = nE and rₜ = nr (more voltage, more internal resistance). n identical cells in parallel give Eₜ = E and rₜ = r/n (same voltage, less internal resistance, more current for a low-R load). These parallel rules (Eₜ = E, rₜ = r/n) hold only for identical cells. For n identical cells, parallel delivers more current than series exactly when R < r.

Equations

Rseries = ΣR  ·  ¹⁄Rparallel = Σ ¹⁄R
W = IVt = I²Rt = (V²/R)t  ·  P = IV = I²R = V²/R
η = R/(R + r) = V/E  (× 100 %)

The kilowatt-hour on the light bill: one "unit" on a NEPA/PHCN prepaid meter is 1 kWh, a unit of energy, not power. 1 kWh = 1 kW × 1 h = 1 000 W × 3 600 s = 3.6 × 10⁶ J. A 750 W freezer running for 8.0 h uses E = Pt = 0.75 kW × 8.0 h = 6.0 kWh (six units) = 2.16 × 10⁷ J.

Worked examples, step by step

Try each on paper first, then check the working.

EXAMPLE 1

A mixed network

A battery of EMF 14.0 V and internal resistance 1.0 Ω feeds a 3.0 Ω resistor in series with a parallel pair of 6.0 Ω and 12.0 Ω. Find the total current and the current in the 6.0 Ω branch.

  1. Parallel pair: R_p = (6.0 × 12.0)/(6.0 + 12.0) = 72/18 = 4.0 Ω.
  2. External total: R_ext = 3.0 + 4.0 = 7.0 Ω.
  3. Total current: I = E/(R_ext + r) = 14.0/(7.0 + 1.0) = 14.0/8.0 = 1.75 A.
  4. Branch current: V_p = I × R_p = 1.75 × 4.0 = 7.0 V, so I₆ = V_p/6.0 = 7.0/6.0 = 1.17 A.
I = 1.75 A · I₆ = 1.17 A
EXAMPLE 2

Cell arrays compared

Four identical cells, each 1.5 V with r = 2.0 Ω, feed a 1.0 Ω pump. Compare the current when they are all in parallel with when they are all in series. (Emeka's story has three cells; here we use four.)

  1. Parallel: Eₜ = 1.5 V, rₜ = r/n = 2.0/4 = 0.5 Ω; I = 1.5/(1.0 + 0.5) = 1.5/1.5 = 1.0 A.
  2. Series: Eₜ = 4 × 1.5 = 6.0 V, rₜ = 4 × 2.0 = 8.0 Ω; I = 6.0/(1.0 + 8.0) = 6.0/9.0 = 0.67 A.
  3. Compare: 1.0 A > 0.67 A, so for this low-resistance pump the parallel array delivers the larger current (R < r).
Parallel 1.0 A · series 0.67 A · parallel wins here
EXAMPLE 3

Power and efficiency

A cell of EMF 5.0 V and internal resistance 0.5 Ω drives a 4.5 Ω load. Find the power delivered to the load and the efficiency.

  1. Current: I = E/(R + r) = 5.0/(4.5 + 0.5) = 5.0/5.0 = 1.0 A.
  2. Power: load P = I²R = 1.0² × 4.5 = 4.5 W; total EI = 5.0 × 1.0 = 5.0 W.
  3. Efficiency: η = P/EI × 100 = 4.5/5.0 × 100 = 90 % (check: R/(R + r) = 4.5/5.0 = 90 %).
P = 4.5 W · η = 90 %

Part A · Home investigation: two bulbs, two ways

Which arrangement shines brighter and drains the cells faster?

You need: two identical small torch bulbs (2.5 V or 3.8 V), two new AA cells, a two-cell holder (or tape the cells inside a paper tube), four short stripped wires, cellotape and a clock. Use only torch cells. Never use mains electricity, and never join the cell terminals with a bare wire.

  1. Join the two cells in series (about 3 V). Connect one bulb alone and rate its brightness from 0 (off) to 5 (very bright).
  2. Connect the two bulbs in series across the cells and rate each bulb.
  3. Connect the two bulbs in parallel (each joined directly across the cells) and rate each bulb.
  4. In the parallel set-up, unscrew one bulb and note what happens to the other. Repeat for the series set-up.
  5. Leave each set-up on for 10 minutes, then feel the cells gently and note whether they are cool or warm. Disconnect when you finish.

A good answer shows that in series the bulbs share the cells' p.d., so each gets about half and glows dimly; in parallel each gets the full p.d. and glows as brightly as a single bulb, but the cells supply about twice the current and run down faster (P = IV). Removing one bulb breaks the only series path but leaves the other parallel branch complete, which is why house and workshop lamps are wired in parallel. The cells feel warm in the parallel test because the larger current wastes more power inside them as I²r, like Emeka's hot battery. One limitation with its direction: brightness judged by eye is subjective, and the cells' internal resistance rises as they warm, so ratings taken late in the parallel run come out too low.

Part B · Written paper (30 marks)

1. Definitions

3 marks

Define each term: (a) electrical power; (b) the kilowatt-hour; (c) the efficiency of a cell in a circuit.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) Electrical power is the rate at which electrical energy is changed into other forms, measured in watts (1 W = 1 J s⁻¹). (1) (b) The kilowatt-hour is the energy used when a 1 kW device works for one hour; it equals 3.6 × 10⁶ J. (1) (c) The efficiency of a cell is the fraction (or percentage) of the total power it supplies that reaches the external load: η = useful power ÷ total power = R/(R + r). (1)

Mark scheme note: In (a) "energy" alone, without "rate" or "per second", scores zero. In (b) calling the kilowatt-hour a unit of power is the commonest error and scores zero; it is a unit of energy. In (c) the answer must compare useful (load) power with total power.

2. Why Parallel?

3 marks

(a) Explain why the combined resistance of two resistors in parallel is smaller than either resistor alone. (b) Give two reasons why the lamps in Engr. Nkechi's workshop are wired in parallel rather than in series.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) A second branch gives the charge an extra path, so for the same p.d. more total current flows; since R = V/I, a larger current at the same p.d. means a smaller combined resistance. (1) (b) Each lamp receives the full supply p.d., so every lamp works at its rated brightness. (1) If one lamp fails or is switched off, the other branches stay complete and remain lit. (1)

Mark scheme note: In (a) a bare formula without the idea of an extra path or larger current scores zero. In (b) "it uses less current" is not credited: parallel lamps draw more total current. The two reasons must be different.

3. Deriving the Parallel Rule

4 marks

(a) Two resistors R₁ and R₂ are connected in parallel across a p.d. V. Starting from the behaviour of current and p.d. in parallel, derive the expression for their combined resistance R. (b) Show that two equal resistors, each R₀, in parallel have a combined resistance R₀/2.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) Both resistors have the same p.d. V. (1) The supply current divides at the junction, so I = I₁ + I₂. (1) Substituting I = V/R, I₁ = V/R₁ and I₂ = V/R₂ gives V/R = V/R₁ + V/R₂; dividing by V, 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂. (1) (b) 1/R = 1/R₀ + 1/R₀ = 2/R₀, so R = R₀/2. (1)

Mark scheme note: Writing "the current is the same in each branch" is wrong and loses a mark. In (b), "half because there are two" with no working is not credited.

4. Solving a Mixed Network

5 marks

A battery of EMF 24.0 V and internal resistance 1.0 Ω is connected to a 5.0 Ω resistor in series with a parallel pair of 6.0 Ω and 3.0 Ω. Calculate: (a) the combined resistance of the parallel pair; (b) the current drawn from the battery; (c) the terminal p.d.; (d) the p.d. across the parallel pair; (e) the current in the 3.0 Ω resistor.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) R_p = (6.0 × 3.0)/(6.0 + 3.0) = 18/9.0 = 2.0 Ω. (1) (b) R = 5.0 + 2.0 = 7.0 Ω; I = E/(R + r) = 24.0/(7.0 + 1.0) = 3.0 A. (1) (c) V = E − Ir = 24.0 − 3.0 × 1.0 = 21 V (check: IR = 3.0 × 7.0 = 21 V). (1) (d) V_p = I × R_p = 3.0 × 2.0 = 6.0 V. (1) (e) I₃ = V_p/3.0 = 6.0/3.0 = 2.0 A. (1)

Mark scheme note: Leaving out the 1.0 Ω internal resistance in (b) gives 3.4 A and loses that mark. Giving 24.0 V as the terminal p.d. in (c) is the voltmeter-reads-EMF trap and scores zero. Units required on every answer.

5. Series or Parallel for the Pump?

5 marks

Six identical cells, each 1.5 V with r = 0.60 Ω, drive a pump of resistance 0.50 Ω. (a) Find the current with all six in series. (b) Find the current with all six in parallel. (c) State which Emeka should choose, supporting it with the power delivered to the pump in each case.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) Series: Eₜ = 6 × 1.5 = 9.0 V, rₜ = 6 × 0.60 = 3.6 Ω. (1) I = 9.0/(0.50 + 3.6) = 9.0/4.1 = 2.2 A. (1) (b) Parallel: Eₜ = 1.5 V, rₜ = 0.60/6 = 0.10 Ω. (1) I = 1.5/(0.50 + 0.10) = 1.5/0.60 = 2.5 A. (1) (c) Parallel: pump power I²R = 2.5² × 0.50 = 3.1 W, against 2.2² × 0.50 = 2.4 W in series, so parallel delivers more current and more useful power to this low-resistance pump. (1)

Mark scheme note: Writing Eₜ = 9.0 V for the parallel case, or rₜ = 3.6 Ω for it, is the classic mix-up and loses that step. Ignoring internal resistance (18 A in series) scores zero. In (c) the choice must be backed by numbers.

6. Practical: The Load-Power Curve

5 marks

A cell of EMF 3.0 V and internal resistance 2.0 Ω is connected to a series of load resistors. For R = 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 4.0, 8.0 Ω the currents are 1.20, 1.00, 0.75, ?, 0.30 A and the powers 0.72, 1.00, 1.13, ?, 0.72 W. (a) Find the missing current and power at R = 4.0 Ω. (b) Describe the line drawn through a graph of P (vertical) against R. (c) State the R at which P is greatest and what it shows. (d) State the efficiency at that load.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) I = E/(R + r) = 3.0/(4.0 + 2.0) = 0.50 A. (1) P = I²R = 0.50² × 4.0 = 1.00 W. (1) (b) A smooth curve that rises to a single peak and then falls, drawn freehand through the points, not a straight line of best fit. (1) (c) P is greatest at R = 2.0 Ω; maximum power is delivered when the load equals the internal resistance, confirming r = 2.0 Ω. (1) (d) η = R/(R + r) = 2.0/4.0 × 100 % = 50 %. (1)

Mark scheme note: A ruled straight line, or dot-to-dot, loses (b) because power against load is not linear. In (c) the reason (R = r for maximum power) is required. 100 % in (d) confuses maximum power with maximum efficiency and scores zero.

7. Practical: Precautions and Errors

3 marks

(a) State two precautions taken while measuring load power and efficiency on the cell-array bench. (b) During a long run the cells polarised (reaction products built up at their plates). State and explain the effect on the efficiency values recorded for the later loads.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) Any two, in the past tense: the key was opened between readings so the cells did not polarise (1); the same cells were used throughout so only the arrangement or load changed (1); the connections were cleaned and tightened (1); parallax error was avoided by reading each meter square to its scale (1). (Maximum 2.) (b) The later efficiency values were too low, because polarisation raised the internal resistance r, so a larger share of the power was wasted inside the cells and R/(R + r) fell. (1)

Mark scheme note: Precautions written as commands or in the future tense lose the marks. "Avoid errors" is not a precaution. In (b) the direction (too low) and the reason (rise in r) are both needed.

8. Why Emeka's Battery Runs Hot

2 marks

Emeka's worn battery has EMF 12.0 V and internal resistance 1.2 Ω and drives his 2.8 Ω starter. (a) Calculate the efficiency of the battery in this circuit. (b) A new battery of the same EMF has an internal resistance of only 0.10 Ω. Explain, in terms of power, why the worn battery runs hotter.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) η = R/(R + r) = 2.8/(2.8 + 1.2) × 100 % = 2.8/4.0 × 100 % = 70 %. (1) (b) The worn battery's larger internal resistance turns a much bigger share of its power into heat inside itself (I²r): about 30 % heats the battery, against only about 3 % for the new one (0.10/2.9). (1)

Mark scheme note: In (a) using r/(R + r) gives the wasted 30 % and scores zero. In (b) the answer must link the higher temperature to energy dissipated inside the battery as I²r; "the battery is old" is not credited.
