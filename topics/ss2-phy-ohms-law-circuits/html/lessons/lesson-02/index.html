Practical analysis questions

Answer these five questions, then compare with the model answers.

  1. Why is the open-circuit reading the EMF?
  2. Why does the terminal p.d. fall as the current rises?
  3. What does the vertical intercept represent, and what does the gradient represent?
  4. Why should the voltmeter resistance be high?
  5. Why does a battery feel warm under heavy load?
Model answers unlocks after you try
  1. With the key open almost no current flows, so Ir is practically zero and the voltmeter shows V = E − Ir = E, the full energy per coulomb the cell supplies.
  2. A bigger current means a bigger drop Ir across the internal resistance, so less is left for the terminals: V = E − Ir falls as I rises.
  3. The vertical intercept (I = 0) is the EMF E; the magnitude of the gradient (−ΔV/ΔI) is the internal resistance r, from V = E − rI.
  4. A high-resistance voltmeter draws almost no current on "open circuit", so the Ir it causes is negligible and the EMF reading is not pulled below E.
  5. Energy is wasted as heat inside the cell at the rate I²r, so the heavier the current the warmer the battery gets.
3Make sense · 15 min

Where the missing volts go

Now we connect the readings you took to the names and equations WAEC, NECO and JAMB expect.

Movement break (45 min mark, 5 minutes): stand up and act out the circuit. Hold both hands at your chest as the cell's EMF. Push them out fully: that is the energy per coulomb, E. Now take a small step back each time you "feed a bigger current" — the further you step, the more energy you keep for yourself (that is the lost volts Ir) and the less reaches your outstretched hands (the terminal p.d.). Do it three times, stepping back a little further each time, then shake out your arms and sit down.

Electromotive force (EMF, E) is the total electrical energy the source gives to each coulomb driven round the whole circuit, in volts; it is the open-circuit terminal reading (I = 0). Internal resistance (r) is the opposition to current inside the source itself — its electrolyte and electrodes. Lost volts is the p.d. used up inside, Ir. Terminal p.d. (V) is what is left for the external circuit, V = E − Ir = IR.

The equations

E = I(R + r)
V = E − Ir   and   V = IR
lost volts = Ir   two readings: E = I₁(R₁ + r) = I₂(R₂ + r)

Open circuit versus on load

ConditionCurrent ILost volts IrVoltmeter reads
Open circuit (I = 0)00E (the full EMF)
On load (I > 0)E ÷ (R + r)IrE − Ir (the terminal p.d.)

The heavier the load (the smaller R), the larger the current, the larger the lost volts and the smaller the terminal p.d. The EMF itself never changes as the load changes.

Worked examples, step by step

EXAMPLE 1

The mystery battery

Emeka's battery reads 12.0 V on open circuit and 8.4 V while driving the 2.8 Ω starter. Find (a) the current, (b) the lost volts, (c) the internal resistance.

  1. Current from the terminal p.d.: I = V ÷ R = 8.4 ÷ 2.8 = 3.0 A.
  2. Lost volts: Ir = E − V = 12.0 − 8.4 = 3.6 V.
  3. Internal resistance: r = (E − V) ÷ I = 3.6 ÷ 3.0 = 1.2 Ω.
I = 3.0 A · lost volts = 3.6 V · r = 1.2 Ω (check: E = I(R + r) = 3.0 × (2.8 + 1.2) = 12.0 V).
EXAMPLE 2

Two readings, same cell

A cell drives 0.80 A through a 4.0 Ω lamp, and 0.50 A through a 7.0 Ω lamp. Find its internal resistance and EMF.

  1. The EMF is the same both times: 0.80(4.0 + r) = 0.50(7.0 + r).
  2. Expand both sides: 3.2 + 0.80r = 3.5 + 0.50r, so 0.30r = 0.30.
  3. Internal resistance: r = 1.0 Ω.
  4. EMF: E = 0.80 × (4.0 + 1.0) = 0.80 × 5.0 = 4.0 V.
r = 1.0 Ω · E = 4.0 V.
EXAMPLE 3

E and r from a graph, then a load

A V–I graph for a cell has a vertical intercept of 6.0 V and a gradient of −1.5 V A⁻¹. Find the terminal p.d. when the current is 1.2 A.

  1. Read the graph: intercept gives E = 6.0 V and the gradient magnitude gives r = 1.5 Ω.
  2. Lost volts at this current: Ir = 1.2 × 1.5 = 1.8 V.
  3. Terminal p.d.: V = E − Ir = 6.0 − 1.8 = 4.2 V.
V = 4.2 V.

The big idea of Lesson 2: EMF is what the cell can give; terminal p.d. is what survives after Ir is paid inside. The missing volts never left the chemistry — they were spent on the battery's own internal resistance. A tired battery has a larger r, so it drops more volts under load even though its EMF is unchanged.

Part A · Home investigation: new cell or tired cell?

New cell or tired cell: whose internal resistance is bigger?

You need: one new and one well-used AA (1.5 V) torch cell, a 10 Ω resistor rated at least 0.5 W, two short clip leads, and a digital multimeter on the 20 V DC range. Use only small torch cells: never a car, keke or inverter battery, never the mains, and never join a cell's two terminals with a bare wire.

  1. Measure the voltage across the new cell with nothing else connected. Record it as E.
  2. Clip the 10 Ω resistor across the cell and at once read the voltage across the terminals as V, then unclip (leave it on for no more than about 10 seconds).
  3. Repeat steps 1–2 two more times and average the readings.
  4. Do the same for the used cell.
  5. For each cell work out I = V/10, the lost volts E − V, and r = (E − V)/I.
  6. If an adult has a car or keke, watch from a safe distance whether the headlamps dim while the engine is started, and note it.

Both cells read close to their EMF on open circuit, but the terminal p.d. drops once current flows. The used cell's EMF is only a little lower, while its internal resistance is several times larger, so it loses far more volts under load and delivers less current. The current is worked out from V/R using the terminal reading, not the EMF. A strong answer links this to Emeka: his battery's resting 12.0 V hides an internal resistance that only shows up when the starter draws a large current, just as the headlamps dim during starting. One source of error with its direction: leaving the resistor connected too long let the cell's p.d. sag as it drained, so V was too low and r came out too high; the meter's own resistance is very high, so the open-circuit E was only very slightly too low.

Part B · Written paper (30 marks)

1. Definitions

3 marks

Define (a) the electromotive force of a cell; (b) the internal resistance of a cell; (c) the terminal potential difference of a cell.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) The EMF of a cell is the total energy it supplies to each coulomb of charge it drives round the complete circuit, including through the cell itself; it equals the p.d. across the terminals when no current is drawn. (1) (b) The internal resistance is the opposition to current that comes from the cell's own materials, its electrolyte and plates. (1) (c) The terminal p.d. is the p.d. across the cell's terminals while it is supplying a current; it is the energy per coulomb available to the external circuit, V = E − Ir. (1)

Mark scheme note: Equivalent wordings that keep the key ideas are credited. In (a) the idea of energy per coulomb for the whole circuit (or the open-circuit reading) is required; "the voltage of the battery" scores zero. In (b) the opposition must be placed inside the cell. In (c) the condition "while current flows" is needed.

2. Where the 3.6 V went

3 marks

Chinedu's voltmeter reads 12.0 V across Emeka's resting battery and 8.4 V while the starter runs. Explain why the two readings differ, without using any numbers other than those given.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(1) With the starter off, almost no current flows through the battery, so nothing is dropped across its internal resistance and the voltmeter shows the EMF, 12.0 V. (1) (2) When the starter runs, a large current flows through the battery's internal resistance, and a p.d. Ir is used up inside the battery (as heat), here 3.6 V. (1) (3) The voltmeter now shows only what is left for the external circuit, the terminal p.d. V = E − Ir = 8.4 V; the EMF itself has not fallen. (1)

Mark scheme note: "The battery is weak" or "the voltage is lost in the wires" scores zero. Point 2 needs both the current and the internal resistance. Point 3 needs the statement that the EMF is unchanged.

3. Deriving the terminal p.d. relation

4 marks

A cell of EMF E and internal resistance r drives a current I through an external resistor R. (a) Starting from E = I(R + r), show that V = E − Ir. (b) Show that V = ER/(R + r). (c) Explain what V becomes when R is made very large.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) Expanding, E = IR + Ir. (1) The terminal p.d. is the p.d. across R, V = IR, so E = V + Ir and V = E − Ir. (1) (b) The current is I = E/(R + r); substituting into V = IR gives V = ER/(R + r). (1) (c) As R becomes very large, R/(R + r) approaches 1, so V approaches E: on open circuit the terminal p.d. equals the EMF. (1)

Mark scheme note: Each mark needs its own step; writing V = E − Ir as a remembered formula earns only the second mark in (a). In (c) "V becomes zero" is the short-circuit case (R → 0) and is not credited.

4. Emeka's battery and the horn

5 marks

Emeka's battery (EMF 12.0 V, internal resistance 1.2 Ω) is connected to his 6.8 Ω horn alone. (a) Calculate the current. (b) The terminal p.d. (c) The lost volts. (d) The rate at which energy is wasted as heat inside the battery. (e) The percentage of the battery's total power delivered to the horn.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) I = E/(R + r) = 12.0 ÷ (6.8 + 1.2) = 12.0 ÷ 8.0 = 1.5 A. (1) (b) V = IR = 1.5 × 6.8 = 10.2 V. (1) (c) Lost volts = Ir = 1.5 × 1.2 = 1.8 V (check: 12.0 − 10.2 = 1.8 V). (1) (d) P wasted = I²r = 1.5² × 1.2 = 2.7 W. (1) (e) Power to horn = I²R = 1.5² × 6.8 = 15.3 W; total power = EI = 12.0 × 1.5 = 18.0 W; percentage = 15.3 ÷ 18.0 × 100 = 85 %. (1)

Mark scheme note: Error carried forward is allowed. I = E/R = 1.76 A (internal resistance left out) loses (a). In (d) giving Ir (1.8) with the unit W loses the mark. In (e) R/(R + r) × 100 = 85 % is accepted.

5. Two loads, one battery

4 marks

A solar-charged battery drives 0.40 A through a 22.0 Ω lamp, and 1.00 A through an 8.5 Ω fan motor. Assume both loads behave as fixed resistances. (a) Write an equation for the EMF in each case. (b) Solve them for the internal resistance. (c) Find the EMF.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) E = 0.40(22.0 + r) and E = 1.00(8.5 + r). (1) (b) Equating: 8.8 + 0.40r = 8.5 + 1.00r. (1) 0.30 = 0.60r, so r = 0.50 Ω. (1) (c) E = 0.40 × (22.0 + 0.50) = 0.40 × 22.5 = 9.0 V (check: 1.00 × 9.0 = 9.0 V). (1)

Mark scheme note: Both EMF equations must include r. An algebra slip in (b) loses only the mark where it occurs; (c) may still be earned by error carried forward. "r = 0.5" without a unit loses that mark.

6. Practical: V–I graph for a cell

6 marks

Chinedu varied the load on an accumulator cell and recorded I / A: 0.20, 0.40, 0.60, 0.80, 1.00 and V / V: 1.92, 1.85, 1.76, 1.69, 1.60. (a) He is told to "plot V against I". State which quantity goes on the vertical axis and why a straight line is expected. (b) From the line extended to I = 0, find the EMF. (c) Using a large triangle, find the gradient, and hence r. (d) Find the short-circuit current. (e) Find the terminal p.d. when I = 0.50 A.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) V goes on the vertical axis; a straight line is expected because V = E − Ir with E and r constant, so V falls linearly as I increases. (1) (b) The line meets the V axis at E = 2.00 V (accept 1.98–2.02 V). (1) (c) Using (0.10 A, 1.96 V) and (1.10 A, 1.56 V): gradient = (1.56 − 1.96) ÷ (1.10 − 0.10) = −0.40 ÷ 1.00 = −0.40 V A⁻¹. (1) r = magnitude = 0.40 Ω (accept 0.38–0.42 Ω). (1) (d) At short circuit V = 0, so I = E/r = 2.00 ÷ 0.40 = 5.0 A. (1) (e) V = E − Ir = 2.00 − 0.50 × 0.40 = 1.80 V. (1)

Mark scheme note: The line of best fit is a straight line; joining the points dot to dot is not accepted. A triangle covering less than half the line loses the first mark in (c). Giving r as −0.40 Ω loses the second mark in (c). Error carried forward from (b) and (c) is allowed in (d) and (e).

7. Practical: precautions and errors

3 marks

(a) State two precautions taken in the experiment of question 6. (b) The cell polarised during the run (reaction products built up at its plates), and its internal resistance rose. State and explain the effect on the value of r obtained from the graph.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) Any two, in the past tense: the key was opened between readings so the cell did not polarise and the resistor did not heat up (1); the open-circuit EMF was read with a high-resistance voltmeter so the current drawn was negligible (1); the connections were tightened and the terminals cleaned (1); parallax was avoided by reading each meter square to the scale (1); readings were repeated and spread over a wide range (1). (Maximum 2.) (b) The value of r was too high, because the later (higher-current) terminal readings sagged below the line they should have followed, making the gradient steeper. (1)

Mark scheme note: Commands and future-tense statements score zero, as do vague precautions. In (b) both the direction (too high) and the reason (later points too low, steeper gradient) are needed.

8. The verdict on Tobe's claim

2 marks

Tobe says Emeka's battery "has lost its voltage" and must be thrown away. Using the evidence from Lesson 2, give two reasons why this conclusion is not justified yet.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(1) On open circuit the battery still reads 12.0 V, so its EMF has not fallen; the chemistry is still supplying the full energy per coulomb. (1) (2) The drop to 8.4 V happens only while current flows, so it is caused by lost volts across the internal resistance (1.2 Ω); the right next step is to measure r and compare it with a healthy battery's, not to discard the battery on the EMF. (1)

Mark scheme note: "The meter is faulty" scores zero. The second mark needs the link between the drop and the current through the internal resistance; "the voltage is lost in the wires" is not credited.
