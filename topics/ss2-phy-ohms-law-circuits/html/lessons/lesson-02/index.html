3Make sense · 12 min

The Complete Circuit Law & Two-Readings Method

Every battery or cell consists of a pure voltage source of electromotive force E in series with its own internal resistance r.

1 · Complete Circuit Equation & Lost Volts

By conservation of energy, the total electrical energy per coulomb supplied by the cell (E) equals the useful potential difference across the external load (V = IR) plus the potential drop wasted inside the cell (v = Ir):

E = V + v = IR + Ir = I(R + r)   ⇒   I = E ÷ (R + r)
Terminal P.D.:   V = E − Ir    ·    Lost Volts:   v = E − V = Ir

2 · The “Two Readings, Same Cell” Archetype

In WAEC and JAMB exams, you are often told that a cell supplies current I1 when connected to resistor R1, and current I2 when connected to resistor R2. Because the cell’s EMF E and internal resistance r are unchanged:

E = I1(R1 + r) = I2(R2 + r)   ⇒   r = (I2R2 − I1R1) ÷ (I1 − I2)
Short-circuit (maximum) current when R = 0:   Imax = E ÷ r

At the battery exit door, pay an internal toll of Ir = 3.6 J (lost volts) and carry the remaining V = 8.4 J out to the starter motor!

4Apply · Worked examples

Worked examples, step by step

Master the three classic exam calculation patterns on EMF, lost volts, and internal resistance.

EXAMPLE 1

Solving the Aba Workshop battery drop (V = E − Ir)

An accumulator has an open-circuit EMF of 12.0 V. When connected across a 2.8 Ω starter resistor, the terminal potential difference drops to 8.4 V. Calculate: (a) the current I flowing in the circuit, (b) the lost volts v inside the accumulator, and (c) the internal resistance r of the accumulator.

  1. Current I from external load: I = V ÷ R = 8.4 ÷ 2.8 = 3.0 A.
  2. Lost volts v: v = E − V = 12.0 − 8.4 = 3.6 V.
  3. Internal resistance r: r = v ÷ I = 3.6 ÷ 3.0 = 1.2 Ω.
(a) I = 3.0 A  ·  (b) v = 3.6 V  ·  (c) r = 1.2 Ω
EXAMPLE 2

“Two readings, same cell” simultaneous calculation

A cell supplies a current of 0.80 A when connected to a 4.0 Ω external resistor, and a current of 0.50 A when connected to a 7.0 Ω external resistor. Calculate: (a) the internal resistance r of the cell, and (b) the electromotive force E of the cell.

  1. Equate E for both loads: E = I₁(R₁ + r) = I₂(R₂ + r) ⇒ 0.80(4.0 + r) = 0.50(7.0 + r).
  2. Expand and solve for r: 3.20 + 0.80r = 3.50 + 0.50r ⇒ 0.30r = 0.30 ⇒ r = 1.0 Ω.
  3. Substitute r = 1.0 Ω to find E: E = 0.80 × (4.0 + 1.0) = 0.80 × 5.0 = 4.0 V.
(a) r = 1.0 Ω  ·  (b) E = 4.0 V
EXAMPLE 3

Intercepts & gradient of a V–I cell graph

When terminal potential difference V is plotted on the vertical axis against current I on the horizontal axis for a battery, the straight line has a vertical intercept of 6.0 V and a horizontal intercept of 4.0 A. Find: (a) the EMF E, (b) the internal resistance r, and (c) the terminal p.d. when the battery delivers 1.2 A.

  1. Vertical intercept (at I = 0): E = 6.0 V.
  2. Internal resistance from slope magnitude: r = E ÷ Imax = 6.0 ÷ 4.0 = 1.5 Ω.
  3. Terminal p.d. at I = 1.2 A: V = E − Ir = 6.0 − (1.2 × 1.5) = 6.0 − 1.8 = 4.2 V.
(a) E = 6.0 V  ·  (b) r = 1.5 Ω  ·  (c) V = 4.2 V

Suppose we didn’t even have an open-circuit voltmeter reading — only two load readings: R₁ = 10.8 Ω (I₁ = 1.0 A) and R₂ = 2.8 Ω (I₂ = 3.0 A). Step through the algebra below to uncover both r and E!

E = 1.0(10.8 + r)
V₁ = 10.8 Vfirst equation

Reading 2 · Starter Load · R₂ = 2.8 Ω, I₂ = 3.0 A

V₂ = 8.4 V (Find r & E!)second equation

Why does the cabin roof light of a car dim momentarily when the driver cranks the starter motor? Explain using V = E − Ir.

2 marks
Model answer unlocks after you try

The starter motor has very low resistance and draws a huge current I from the battery, causing a large internal voltage drop v = Ir across the battery's internal resistance (1). Consequently, the terminal potential difference V = E − Ir across the battery (and across the parallel cabin lamp) drops sharply, dimming the bulb (1).

Part B · Structured Written Questions (30 marks)

1. (a) Distinguish between the electromotive force (E) and the terminal potential difference (V) of a cell. (b) Explain why E is greater than V when the cell is discharging.

10 marks
Solution unlocks after you try

(a) EMF E is the total electrical energy supplied per coulomb by the cell around the entire circuit (measured when I = 0), whereas terminal p.d. V is the electrical energy converted per coulomb in the external load R only when current flows (6).
(b) When current I flows, work per coulomb v = Ir is expended overcoming the internal resistance r of the electrolyte, so V = E − Ir < E (4).

2. A cell delivers a current of 0.75 A through a 3.0 Ω resistor and 0.45 A through a 5.4 Ω resistor. Calculate: (a) the internal resistance r of the cell, and (b) the EMF E of the cell.

10 marks
Solution unlocks after you try

(a) Using I₁(R₁ + r) = I₂(R₂ + r): 0.75(3.0 + r) = 0.45(5.4 + r) (3).
2.25 + 0.75r = 2.43 + 0.45r ⇒ 0.30r = 0.18 ⇒ r = 0.60 Ω (4).
(b) E = 0.75 × (3.0 + 0.60) = 0.75 × 3.60 = 2.70 V (3).

3. A student plots terminal p.d. V (vertical) against current I (horizontal) for a lead-acid cell, obtaining a vertical intercept of 2.10 V and a horizontal intercept of 3.50 A. Determine: (a) the internal resistance r, and (b) the terminal voltage when I = 1.50 A.

10 marks
Solution unlocks after you try

(a) Vertical intercept E = 2.10 V (2). Magnitude of gradient r = 2.10 ÷ 3.50 = 0.60 Ω (4).
(b) At I = 1.50 A: V = E − Ir = 2.10 − (1.50 × 0.60) = 2.10 − 0.90 = 1.20 V (4).
