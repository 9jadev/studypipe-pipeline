Inside the Cell: Organelles, Plant vs Animal Cells

What is inside an ugu cell that a blood cell lacks, and why does that matter for water?

  • 01Describe the structure and state the function of each cell component.
  • 02Classify organelles by membrane: double (nucleus, mitochondrion, chloroplast), single (lysosome, vacuole with tonoplast, ER, Golgi body), none (ribosome, centrioles).
  • 03Compare plant and animal cells, and the cell wall and cell membrane, in matched tables.
  • 04Trace the route of a secreted protein: ribosome → rough ER → Golgi body → cell membrane.
  • 05Explain why ribosomes can never be seen with a light microscope, and why mitochondria, ER and Golgi bodies are not visible on a stained school slide.

  1. The cellulose wall, chloroplasts and the large sap vacuole.

  3. Contracting muscle needs a steady supply of energy, and mitochondria release that energy (as ATP) in aerobic respiration, so a muscle cell is packed with them.

3 · Cell wall against cell membrane

Magnification is not resolution. Magnification makes an image bigger. Resolution is the ability to show two close points as separate. A school light microscope cannot separate points closer than about 0.2 µm, so anything smaller (ribosomes, the inner folds of a mitochondrion) stays a blur at any magnification, and only an electron microscope shows it. Mitochondria (about 1 µm) are above the limit, but iodine and methylene blue do not stain them, so on a school slide they are not visible.

Worked examples, step by step

Attempt each problem on paper first, then check the working.

EXAMPLE 1

Size from an electron micrograph

An electron micrograph at ×15 000 shows a mitochondrion 30 mm long. Find its actual length in µm.

  1. Actual size = image size ÷ magnification: actual = 30 mm ÷ 15 000 = 0.002 mm.
  2. Convert mm to µm (× 1000): 0.002 mm × 1000 = 2 µm.
Actual length = 2 µm
EXAMPLE 2

Why ribosomes are invisible on a school slide

A ribosome is about 0.025 µm across. At ×400, how large is its image, and can the eye see it? (The unaided eye can just make out about 0.1 mm.)

  1. Image size = actual size × magnification: image = 0.025 µm × 400 = 10 µm.
  2. Convert to mm (÷ 1000): 10 µm = 0.010 mm.
  3. Compare with the eye's limit: 0.010 mm < 0.1 mm, so the image is ten times too small to see.
  4. Would more magnification help? No. A light microscope cannot resolve points closer than about 0.2 µm, and 0.2 ÷ 0.025 = 8, so a ribosome is about 8 times below that limit and stays an unresolved blur at any magnification.
Image = 10 µm. Not visible on a light microscope at any magnification (only an electron microscope shows ribosomes), so they are never labelled on a Paper 3 drawing.

Assignment: Inside the Cell

Part A and Part B from 03-questions.md § Lesson 02. Type your attempt first to unlock each model solution.

Part A · Home investigation: wall or no wall? Starch or no starch?

You will model the job of the cell wall and test where plant and animal tissues store food, using items found in most Nigerian homes.

Practical task

You need: two small transparent polythene bags (the “pure water” type), a small rigid plastic cup or empty tomato-paste tin with smooth edges, water, a teaspoon, tincture of iodine from the first-aid box (or povidone-iodine diluted 1 : 5 with water), a slice of raw yam, a pinch of garri, a small piece of cooked chicken or beef, and a white saucer.

Safety: An adult must cut the yam. Iodine stains skin and clothing and must not be swallowed. Wash your hands afterwards, and do not eat any food that has been tested.

  1. Wall model. Fill both bags with water to the same level and tie them. Leave bag 1 on the table. Push bag 2 snugly into the cup or tin. Press each bag gently with the same finger pressure and record what happens to its shape.
  2. Undo bag 2 inside the cup, add three more teaspoons of water, retie it, and press again. Note whether it swells outward or holds its shape.
  3. Food-store test. Put the yam slice, the garri and the meat on the white saucer, well apart. Add two drops of iodine to each and wait one minute. Record the colour.
  4. Explain your results using the words cellulose wall, cell membrane, starch and glycogen.

The bag in the cup stands for the plant cell (the cup is the cellulose wall and the bag is the membrane), while the bag alone stands for an animal cell such as a red blood cell. The rigid wall stops the plant cell bursting when water enters, which links to Mama Ngozi's crisp ugu. Plant cells store starch and animal cells store glycogen, not starch. The model has limits: a real cell wall is fully permeable (unlike a plastic cup) and the real membrane is selectively permeable.

2. Cell wall against cell membrane. State three differences between the cell wall and the cell membrane, in a matched table.

Solution unlocks after you try
FeatureCell wallCell membrane

3. The mitochondrion. (a) Describe the structure of a mitochondrion. (3 marks) (b) State its function. (1 mark)

4 marks
Solution unlocks after you try

(a) It is a rod-shaped or oval organelle bounded by a double membrane (1). The inner membrane is folded inward to form cristae, which give a large surface area (1). The space inside is filled with a fluid matrix containing enzymes (1).
(b) It is the site of aerobic respiration, releasing energy in the form of ATP (1).

“Produces energy” is penalised: energy is released, not produced. “Single membrane” loses the first mark. Do not accept “grana” or “stroma” for a mitochondrion.

4. Chloroplast and nucleus. (a) Describe the structure of a chloroplast and state its function. (2 marks) (b) State two functions of the nucleus. (2 marks)

4 marks
Solution unlocks after you try

(a) It is a double-membraned organelle containing stacks of membranes called grana, which hold chlorophyll, set in a fluid stroma (1). It is the site of photosynthesis (1).
(b) It controls all the activities of the cell (1). It contains the chromosomes (DNA) that carry hereditary information, and the DNA is copied there before cell division (1).

“Chloroplasts are found in all plant cells” is not credited: onion bulb and root cells lack them. “The brain of the cell” alone is too vague to score.

5. The route of a secreted protein. A cell lining Chinedu's stomach releases the enzyme pepsin (a protein). Trace the route of pepsin from where it is made to where it leaves the cell, naming the organelle at each stage and what happens there.

4 marks
Solution unlocks after you try

Pepsin is made (synthesised) from amino acids on ribosomes (1). The ribosomes are attached to the rough endoplasmic reticulum, which carries the protein through its channels (1). Vesicles take the protein to the Golgi body, where it is modified and packaged into secretory vesicles (1). The vesicles move to the cell membrane, fuse with it and release the enzyme out of the cell (secretion, or exocytosis) (1).

The order must be correct. Putting the Golgi body before the ER scores at most 2. The nucleus holds the instructions, but ribosomes make the protein. Do not credit “passes through the cell wall”, because animal cells have no wall.

6. Classifying organelles by membranes. Sort the following into organelles with two membranes, one membrane and no membrane: ribosome, lysosome, nucleus, Golgi body, mitochondrion, centriole.

3 marks
Solution unlocks after you try

Two membranes: nucleus, mitochondrion (1). One membrane: lysosome, Golgi body (1). No membrane: ribosome, centriole (1). Each group must be fully correct to score its mark.

The most common error is giving the ribosome one membrane, or giving the lysosome two. Adding “cell wall” to any membrane group is penalised: the wall is not a membrane.

7. Calculation: size and visibility. Dr Amaka shows an electron micrograph of a liver cell taken at ×25 000. (a) The nucleus has an image diameter of 250 mm. Calculate its actual diameter in micrometres. (2 marks) (b) A Golgi body on the same micrograph has an image length of 30 mm. Calculate its actual length in micrometres. (1 mark) (c) The nucleus is viewed with a school light microscope at a total magnification of ×100. Calculate the size of its image in millimetres and state whether it can be seen, given that the eye can just make out about 0.1 mm. (2 marks)

5 marks
Solution unlocks after you try

(a) Actual size = image size ÷ magnification: 250 mm ÷ 25 000 = 0.01 mm (1); 0.01 mm × 1000 = 10 µm (1).
(b) 30 mm ÷ 25 000 = 0.0012 mm = 1.2 µm (1).
(c) Image = actual × magnification: 10 µm × 100 = 1000 µm = 1 mm (1). 1 mm is greater than 0.1 mm, so the nucleus is visible, which is why it can be labelled on a Paper 3 drawing (1).

Answers without units, or with the wrong unit (for example “10 mm”), lose the answer mark. Multiplying instead of dividing in (a) scores zero. Magnification has no unit.

8. Predicting organelles from function. For each cell, name the organelle you would expect to find in large numbers and give a reason. (a) A palisade cell in an ugu leaf exposed to bright sunlight at Mile 12 Market. (1 mark) (b) A white blood cell at LUTH that engulfs and digests bacteria. (1 mark) (c) A sperm cell that swims using its tail (flagellum). (1 mark)

3 marks
Solution unlocks after you try

(a) Chloroplasts, to absorb light for photosynthesis (1). (b) Lysosomes, whose digestive enzymes break down the engulfed bacteria (1). (c) Mitochondria (in the middle piece), to release energy (ATP) for the beating flagellum (1).

The organelle and the reason must both be correct for the mark. In (c), the flagellum arises from a basal body, not from the mitochondria; the mitochondria only supply the energy. “Vacuole” for (b) is not accepted.
