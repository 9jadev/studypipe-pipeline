Practical analysis questions

Answer these five questions, then compare with the model answers.

Practical
  1. Why do we calculate % change in mass instead of the change in mass?
  2. Why were the strips blotted before each weighing?
  3. Which way did water move at 0.0 mol dm⁻³, and why?
  4. What is the isotonic point, and what happens to the strip there?
  5. Why did no strip burst?
Model answers unlocks after you try
  1. The strips start with slightly different masses, so a % change is a fair comparison between tubes.
  2. Blotting removes the surface solution, so the balance weighs only the strip. Without it the final mass would be too high.
  3. Water moved into the strip by osmosis, because pure water is more dilute than the cell sap. The strip gained mass.
  4. The isotonic point is the concentration where the % change is zero, about 0.34 mol dm⁻³. Water enters and leaves at the same rate, so there is no net change in mass.
  5. Each plant cell has a strong cellulose cell wall. It pushes back as the cell becomes turgid, so the cell cannot burst.

3Make sense · 15 min

Three ways in and out, and three kinds of solution

Now connect what you measured to the words examiners use. Three movements, one membrane, and one simple formula.

Diffusion: the overall spreading of particles from where they are crowded to where they are fewer, until they are evenly spread.

Osmosis: the passage of water molecules across a selectively permeable membrane, from the side where water is more plentiful (dilute solution) to the side where it is less plentiful (concentrated solution).

Active transport: the pumping of molecules or ions by a cell from where they are scarce to where they are already plentiful, which costs energy (ATP) from respiration.

4Apply · Worked examples · 8 min

Worked examples, step by step

Try each one on paper first, then check every step of the working.

EXAMPLE 1

% change in pure water

The strip in 0.0 mol dm⁻³ went from 2.50 g to 2.85 g. Find the % change in mass.

  1. Change in mass: Δm = m₁ − m₀ = 2.85 − 2.50 = +0.35 g.
  2. Put it over the initial mass: %Δm = Δm ÷ m₀ × 100 = 0.35 ÷ 2.50 × 100.
  3. Work it out: 0.35 ÷ 2.50 × 100 = 0.14 × 100 = +14.0%.
%Δm = +14.0%

Water entered the strip by osmosis.

EXAMPLE 2

% change in a strong solution

The strip in 0.8 mol dm⁻³ went from 2.46 g to 2.16 g. Find the % change in mass.

  1. Change in mass: Δm = m₁ − m₀ = 2.16 − 2.46 = −0.30 g.
  2. Put it over the initial mass: %Δm = Δm ÷ m₀ × 100 = −0.30 ÷ 2.46 × 100.
  3. Work it out: −0.30 ÷ 2.46 × 100 = −0.122 × 100 = −12.2%.
%Δm = −12.2%

Water left by osmosis, and the cells plasmolysed.

EXAMPLE 3

Finding the isotonic point

The % change is +4.8 at 0.2 mol dm⁻³ and −2.0 at 0.4 mol dm⁻³. Estimate the isotonic concentration.

  1. Where is zero? The line crosses 0% between the two readings, because one is a gain and the other is a loss.
  2. Fraction f of the way from 0.2 to 0.4: f = 4.8 ÷ (4.8 + 2.0) = 4.8 ÷ 6.8 = 0.71.
  3. Convert to a concentration c: c = 0.2 + 0.71 × 0.2 = 0.2 + 0.142 = 0.34 mol dm⁻³.
c ≈ 0.34 mol dm⁻³

Water enters and leaves equally here. A straight line between two points gives an estimate; reading the smooth curve gives about 0.33 to 0.34.

The big idea of Lesson 4: Water moves by osmosis from dilute to concentrated. Walls protect plant cells from bursting, but nothing protects a red blood cell. That is why salty water wilts ugu, and why a drip must be isotonic 0.9% saline.
