Diffusion, Osmosis, Tonicity & Active Transport

1 · Comparing Diffusion, Osmosis & Active Transport

FeatureSimple DiffusionOsmosisActive Transport
DefinitionNet movement of solute, gas, or liquid particles from a region of higher concentration to lower concentration down a concentration gradient due to random kinetic energy.Net movement of water (solvent) molecules from a dilute solution (higher water potential, $\Psi_w$) to a more concentrated solution (lower $\Psi_w$) across a selectively permeable membrane.Movement of ions or molecules from a region of lower concentration to higher concentration (against a concentration gradient) across a living membrane using ATP.
Selectively Permeable Membrane Required?No (occurs in air, liquids, or across permeable/selective membranes).Yes — mandatory (plasma membrane, tonoplast, Visking tubing, or living yam/pawpaw tissue).Yes — mandatory (living plasma membrane or tonoplast containing specific carrier protein pumps).
Metabolic Energy (ATP) Required?No (passive physical process driven by kinetic energy).No (passive water movement down water-potential gradient).Yes — requires ATP from aerobic respiration in mitochondria (halted by cyanide, $\text{O}_2$ deprivation, or boiling).
Factors Increasing RateHigher temperature, steeper concentration gradient, larger surface area ($\frac{SA}{V}$), shorter diffusion distance, smaller molecular mass (Graham’s law: $r \propto 1/\sqrt{M_r}$).Greater water-potential difference ($\Delta \Psi_w$), higher temperature, larger membrane surface area, more aquaporin channels.Abundant oxygen supply, high glucose/ATP availability, optimal temperature for respiratory enzymes, high density of mitochondria and carrier proteins.
Biological Examples$\text{O}_2 / \text{CO}_2$ exchange in lungs, gills, and leaf stomata; diffusion of digested food in villi; translocation odors/pollination scents.Water absorption by root hairs; cell-to-cell water movement across the root cortex; water reabsorption in kidney nephron tubules; opening/closing of guard cells.Uptake of $\text{NO}_3^-$, $\text{K}^+$, and $\text{PO}_4^{3-}$ by root hair cells from dilute soil water; $\text{Na}^+/\text{K}^+$ pump in nerve axons; complete glucose absorption in ileum villi; selective reabsorption in kidney proximal tubules.

2 · Bulk Transport: Endocytosis & Exocytosis

  • Endocytosis: Active intake of large particles or droplets by infolding of the plasma membrane to pinch off an intracellular vesicle (requires ATP):
    • Phagocytosis (“Cell Eating”): Extension of pseudopodia to engulf solid particles such as bacteria or cellular debris into a food vacuole / phagosome (e.g., Amoeba feeding; mammalian neutrophils and macrophages engulfing pathogens).
    • Pinocytosis (“Cell Drinking”): Infolding of the plasma membrane to take in tiny droplets of extracellular fluid and dissolved solutes.
  • Exocytosis: Fusion of intracellular secretory or waste vesicles with the plasma membrane to expel contents outside the cell (e.g., secretion of digestive enzymes, hormones like insulin, neurotransmitters, and expulsion of undigested residue in Amoeba/Paramecium).

3 · Plant & Animal Cell Responses to Hypotonic, Isotonic & Hypertonic Solutions

External SolutionEffect on Plant Cell (Has Rigid Cellulose Wall)Effect on Animal Cell / RBC (No Cell Wall)
1. Hypotonic Solution
(Dilute / Distilled Water; higher $\Psi_w$ than cytoplasm)		 Endosmosis → Turgidity: Water enters the central sap vacuole by osmosis; the vacuole swells and pushes the cytoplasm against the cellulose wall (turgor pressure, TP). The inelastic wall pushes back with equal and opposite wall pressure (WP), preventing bursting. At full turgor, $\text{TP} = \text{OP}$ and net water entry stops ($\text{SP} = 0$).
Importance: Mechanical support in herbaceous stems, holding leaves flat to sunlight, opening stomata (turgid guard cells), root growth penetration.		 Endosmosis → Haemolysis (Lysis): Water enters rapidly by osmosis. Because animal cells lack a rigid cellulose cell wall, the plasma membrane stretches beyond its elastic limit and bursts, releasing haemoglobin into the solution (forming a clear red solution in red blood cells = haemolysis).
Adaptation in freshwater protists: Amoeba and Paramecium use contractile vacuoles to pump out excess water continuously.
2. Isotonic Solution
(Equal $\Psi_w$; e.g., $0.30\text{ M}$ sucrose for yam; $0.9\%\text{ NaCl}$ for human blood)		 Dynamic Equilibrium → Incipient Flaccidity: Water molecules enter and leave at equal rates ($\Delta m = 0\%$). Turgor pressure drops to zero ($\text{TP} = 0$), so the cell is soft (flaccid) but not plasmolysed. Dynamic Equilibrium → Normal Shape: Water enters and leaves at equal rates. Red blood cells retain their normal biconcave disc shape. (Basis of hospital 0.9% normal saline IV drips and Ringer’s solution).
3. Hypertonic Solution
(Concentrated Brine/Syrup; lower $\Psi_w$ than cytoplasm)		 Exosmosis → Plasmolysis: Water leaves the sap vacuole by osmosis; the vacuole shrinks and the protoplasm (plasma membrane + cytoplasm) pulls away from the cell wall, leaving the space between wall and membrane filled with the external hypertonic solution. Causes wilting. (Reversible by deplasmolysis if transferred promptly to pure water).
Applications: Salting fish/meat and sugaring jam/fruits to plasmolyse spoilage bacteria; salting weeds.		 Exosmosis → Crenation: Water leaves the cytoplasm by osmosis; the red blood cell shrinks, shrivels, and develops a notched, spiked surface (crenation). Note: Animal cells undergo crenation, never plasmolysis (because they have no cell wall to pull away from!).

EXAMPLE 1

Yam Cylinder Percentage Mass Gain in Water & Loss in Hypertonic Sucrose

Two uniform yam cylinders X and Y each have an initial mass of 2.50 g. Cylinder X is placed in distilled water (0.00 M) and its final mass after 45 minutes is 2.95 g. Cylinder Y is placed in 0.70 M sucrose solution and its final mass is 2.05 g. Calculate the percentage change in mass (%Δm) for: (a) Cylinder X, and (b) Cylinder Y.

  1. Part (a) Step 1 — Calculate mass change Δm for Cylinder X in distilled water: Δm_X = 2.95 − 2.50 = 0.45 g.
  2. Part (a) Step 2 — Calculate percentage mass change %Δm for Cylinder X: %Δm_X = (0.45 ÷ 2.50) × 100 = 0.18 × 100 = +18.0%.
  3. Part (b) Step 1 — Calculate mass change Δm for Cylinder Y in 0.70 M sucrose: Δm_Y = 2.05 − 2.50 = −0.45 g.
  4. Part (b) Step 2 — Calculate percentage mass change %Δm for Cylinder Y: %Δm_Y = (−0.45 ÷ 2.50) × 100 = −0.18 × 100 = −18.0%.
(a) Cylinder X = +18.0% (Endosmosis / Turgid)  ·  (b) Cylinder Y = −18.0% (Exosmosis / Plasmolysed)
EXAMPLE 2

Plant Cell Suction Pressure (SP = OP − TP) & Water Potential (Ψ_w = Ψ_s + Ψ_p)

A partially turgid leaf mesophyll cell has an osmotic pressure (OP) of 14 atm and a turgor pressure (TP) of 9 atm. (a) Calculate its suction pressure (SP) when placed in pure water. (b) What does its suction pressure become when the cell reaches full turgidity (TP = 14 atm)? (c) Express the partially turgid cell's water potential Ψ_w in kPa given solute potential Ψ_s = −1400 kPa and pressure potential Ψ_p = +900 kPa.

  1. Part (a) — Suction pressure SP of partially turgid cell: SP = OP − TP = 14 − 9 = 5 atm.
  2. Part (b) — Suction pressure SP at full turgidity (TP = OP = 14 atm): SP_full = 14 − 14 = 0 atm.
  3. Part (c) — Water potential Ψ_w in kilopascals: Ψ_w = Ψ_s + Ψ_p = −1400 + 900 = −500 kPa.
(a) SP = 5 atm  ·  (b) SP at full turgor = 0 atm  ·  (c) Ψw = −500 kPa
EXAMPLE 3

Graham’s Law of Relative Diffusion Rates for Two Gases

Compare the rate of diffusion of oxygen gas (O₂, relative molecular mass M₁ = 32) with a heavier organic vapour of relative molecular mass M₂ = 128 at the same temperature and pressure.

  1. Step 1 — Divide the heavier molecular mass M₂ by the lighter molecular mass M₁: Ratio_M = M_2 ÷ M_1 = 128 ÷ 32 = 4.
  2. Step 2 — Take the square root to find the diffusion rate ratio r₁ ÷ r₂: r_1 ÷ r_2 = √4 = 2.
Oxygen (M₁ = 32) diffuses 2 times faster than the vapour (M₂ = 128)

Sample answer unlocks after you try

Part B · Structured Written Questions (30 marks)

1. (a) Define (i) Osmosis (ii) Plasmolysis (iii) Haemolysis (iv) Active transport [4 marks]. (b) Tabulate three differences between osmosis and active transport [3 marks]. (c) Explain why a freshwater weed (Elodea) dies when transplanted into a marine salt-water lagoon [3 marks].

10 marks
Solution unlocks after you try

(a) (i) Osmosis: Net movement of water molecules from a dilute solution (higher water potential) to a concentrated solution (lower water potential) across a selectively permeable membrane (1). (ii) Plasmolysis: Shrinkage of the vacuole and pulling away of the protoplasm from the cell wall in a plant cell placed in a hypertonic solution (1). (iii) Haemolysis: Swelling and bursting of red blood cells in a hypotonic solution, releasing haemoglobin (1). (iv) Active transport: Movement of ions/molecules against a concentration gradient across a living membrane using energy from ATP (1).
(b) 1. Osmosis moves water down a water-potential gradient; active transport moves solutes/ions against a concentration gradient (1). 2. Osmosis is passive (0 ATP); active transport requires metabolic energy (ATP) (1). 3. Osmosis is unaffected by cyanide/anoxia; active transport is stopped by cyanide or lack of oxygen (1).
(c) Seawater in the lagoon is strongly hypertonic to the cell sap of freshwater Elodea (1). Water leaves the Elodea cells rapidly by exosmosis (1), causing irreversible plasmolysis, dehydration of the protoplasm, and death of the plant (1).

2. Describe a laboratory experiment using a fresh yam tuber and a boiled yam tuber to demonstrate osmosis in living tissue. Your account must include: (a) Aim and Apparatus (b) Method (Control and Experimental setups) (c) Observation after 2 hours (d) Conclusion [10 marks].

10 marks
Solution unlocks after you try

(a) Aim: To demonstrate osmosis using living yam tissue. Apparatus: Peeler, knife, one fresh raw yam tuber, one boiled yam tuber, concentrated sucrose solution, distilled water, two petri dishes/beakers, pins (2).
(b) Method: Peel the bottom of both the raw yam (Setup A) and boiled yam (Setup B) so they stand flat, and scoop out a deep central cavity in each. Pour concentrated sucrose solution half-way into each cavity and mark the initial liquid level with a pin. Stand both yam cups in petri dishes containing distilled water and leave for 2 hours (4).
(c) Observation: In Setup A (raw living yam), the sugar level inside the cavity rises significantly above the pin while the water level in the petri dish falls. In Setup B (boiled yam control), the liquid level inside the cavity does not rise (2).
(d) Conclusion: Water moved by osmosis from the hypotonic distilled water across the living selectively permeable cells of the raw yam into the hypertonic sucrose solution; boiling killed the cells in Setup B and destroyed selective permeability (2).

3. (a) A potato cylinder of initial mass 4.00 g is immersed in 0.60 M salt solution for 1 hour and its final mass is 3.40 g. Calculate the change in mass and percentage change in mass [4 marks]. (b) State two reasons why salt is used to preserve fresh fish in Nigeria [3 marks]. (c) Explain why oxygen is required by plant roots for the absorption of nitrate ions from soil water [3 marks].

10 marks
Solution unlocks after you try

(a) Change in mass: Δm = 3.40 − 4.00 = −0.60 g (2).
Percentage change in mass: %Δm = (−0.60 ÷ 4.00) × 100 = −0.15 × 100 = −15.0% (2).
(b) Salt forms a strongly hypertonic solution on the fish surface (1), drawing water out of the fish tissues and out of spoilage bacteria/fungi by exosmosis (1), which plasmolyses the microbes and prevents decay (1).
(c) Nitrate ions are absorbed against a concentration gradient by active transport (1). Oxygen is required by root hair mitochondria for aerobic respiration (1) to generate the ATP needed by membrane carrier pumps (1).
