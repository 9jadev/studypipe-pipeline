3D Compound Microscope Bench: Verifying Linear Magnification (M = I ÷ A)

Step up to Dr. Kunle’s compound light microscope fitted with a ×10 eyepiece lens. Rotate the nosepiece across five objective lenses (×4, ×10, ×20, ×40, ×60), measure the apparent image length I (mm) of an onion epidermal cell, and verify that A = I ÷ M remains constant at 0.060 mm (60 µm).

WAEC Paper 3 Microscope Handling Rules: Always begin focusing with the lowest-power objective lens (×4) clicked into position. Once switched to the high-power objective (×40), turn only the fine adjustment knob — never the coarse adjustment knob — so the objective lens does not smash through the coverslip and glass slide.

STEP 01

Clip slide on stage

Centre the stained wet-mount slide over the stage aperture and secure it with the metallic stage clips.

STEP 02

Start with ×4 low-power lens

Rotate the nosepiece to the ×4 scanning objective and adjust the iris diaphragm and mirror/illuminator for even brightness.

STEP 03

Focus with coarse, then fine knob

Bring the specimen into rough focus using the coarse knob at low power, then switch to ×10 or ×40 and sharpen with the fine knob only.

STEP 04

Compute M = I ÷ A in same units

Measure image length I in mm, convert to µm (×1000), and divide by actual size A (or divide I by M to find A).

Model answers unlocks after you try
  1. The working distance between the ×40 objective lens and the coverslip is less than 1 mm; turning the coarse knob moves the stage rapidly and can crack the slide and scratch the objective lens.
  2. Convert millimetres to micrometres: A = 0.060 mm × 1000 µm/mm = 60 µm.
  3. At 4× higher magnification, light from a 16-times smaller area of the slide is spread across the eyepiece, reducing brightness and narrowing the field diameter by 4 times.

Discovery of the Cell, Modern Cell Theory & Prokaryotes vs Eukaryotes

Let’s connect our 3D story and microscope bench observations to the complete historical timeline, equations, and comparison tables tested in WAEC, NECO, and JAMB Biology.

1 · Chronological Pioneers of Cell Discovery

YearScientist(s)Nationality / FieldLandmark Contribution to Cytology
1665Robert HookeEnglish physicistExamined thin slices of dead bottle cork (Quercus suber) with a compound microscope; saw honeycomb chambers and coined the term “cell” (cellulae). Actually saw dead suberised plant cell walls lacking protoplasm.
1674–1683Antonie van LeeuwenhoekDutch microscopistBuilt high-clarity single-lens microscopes (~270×); first to observe free-living cells (“animalcules” — protozoa, bacteria, red blood cells, and spermatozoa) in pond water and body fluids.
1831Robert BrownScottish botanistDiscovered and named the dense spherical nucleus inside orchid epidermal cells.
1835Félix DujardinFrench zoologistObserved the living, semi-fluid jelly inside single-celled animals (protozoa) and named it “sarcode”.
1838Matthias Jakob SchleidenGerman botanistConcluded that all plant tissues are composed of cells and that the cell is the basic unit of plant structure.
1839Theodor SchwannGerman zoologistConcluded that all animal tissues are composed of cells and formulated the classical Cell Theory jointly with Schleiden.
1839 / 1846J. E. Purkinje & Hugo von MohlCzech / GermanCoined and applied the term “protoplasm” for the living substance (cytoplasm + nucleus) inside the cell.
1855Rudolf VirchowGerman physicianAdded the third pillar: Omnis cellula e cellula — every new cell arises only by division of a pre-existing living cell (refuting spontaneous generation).

2 · The Modern Cell Theory & Its Notable Exceptions

The four tenets of Modern Cell Theory state that:

  1. All known living organisms are composed of one or more cells and their products (the structural unit of life).
  2. The cell is the smallest independent unit that carries out all metabolic processes of life (the functional unit of life).
  3. All new cells arise solely by the division (binary fission, mitosis, or meiosis) of pre-existing living cells.
  4. Cells contain hereditary information (DNA) which is replicated and passed from parent cell to daughter cell during cell division.

Exceptions & Borderline Cases Tested by WAEC & JAMB:

  • Viruses (Borderline between Living and Non-Living): Viruses are acellular (no plasma membrane, no cytoplasm, no ribosomes, no ATP synthesis). Outside a host cell they are inert chemical crystals; inside a specific host cell they hijack host enzymes to replicate their DNA or RNA (never both).
  • Coenocytic / Multinucleate Structures: Non-septate fungal hyphae (e.g., Rhizopus), coenocytic algae (Vaucheria), and vertebrate striated skeletal muscle fibres contain many nuclei sharing one continuous cytoplasm without cross-walls.
  • Anucleate Mature Cells: Mature mammalian red blood cells (erythrocytes) and plant phloem sieve tube elements lose their nuclei at maturity to maximise space for haemoglobin transport and translocation respectively.

3 · Microscope Magnification & Unit Conversion Equations

In microscopy and WAEC Paper 3 biological drawings, every calculation follows three exact relationships:

M_total = M_eyepiece × M_objective = 10 × 40 = 400×
M = I ÷ A   ⇒   A = I ÷ M
1 mm = 1000 µm   ⇒   48 mm = 48 × 1000 = 48000 µm

Worked examples, step by step

Every calculation is broken down one atomic mathematical step at a time with a plain-English explainer note underneath.

EXAMPLE 1

Total Microscope Magnification & Drawing Magnification in Micrometres

A student examines an Allium cepa (onion) epidermal cell under a compound microscope fitted with a ×10 eyepiece and a ×40 objective lens. She makes a biological drawing of one cell measuring 48 mm in length. Given that the actual length of the onion cell is 60 µm, calculate: (a) the total magnification of the microscope, and (b) the linear magnification of her biological drawing.

  1. Part (a) — Total microscope magnification: M_total = M_eyepiece × M_objective = 10 × 40 = 400×.
  2. Part (b) Step 1 — Convert drawing length I from mm to µm: I = 48 × 1000 = 48000 µm.
  3. Part (b) Step 2 — Calculate linear magnification M of the drawing: M = I ÷ A = 48000 ÷ 60 = 800×.
(a) Total Microscope Magnification = ×400  ·  (b) Drawing Magnification = ×800
EXAMPLE 2

Finding Actual Bacterial Size A (µm) from Photomicrograph Width

In an electron photomicrograph taken at a linear magnification of ×15000, the image of a rod-shaped bacterium (Escherichia coli) measures 30 mm in length. Calculate the actual length A of the bacterium in micrometres (µm).

  1. Step 1 — Convert image length I from millimetres to micrometres: I = 30 × 1000 = 30000 µm.
  2. Step 2 — Divide image length I by magnification M to find actual size A: A = I ÷ M = 30000 ÷ 15000 = 2.0 µm.
Actual length of bacterium A = 2.0 µm
EXAMPLE 3

Comparing Eukaryotic vs Prokaryotic Cell Length Ratio

An onion epidermal cell has an actual length of 0.060 mm while a bacterium has an actual length of 2.0 µm. How many of these bacteria laid end-to-end would equal the length of one onion epidermal cell?

  1. Step 1 — Convert onion cell length from mm to µm: A_onion = 0.060 × 1000 = 60 µm.
  2. Step 2 — Divide eukaryotic cell length by prokaryotic cell length: N = A_onion ÷ A_bacterium = 60 ÷ 2.0 = 30.
Number of bacteria = 30 bacteria laid end-to-end

The big idea of Lesson 1: Robert Hooke’s 1665 cork chambers were dead cell walls, whereas living cells contain protoplasm and arise only from pre-existing cells (Omnis cellula e cellula). Across the living world, prokaryotes (70S, naked circular DNA, no membrane-bound organelles) and eukaryotes (80S, true nucleus, mitochondria) obey the universal magnification law M = I ÷ A!

1. (a) State the specific contribution of each of the following scientists to cell biology: (i) Robert Hooke (ii) Antonie van Leeuwenhoek (iii) Matthias Schleiden & Theodor Schwann (iv) Rudolf Virchow [4 marks]. (b) State four tenets of the Modern Cell Theory [4 marks]. (c) Give two reasons why a virus is regarded as being on the borderline between living and non-living things [2 marks].

10 marks
Solution unlocks after you try

(a) (i) Hooke (1665): Observed dead cork cell walls and coined the term “cell” (1). (ii) Leeuwenhoek (1674): First observed living single-celled organisms (protozoa/bacteria) (1). (iii) Schleiden & Schwann (1838–1839): Established that all plants and animals are composed of cells (1). (iv) Virchow (1855): Proved that all cells arise from pre-existing living cells (Omnis cellula e cellula) (1).
(b) 1. All living organisms are composed of cells and their products (1). 2. The cell is the structural and functional unit of life (1). 3. All new cells arise by division of pre-existing cells (1). 4. Cells contain hereditary DNA passed to daughter cells (1).
(c) Non-living trait: A virus is acellular (lacks protoplasm/organelles) and can be crystallised like a salt outside a host (1). Living trait: Inside a living host cell it replicates its nucleic acid (DNA or RNA) and mutates (1).

2. (a) Construct a table contrasting a prokaryotic bacterial cell with a eukaryotic plant cell under six distinct headings [6 marks]. (b) State two cell types or organisms that are exceptions to the rule that every cell has a single nucleus [4 marks].

10 marks
Solution unlocks after you try

(a) 1. Nuclear envelope: Absent in bacterium (nucleoid) vs Present in plant cell (1). 2. DNA form: Single circular naked DNA vs Multiple linear DNA molecules on histones (1). 3. Ribosomes: 70S vs 80S in cytoplasm (1). 4. Cell wall: Peptidoglycan (murein) vs Cellulose (1). 5. Membrane-bound organelles: Absent vs Present (mitochondria, chloroplasts, ER, Golgi) (1). 6. Cell division: Binary fission vs Mitosis/meiosis with spindle (1).
(b) 1. Mature mammalian red blood cell (or phloem sieve tube element): anucleate (0 nuclei) at maturity (2). 2. Rhizopus hypha (or skeletal muscle fibre): coenocytic / multinucleate (many nuclei in shared cytoplasm) (2).

3. (a) A microscope has a ×15 eyepiece and a ×40 objective lens. Calculate its total magnification [2 marks]. (b) A student draws a cheek cell measuring 54 mm across. If the actual diameter of the cheek cell is 45 µm, calculate the linear magnification of the drawing [4 marks]. (c) Under ×400 magnification, an image of a mitochondrion is 0.6 mm long. Calculate its actual length in micrometres [4 marks].

10 marks
Solution unlocks after you try

(a) Total magnification: M_total = M_e × M_o = 15 × 40 = 600× (2).
(b) Convert drawing size to µm: I = 54 × 1000 = 54000 µm (2). Linear magnification: M = I ÷ A = 54000 ÷ 45 = 1200× (2).
