The key insight: the cell width stays the same at every power. Magnifying changes the image, not the cell. Higher power shows fewer cells because the field of view gets smaller.

Practical analysis questions

Try all five, then compare with the model answers.

  1. Why does the cell width stay the same even though the image grows?
  2. Why did we start on the lowest power?
  3. Why is only the fine knob used at ×40?
  4. What would you see on a cork slide that differs from the onion peel?
  5. Calculate the total magnification for a ×15 eyepiece and a ×40 objective.
Model answers unlocks after you try
  1. Magnification enlarges the image, not the cell. The real width is found from the field diameter ÷ number of cells, and neither the cell nor the maths changes between powers.
  2. Low power gives the widest field of view, so the specimen is easy to find and centre before moving to a higher power.
  3. The ×40 objective is very close to the slide. The tiny movements of the fine knob focus it safely; the coarse knob could drive the lens into the slide and crack it.
  4. Cork shows only the empty walls of dead cells, with no contents. The onion peel shows living cells with contents and a stained nucleus.
  5. Total magnification = 15 × 40 = ×600.

3Make sense · 15 min

Cells, the cell theory and how big is a cell?

Now connect what you saw at the microscope to the ideas that examiners ask about.

1 · A cell and an organelle

A cell is the basic unit of life: the smallest part of an organism that is both its building block (structure) and its working unit (function).

An organelle is a tiny part inside the cytoplasm that is specialised to do one particular job for the cell.

The cell theory: (1) every organism consists of one cell or of many cells; (2) organisms are built from cells, and cells carry out their life processes; (3) new cells form only when existing cells divide (Virchow, 1855; useful though not required).

2 · Equations to learn

Total magnification = Meyepiece × Mobjective
Actual size = image size ÷ magnification
Cell width = field diameter ÷ number of cells side by side
1 mm = 1000 µm

4Apply · Worked examples

Worked examples, step by step

Try each problem on paper first, then check the working.

EXAMPLE 1

Cell width from the field of view

With a ×10 eyepiece and a ×40 objective, the field of view is 0.40 mm wide and 8 onion cells lie side by side across it. Find the total magnification and the width of one cell in µm.

  1. Total magnification: 10 × 40 = ×400 (multiply, never add).
  2. Cell width: 0.40 mm ÷ 8 = 0.050 mm.
  3. Convert to µm: 0.050 mm × 1000 = 50 µm.
×400 · 50 µm
EXAMPLE 2

Actual size from an image

A photomicrograph taken with a ×10 eyepiece and a ×40 objective shows a cheek cell 24 mm long. Find its actual length.

  1. Total magnification: 10 × 40 = ×400.
  2. Actual size: 24 mm ÷ 400 = 0.060 mm.
  3. Convert to µm: 0.060 mm × 1000 = 60 µm.
60 µm
EXAMPLE 3

Magnification of a drawing

An onion cell 0.25 mm long is drawn 50 mm long. (a) Find the magnification of the drawing. (b) How long would the drawing be at ×150?

  1. (a) Magnification: 50 mm ÷ 0.25 mm = ×200 (no unit, because the mm cancel).
  2. (b) Drawing length at ×150: 150 × 0.25 mm = 37.5 mm.
(a) ×200 · (b) 37.5 mm
