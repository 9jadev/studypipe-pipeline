Slide bench: onion in iodine, cheek in methylene blue

Prepare slide A (onion epidermis) and slide B (cheek cells) on Dr Amaka’s bench. Choose your technique, mount the slide, and see on the eyepiece disc exactly what the examiner would see.

Safety (L3): Handle slides and cover slips by their edges. Use only your own cheek cells, use the spatula once, and put it in the disinfectant afterwards. Do not taste or touch the stains.

METHOD 1

Peel, then lay flat in iodine

Peel the thin inner epidermis of a fleshy scale leaf and lay it flat, unfolded, in a drop of iodine on the slide.

METHOD 2

Lower the cover slip at about 45°

Touch one edge to the liquid and lower the cover slip slowly with a mounted needle, so no air bubbles are trapped.

METHOD 3

Cheek cells: scrape, smear, stain

Scrape the cheek gently, smear thinly, add methylene blue and a cover slip. Put the used spatula in disinfectant.

METHOD 4

Low power first, then high power

View each slide on low power (×100) and find the cells, then switch to high power (×400) and focus with the fine knob.

Analysis questions

Answer these five WAEC-style questions in your own words, then unlock the model answers.

  1. Why was a thin inner peel used rather than a slice of onion?
  2. What would you see if the onion peel were mounted in strong salt solution instead of iodine?
  3. Why does methylene blue suit cheek cells better than plain water?
  4. Why is the cell membrane not labelled on an onion cell?
  5. How would you tell an air bubble from a cell on your slide?
Model answers unlocks after you try
  1. The inner peel is only one cell thick, so light passes through it and each cell can be seen on its own. A slice is many cells thick, so the cells overlap, look dark, and no single layer can be focused.
  2. Plasmolysis: water leaves the vacuole by osmosis, so the cell membrane and cytoplasm pull away from the cell wall.
  3. Cheek cells are thin and almost colourless, so they are very hard to see in water. Methylene blue stains the nucleus dark blue and the cytoplasm pale blue, which gives contrast.
  4. It is pressed tightly against the cell wall by the full vacuole, so it cannot be seen as a separate line. It is seen only if the cell is plasmolysed.
  5. An air bubble is perfectly round or oval, with a thick dark rim and a bright empty centre and no nucleus. A cell has a thin boundary, stained cytoplasm and a nucleus, and is box-like (onion) or irregular (cheek).

3Make sense · 15 min

The nine rules, what you can see, and magnification

Now connect the bench and the audit to what WAEC marks in Paper 3.

1 · The nine drawing rules

Nine rules the examiner marks (10 marks in all):

  1. Title under the drawing, generic name with a capital letter.
  2. Magnification written below it, with no unit (Mag. ×200).
  3. Size exactly as instructed, for example 6–8 cm.
  4. Sharp, continuous lines from an HB pencil. No shading, no colour.
  5. Guidelines ruled, horizontal, touching the part, never crossing, no arrowheads (2 marks).
  6. Labels in the singular, lined up in a column.
  7. Correct spelling and capital letters for generic names.
  8. Double lines where two walls meet or a surface is cut; one clean line for a single outline.
  9. Draw what you see, and label only what you can see.

2 · Observable differences

On a school slide, the observable differences are the ones you can see with a light microscope:

FeatureOnion (A) · iodineCheek (B) · methylene blue
ShapeRegular, box-likeIrregular, rounded
Cell wallPresentAbsent
NucleusAt the sideCentral
VacuoleLarge, visibleNot visible
StainYellow-brownBlue

Textbook-only differences (mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi body, ribosomes) are true, but they are not visible on a school slide, so they score nothing in an “observable differences” question.

Why no chloroplasts in an onion bulb?

Chloroplasts develop in cells that receive light. The fleshy scale leaves of an onion bulb grow underground in the dark, so their cells never make chloroplasts, just like root cells. (Chloroplasts are large enough to see in Spirogyra or a leaf cell.)

Why are mitochondria not labelled?

Cells do have mitochondria, but at ×400 they are only a few micrometres long and iodine and methylene blue do not stain them. Ribosomes are smaller than 0.2 µm, the limit of a light microscope. If you cannot see it on your slide, do not draw or label it.

3 · Magnification of a drawing

M = drawing length ÷ actual length   (same unit for both, so M has no unit)
actual length = drawing length ÷ M   and write “Mag. ×200” under the drawing

4Apply · Worked examples · 10 min

Worked examples, step by step

Try each calculation on paper first, then compare with the working. Always put both lengths in the same unit before dividing.

EXAMPLE 1

Magnification of a drawing

A Spirogyra filament segment 0.40 mm long is drawn 8.0 cm long. Calculate the magnification of the drawing.

  1. Convert to the same unit: 8.0 cm = 80 mm.
  2. Write the formula and substitute: M = drawing length ÷ actual length = 80 ÷ 0.40.
  3. Divide: M = 200, written as ×200 with no unit.
Answer: Mag. ×200
EXAMPLE 2

Does the drawing meet the size rule?

A specimen 2.5 cm long must be drawn between 6 and 8 cm long. Chinedu draws it 7.5 cm long. Find the magnification and check the size rule.

  1. Substitute (both already in cm): M = 7.5 ÷ 2.5.
  2. Divide: M = 3.0, so the drawing is ×3.0.
  3. Check the size rule: 7.5 cm lies between 6 cm and 8 cm, so the size rule is met.
Answer: ×3.0, size rule met
EXAMPLE 3

Actual size from a labelled drawing

A drawing labelled ×1500 shows a cell 60 mm long. Find the actual length of the cell in micrometres (µm).

  1. Rearrange and substitute: actual length = drawing length ÷ M = 60 ÷ 1500.
  2. Divide (answer in mm): actual length = 0.04 mm.
  3. Convert to µm (1 mm = 1000 µm): 0.04 × 1000 = 40 µm.
Answer: 40 µm

Part A · Home investigation: drawing to size at home

Practise the nine drawing rules and the magnification formula with foods from your mother’s kitchen.

Practical task

Materials: a clean 30 cm ruler, a sharp HB pencil, an eraser, plain A4 paper, one cowpea (beans) seed, one groundnut kernel, one maize grain, one onion bulb, and a hand lens if you have one.

Safety: ask an adult to cut the onion with the kitchen knife. Do not eat the specimens after handling. Wash your hands when you finish.

  1. Lay each seed or grain on the ruler and measure its length in cm to the nearest 0.1 cm.
  2. For each specimen, choose a whole-number or half-number magnification that makes your drawing 6–8 cm long. Work out drawing length = M × actual length before you draw.
  3. Draw each specimen with clean, continuous lines and no shading. Write a title below it and the magnification (for example “Mag. ×5”) with no unit.
  4. Label at least two parts you can really see (for the cowpea: testa, hilum) with ruled, horizontal guidelines that touch the part, do not cross and have no arrowheads. Write each label as one noun, lined up in a column.

Part B · Written paper (30 marks)

1. Describe how you would prepare a temporary iodine-stained slide of onion epidermis for viewing under a light microscope. Give a reason for any one of your steps.

5 marks
Model answer unlocks after you try

A fleshy scale leaf was removed from the onion bulb and the thin, transparent inner epidermis was peeled off with forceps (1). A small piece was laid flat, without folding, on a clean glass slide (1). It was stained with a drop of dilute iodine solution (1). A cover slip was lowered gently at about 45° with a mounted needle, and excess liquid was blotted off with filter paper (1). Explain briefly why: (a) a thin peel from the inner surface of the scale leaf was used for Specimen A, rather than a slice of onion; (b) methylene blue suits cheek cells better than a drop of plain water; (c) a student can tell an air bubble on the slide apart from a cell; (d) the cell membrane of Specimen A was not labelled in your drawing.

Model answer unlocks after you try

(a) The inner peel is one cell thick, so light passes through and each cell is seen on its own; a slice is many cells thick, so cells overlap, look dark and no single layer can be focused (1).
(b) Cheek cells are thin and almost colourless, so in water they are very hard to see; methylene blue stains the nucleus dark blue and the cytoplasm pale blue, giving contrast (1).
(c) An air bubble is a perfectly round or oval outline with a thick dark rim and a bright empty centre with no nucleus; a cell has a thin boundary, stained cytoplasm and a nucleus, and is box-like (onion) or irregular (cheek) (1).
(d) The membrane is pressed tightly against the cell wall by the full vacuole, so it cannot be seen as a separate line; it is seen only if the cell is plasmolysed, for example in salt solution (1).

6. (a) A cheek cell 0.08 mm across is drawn 64 mm across. Calculate the magnification of the drawing. (b) Chinedu’s drawing of an onion epidermal cell is 7.0 cm long and is labelled “Mag. ×350”. Calculate the actual length of the cell in µm. (c) A strip of onion scale leaf 1.4 cm long is drawn at ×5. Does the drawing meet the instruction “6–8 cm long”? Show your working.

5 marks
Model answer unlocks after you try

(a) M = drawing length ÷ actual length = 64 mm ÷ 0.08 mm (1) = 800, so ×800 (1).
(b) Convert: 7.0 cm = 70 mm; actual = 70 mm ÷ 350 = 0.20 mm (1); 0.20 × 1000 = 200 µm (1).
(c) drawing length = 5 × 1.4 cm = 7.0 cm, which lies between 6 and 8 cm, so yes, it meets the instruction (1).
