WAEC Paper 3 Wet-Mount Precautions: (1) Peel a single-cell-thick translucent inner epidermis so light passes clearly through without overlapping cell layers. (2) Keep the peel flat in a drop of water/stain so it does not fold over on itself. (3) Lower the coverslip slowly at a 45° angle using a mounting needle to expel air bubbles. (4) Blot excess stain around the coverslip edges with filter paper before placing the slide on the microscope stage.

STEP 01

Strip monolayer & mount flat

Use forceps to peel the translucent inner epidermis from a fleshy onion scale leaf and lay it flat in a drop of water.

STEP 02

Add stain & lower coverslip at 45°

Add a drop of iodine solution (or methylene blue for cheek cells) and lower the coverslip at 45° with a mounting needle.

STEP 03

Calibrate FOV: D₂ = D₁ × (M₁ ÷ M₂)

At ×100, field diameter is 1.8 mm = 1800 µm. At ×400, diameter shrinks 4× to 450 µm.

STEP 04

Compute A = DFOV ÷ Ncells

Divide the field diameter D_FOV by the number of cells N spanning the diameter to get mean cell length A.

Practical Analysis Questions

Answer these three WAEC Paper 3 practical questions, then compare with the model answers.

  1. Why does the number of onion cells visible across the diameter drop from 24 cells at ×100 down to 6 cells at ×400?
  2. How can a student immediately tell the difference between a true cell nucleus and a trapped air bubble under the microscope?
  3. State two reasons why a biological specimen is stained before viewing under a light microscope.
Model answers unlocks after you try
  1. Increasing magnification 4 times (×100 → ×400) zooms in on a 4-times narrower diameter of the slide (1800 µm → 450 µm), so only one-quarter as many cells fit across the diameter (24 ÷ 4 = 6 cells).
  2. An air bubble is perfectly circular with a thick, dark black refractive rim and an empty bright centre, whereas a stained nucleus is oval/spherical with granular chromatin, a nucleolus, and a specific stain colour (yellow-brown in iodine or dark blue in methylene blue).
  3. 1. Most cell structures (cytoplasm, nucleus, tonoplast) are nearly transparent; staining increases optical contrast so they become clearly visible.
    2. Differential stains chemically identify specific cell components (e.g., iodine turns starch grains blue-black; acetocarmine stains chromosomes red).

2 · Official WAEC Paper 3 Biological Drawing Rubric

When WAEC or NECO examiners mark a biological drawing (typically 8–12 marks), they award marks strictly under five headings:

  1. Clarity of Lines (CL — 2 marks): Use a sharp HB or 2H pencil. All outlines must be thin, single, sharp, continuous, and completely joined. Never sketch with feathery, broken, or overlapping lines. Draw a double line to show the thickness of a plant cell wall; draw a single line for an animal plasma membrane or tonoplast. Never shade, paint, or cross-hatch (use sparse stippling dots only where granular cytoplasm is required).
  2. Size of Drawing (Sz — 1 mark): The drawing must be reasonably large — normally at least 6 cm to 10 cm long (occupying about one-third to half of the answer space) so internal structures are never cramped.
  3. Accuracy & Proportionality (1–2 marks): Draw what you actually observe (e.g., brick/hexagonal shape and peripheral nucleus for onion epidermis; irregular shape and central nucleus for cheek cell). Never draw stylized textbook cartoons or trapped air bubbles.
  4. Neatness of Labels / Guidelines (NL — 2–4 marks):
    • Rule all label lines with a straight ruler (never freehand).
    • Make label lines horizontal and parallel wherever possible.
    • Never cross two label lines.
    • Never put arrowheads at the end of label lines — the line must simply touch the exact structure being named.
    • Write labels horizontally in neat print at the end of the guideline (never write on top of the guideline).
  5. Title & Linear Magnification (2 marks): Write a clear, underlined title below the drawing (e.g., Drawing of a Stained Onion Epidermal Cell as Seen Under High Power) followed by the calculated linear magnification (e.g., ×800 or ×1200).

3 · Field-of-View Calibration & Drawing Magnification Equations

D_2 = D_1 × (M_1 ÷ M_2) = 1800 × (100 ÷ 400) = 1800 × 0.25 = 450 µm
A = D_FOV ÷ N_cells = 450 ÷ 6 = 75 µm
M_drawing = I ÷ A = (90 × 1000) ÷ 75 = 90000 ÷ 75 = 1200×

EXAMPLE 1

Complete Field-of-View Calibration, Mean Cell Size & Drawing Magnification

Using a transparent millimetre ruler at low power (×100), a student measures the circular field-of-view diameter of her microscope as D₁ = 1.8 mm. She then switches to high power (×400) and counts 6 onion epidermal cells fitting end-to-end across the diameter. Finally, she draws one onion cell measuring 90 mm in length. Calculate: (a) the low-power field diameter in µm, (b) the high-power (×400) field diameter D₂ in µm, (c) the mean actual length A of one onion cell in µm, and (d) the linear magnification M of her biological drawing.

  1. Part (a) — Convert low-power field diameter D₁ from mm to µm: D_1 = 1.8 × 1000 = 1800 µm.
  2. Part (b) — High-power (×400) field-of-view diameter D₂: D_2 = 1800 × (100 ÷ 400) = 1800 × 0.25 = 450 µm.
  3. Part (c) — Mean actual length A of one onion cell: A = D_2 ÷ N_cells = 450 ÷ 6 = 75 µm.
  4. Part (d) Step 1 — Convert drawing length I from mm to µm: I = 90 × 1000 = 90000 µm.
  5. Part (d) Step 2 — Calculate linear magnification M of the drawing: M = I ÷ A = 90000 ÷ 75 = 1200×.
(a) D₁ = 1800 µm  ·  (b) D₂ = 450 µm  ·  (c) A = 75 µm  ·  (d) Drawing Mag. = ×1200
EXAMPLE 2

Estimating Human Cheek Cell Diameter from Field of View

Across the same ×400 field of view (D₂ = 450 µm), a student observes that 10 human cheek epithelial cells would fit end-to-end across the diameter. She makes a biological drawing of one cheek cell measuring 63 mm across. Calculate: (a) the actual diameter A of one cheek cell in µm, and (b) the magnification of her drawing.

  1. Part (a) — Actual diameter A of one cheek cell: A = 450 ÷ 10 = 45 µm.
  2. Part (b) Step 1 — Convert drawing width I from mm to µm: I = 63 × 1000 = 63000 µm.
  3. Part (b) Step 2 — Calculate drawing magnification M: M = 63000 ÷ 45 = 1400×.
(a) Cheek cell diameter A = 45 µm  ·  (b) Drawing Magnification = ×1400
EXAMPLE 3

Comparing Light Microscope vs Electron Microscope Resolution Factor

A school compound light microscope has a resolution limit of d_light = 200 nm, whereas a transmission electron microscope has a resolution limit of d_TEM = 0.2 nm. How many times finer is the resolving power of the transmission electron microscope compared with the light microscope?

  1. Divide the light resolution limit by the TEM resolution limit: Factor = d_light ÷ d_TEM = 200 ÷ 0.2 = 1000.
The TEM has a 1000 times finer resolving power than the light microscope

Part B · Structured Written Questions (30 marks)

1. (a) Describe step by step how you would prepare a temporary stained wet mount of onion epidermal cells for examination under a compound microscope [6 marks]. (b) State four precautions you would take during the preparation and viewing of the slide [4 marks].

10 marks
Solution unlocks after you try

(a) 1. Place a clean glass microscope slide on the bench and put a drop of water in the centre (1). 2. Cut a fleshy scale leaf from an onion bulb and use forceps to peel off a thin, translucent strip of the inner epidermis (1). 3. Spread the epidermal peel flat in the drop of water using a mounting needle (1). 4. Add one drop of iodine solution to stain the cells (1). 5. Hold a clean glass coverslip at a 45° angle with its edge touching the drop and lower it gently using a mounting needle (1). 6. Blot any excess stain around the coverslip edges with filter paper and clip the slide onto the microscope stage, starting with the low-power (×4 or ×10) objective lens (1).
(b) Precautions (past tense): 1. “I lowered the coverslip gently at an angle of 45° using a mounting needle to avoid trapping air bubbles.” (1) 2. “I ensured the epidermal strip was spread flat and not folded on the slide.” (1) 3. “I blotted excess iodine stain around the coverslip with filter paper to keep the stage and lenses clean.” (1) 4. “I used only the fine adjustment knob when focusing under the high-power objective lens.” (1)

2. (a) State five rules that must be obeyed to earn full marks in a WAEC Biology Paper 3 diagram [5 marks]. (b) Tabulate five differences between a compound light microscope and a transmission electron microscope [5 marks].

10 marks
Solution unlocks after you try

(a) 1. Draw sharp, thin, single, continuous outlines with a sharp HB/2H pencil (no feathery lines) (1). 2. Do not shade, paint, or cross-hatch any part of the drawing (1). 3. Make the drawing large (at least 6–10 cm long) and proportional (1). 4. Rule horizontal, uncrossed label lines with a ruler and never put arrowheads on them (1). 5. Write a clear title and the linear magnification (e.g., ×800) below the drawing (1).
(b) 1. Radiation source: Visible light vs Electron beam (1). 2. Lenses: Glass optical lenses vs Electromagnetic lenses (1). 3. Resolving power: Low (~200 nm) vs Very high (~0.2 nm) (1). 4. Maximum magnification: ~×1,500 vs ~×500,000 (1). 5. Specimen state: Living or dead specimens in air/water vs Only dead dehydrated specimens in a vacuum (1).

3. At ×100 magnification, the diameter of the circular field of view of a microscope is 2.0 mm. (a) Convert this diameter to micrometres (µm) [2 marks]. (b) Calculate the diameter of the field of view when the student switches to ×400 magnification [3 marks]. (c) At ×400 magnification, 8 epidermal cells fit end-to-end across the diameter. Calculate the actual length A of one cell in µm [2 marks]. (d) If the student draws one cell measuring 75 mm in length, calculate the magnification of her drawing [3 marks].

10 marks
Solution unlocks after you try

(a) Low-power field diameter in µm: D_1 = 2.0 × 1000 = 2000 µm (2).
(b) High-power (×400) field diameter: D_2 = 2000 × (100 ÷ 400) = 2000 × 0.25 = 500 µm (3).
(c) Mean actual length of one cell: A = 500 ÷ 8 = 62.5 µm (2).
(d) Convert drawing length to µm: I = 75 × 1000 = 75000 µm (1). Drawing magnification: M = 75000 ÷ 62.5 = 1200× (2).
