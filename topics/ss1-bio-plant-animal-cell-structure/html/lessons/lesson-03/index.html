Forms in Which Living Cells Exist & Levels of Organisation

Why can a single microscopic Amoeba in Ogun River water breathe across its plasma membrane alone, while a Tilapia fish or onion plant requires tissues, organs, and transport systems?

  • 02Explain the structural adaptations of Amoeba, Paramecium, Euglena (mixotrophic plant + animal traits), and Chlamydomonas, and contrast colonial Volvox with filamentous Spirogyra.
  • 03Classify biological structures across the five levels of organisation (Cell → Tissue → Organ → Organ System → Organism), including why an onion bulb is an organ and why Hydra is at the tissue level.
  • 04Calculate surface area SA = 6L2, volume V = L3, and SA ÷ V = 6 ÷ L for cuboidal cells to explain why large organisms need mass-transport systems.
  • 05Evaluate the biological advantages and disadvantages of complexity and division of labour in multicellular organisms.

2Investigate · 3D Pond Safari & SA/V Scaling Bench · 15 min

3D Pond Micro-Safari & Surface-Area-to-Volume (SA ÷ V = 6 ÷ L) Bench

Tap Trials A–E below to inspect five 3D pond specimens (Euglena, Paramecium, Amoeba, Volvox, Spirogyra) while measuring how increasing cube side length L from 1.0 mm to 6.0 mm changes surface area SA = 6L², volume V = L³, and the critical ratio SA ÷ V = 6 ÷ L.

Biophysical Scaling Law: Every living cell exchanges oxygen, nutrients, and wastes across its surface area ($\propto L^2$) to support the metabolic demand of its volume ($\propto L^3$). When L grows 6 times larger, surface area increases 36× but volume increases 216× — so SA ÷ V drops to one-sixth!

Practical Analysis Questions

Answer these three WAEC scaling and protist analysis questions, then compare with the model answers.

  1. Why does the ratio SA ÷ V decrease from 6.00 mm⁻¹ to 1.00 mm⁻¹ when cube side length increases from 1 mm to 6 mm?
  2. How do single cells like root hair cells or intestinal epithelial cells increase their SA ÷ V ratio without becoming smaller?
  3. Why does a freshwater Amoeba or Paramecium burst if its contractile vacuole is inactivated, whereas a marine protozoan in isotonic seawater rarely needs a contractile vacuole?
Model answers unlocks after you try
  1. Surface area grows with the square of length (6L²) whereas volume grows with the cube of length (L³), so SA ÷ V = 6 ÷ L falls inversely with length.
  2. By folding their plasma membrane into projections (microvilli in intestinal cells, long tubular extensions in root hair cells, or flattened biconcave discs in red blood cells).
  3. Freshwater is strongly hypotonic to protozoan cytoplasm, so water enters continuously by osmosis and must be pumped out by the contractile vacuole; seawater is nearly isotonic to marine protozoans.

Four Forms of Living Cells & Five Levels of Biological Organisation

Master every example, diagram feature, and classification rule tested in WAEC, NECO, and JAMB Biology.

1 · The Four Forms in Which Living Cells Exist

FormKey Organisms & Structural AdaptationsWAEC / JAMB Highlights
1. Single, Free-Living (Independent) Unicellular Organisms • Amoeba proteus: Irregular shape; finger-like pseudopodia for amoeboid movement & phagocytosis; contractile vacuole for osmoregulation; food vacuoles; ectoplasm & endoplasm.
• Paramecium caudatum: Fixed slipper shape (pellicle); hair-like cilia for fast swimming & sweeping bacteria down the oral groove → cytopharynx (gullet); two star-shaped contractile vacuoles; macronucleus (metabolism) & micronucleus (conjugation); anal pore (cytoproct); trichocysts (defence).
• Euglena viridis (Mixotroph): Spindle-shaped; Plant traits: chloroplasts, pyrenoids, paramylon carbohydrate granules (holophytic/autotrophic in light). Animal traits: flexible protein pellicle (no cellulose wall), long flagellum for locomotion, red stigma (eyespot) for positive phototaxis, gullet/reservoir, contractile vacuole, saprophytic/holozoic nutrition in darkness.
• Chlamydomonas: Unicellular green alga; cellulose cell wall, single large cup-shaped chloroplast, pyrenoid (starch storage), red eyespot, two equal anterior flagella, two apical contractile vacuoles. 		A single cell performs all 7 life processes (MR NIGER D) independently.
2. As a Colony • Volvox aureus: Hollow mucilaginous sphere of 500 – 50,000 Chlamydomonas-like cells interconnected by fine cytoplasmic strands.
• Pandorina (16 cells) & Eudorina (32 cells): Simpler colonies where all cells are similar and reproductive. 		In Volvox, flagella beat in a coordinated wave and cells show division of labour between anterior vegetative somatic cells and posterior reproductive gonidia (daughter colonies).
3. As a Filament • Spirogyra: Unbranched green algal filament of cylindrical cells joined end-to-end; outer mucilage sheath (slimy feel); cellulose cell wall; spiral ribbon chloroplast studded with pyrenoids; nucleus suspended by cytoplasmic strands above a large central vacuole.
• Oscillatoria & Nostoc: Filamentous cyanobacteria. 		Cells divide by mitosis in one plane only and remain attached end-to-end, yet each cell in Spirogyra is physiologically independent.
4. As Part of a Multicellular Organism • Specialized plant cells: palisade mesophyll cell, guard cell, root hair cell, xylem vessel, phloem sieve tube.
• Specialized animal cells: red blood cell, motor neurone, spermatozoon, ovum, ciliated epithelial cell, muscle fibre. 		Cells differentiate structurally to perform one specific role and cannot survive in isolation.

2 · The Five Levels of Biological Organisation

  1. Level 1 — Cell: The basic structural and functional unit of life (e.g., Amoeba, guard cell, neurone, spermatozoon, ovum).
  2. Level 2 — Tissue: A group of similar (or coordinated) cells having a common embryonic origin and intercellular substance that perform a specific function.
    • Plant tissues: Epidermis, parenchyma, collenchyma, sclerenchyma, xylem, phloem.
    • Animal tissues: Epithelial, connective (blood, bone, cartilage), muscular, nervous.
    • Organisms at the Tissue Level: Coelenterates / Cnidarians such as Hydra, jellyfish, and sea anemones (diploblastic: ectoderm + endoderm separated by mesoglea, with no true organs).
  3. Level 3 — Organ: A collection of different tissues grouped together into a distinct structural unit to perform one or more major functions.
    • Plant organs: Onion bulb (scale-leaf epidermis + fleshy storage parenchyma + xylem/phloem + stem disc), leaf, root, stem, rhizome (ginger), tuber (yam/Irish potato), flower, fruit, seed.
    • Animal organs: Heart, kidney, eye, brain, stomach, liver, lungs, skin (largest mammalian organ).
  4. Level 4 — Organ System: A group of coordinated organs working together to carry out a major physiological process (e.g., shoot system and root system in plants; digestive, circulatory, respiratory, excretory, nervous, reproductive, and skeletal systems in animals).
  5. Level 5 — Multicellular Organism: A complete living individual made of integrated organ systems (e.g., maize plant, mango tree, Tilapia fish, human being).

3 · Surface-Area-to-Volume Ratio ($\frac{SA}{V}$) & Complexity Trade-Offs

SA = 6L^2   ·   V = L^3   ⇒   SA ÷ V = 6L^2 ÷ L^3 = 6 ÷ L
At L = 1 mm:   SA ÷ V = 6 ÷ 1 = 6.0 mm⁻¹   ·   At L = 6 mm:   SA ÷ V = 6 ÷ 6 = 1.0 mm⁻¹

4 · Advantages & Disadvantages of Complexity in Higher Organisms

Advantages of Multicellular ComplexityDisadvantages of Multicellular Complexity
1. Cellular Differentiation & Division of Labour: Specialized cells, tissues, and organs perform tasks concurrently without interference.1. Loss of Individual Cell Independence: Differentiated cells (e.g., neurones, RBCs) cannot survive on their own outside the body.
2. High Physiological Efficiency: Specialized structures (lungs, nephrons, palisade layer) operate at peak efficiency.2. Slower Rate of Diffusion (Low $\frac{SA}{V}$): Requires complex, energy-consuming respiratory and circulatory systems.
3. Attainment of Large Body Size: Internal skeletons and vascular tissues support large size.3. Slower Regeneration & Reproduction: Healing damaged organs and sexual reproduction take much longer than binary fission.
4. Adaptation to Diverse Habitats & Longer Lifespan: Homeostasis enables survival across dry land, air, and water; death of one cell does not kill the organism.4. High Interdependence Vulnerability: Failure of one vital organ (e.g., heart or brain) causes death of the entire organism.

EXAMPLE 1

Surface-Area-to-Volume Ratio (SA/V) of Small vs Large Cuboidal Organisms

Compare a cuboidal unicellular model of side length L₁ = 1 mm with a larger multicellular model of side length L₂ = 5 mm. Calculate for each: (a) total surface area SA = 6L², (b) volume V = L³, and (c) the surface-area-to-volume ratio SA ÷ V.

  1. Small Cell (L₁ = 1 mm) — Surface Area, Volume & Ratio: SA_1 = 6 × 1^2 = 6 × 1 = 6 mm².
  2. Small Cell Volume V₁: V_1 = 1^3 = 1 mm³.
  3. Small Cell SA ÷ V Ratio: Ratio_1 = 6 ÷ 1 = 6.0 mm⁻¹.
  4. Large Organism (L₂ = 5 mm) — Surface Area SA₂: SA_2 = 6 × 5^2 = 6 × 25 = 150 mm².
  5. Large Organism Volume V₂: V_2 = 5^3 = 125 mm³.
  6. Large Organism SA ÷ V Ratio: Ratio_2 = 150 ÷ 125 = 1.2 mm⁻¹.
L₁ = 1 mm gives SA/V = 6.0 mm⁻¹  ·  L₂ = 5 mm gives SA/V = 1.2 mm⁻¹ (5 times lower!)
EXAMPLE 2

Colonial Cell Population & Reproductive Gonidia Percentage in Volvox

A parent Volvox colony consists of 2500 cells, of which 10 are specialized reproductive gonidia and the rest are flagellated somatic cells. (a) Calculate the percentage of cells in the colony that are somatic. (b) If each of the 10 gonidia divides to form a daughter colony of 2500 cells, how many total cells are produced across all daughter colonies?

  1. Part (a) Step 1 — Number of somatic cells in parent colony: N_somatic = 2500 − 10 = 2490 cells.
  2. Part (a) Step 2 — Percentage of somatic cells: %Somatic = (2490 ÷ 2500) × 100 = 0.996 × 100 = 99.6%.
  3. Part (b) — Total cells produced across all 10 daughter colonies: N_total = 10 × 2500 = 25000 cells.
(a) 99.6% somatic cells  ·  (b) 25000 cells across 10 daughter colonies
EXAMPLE 3

Number of Cylindrical Cells in an Unbranched Spirogyra Filament

An unbranched filament of Spirogyra floating in Ogun River pond water measures 45 mm in total length. If each cylindrical cell in the filament has an actual length of 150 µm, calculate the total number of cells joined end-to-end in the filament.

  1. Step 1 — Convert total filament length from millimetres to micrometres: L_filament = 45 × 1000 = 45000 µm.
  2. Step 2 — Divide total filament length by single cell length: N_cells = 45000 ÷ 150 = 300 cells.
Total number of cells in filament = 300 cells

The big idea of Lesson 3: Living cells exist as single free-living units (Amoeba, Paramecium, Euglena, Chlamydomonas), colonies (Volvox), filaments (Spirogyra), or differentiated cells in multicellular organisms. Because SA ÷ V = 6 ÷ L falls as size grows, multicellular organisms organise Cells → Tissues → Organs (such as an onion bulb or heart) → Organ Systems to achieve high efficiency and overcome diffusion limits!

Part B · Structured Written Questions (30 marks)

1. (a) State four plant-like characteristics and four animal-like characteristics of Euglena viridis [8 marks]. (b) State two structural differences between Volvox and Spirogyra [2 marks].

10 marks
Solution unlocks after you try

(a) Plant-like traits of Euglena: 1. Possesses green chloroplasts containing chlorophyll (1). 2. Performs holophytic (autotrophic) photosynthesis in sunlight (1). 3. Possesses pyrenoids (1). 4. Stores carbohydrate as paramylon granules (1).
Animal-like traits of Euglena: 1. Lacks a cellulose cell wall; bounded by a flexible proteinaceous pellicle (1). 2. Moves actively using a long whip-like flagellum (1). 3. Possesses a light-sensitive eyespot (stigma) and gullet/reservoir (1). 4. Possesses a contractile vacuole for osmoregulation and feeds saprophytically in darkness (1).
(b) Volvox is a spherical motile colony of flagellated cells joined by cytoplasmic strands, whereas Spirogyra is a non-motile unbranched filament of cylindrical cells joined end-to-end (2).

2. (a) Classify each of the following into its correct level of biological organisation (Cell, Tissue, Organ, or System): (i) Spermatozoon (ii) Mammalian blood (iii) Onion bulb (iv) Hydra (v) Xylem (vi) Mammalian skin [6 marks]. (b) State two advantages and two disadvantages of complexity in higher organisms [4 marks].

10 marks
Solution unlocks after you try

(a) (i) Spermatozoon = Cell (1). (ii) Mammalian blood = Tissue (1). (iii) Onion bulb = Organ (1). (iv) Hydra = Tissue level (1). (v) Xylem = Tissue (1). (vi) Mammalian skin = Organ (1).
(b) Advantages: 1. Division of labour and cell specialization lead to high physiological efficiency (1). 2. Ability to grow large and survive in diverse terrestrial and aquatic habitats (1).
Disadvantages: 1. Individual specialized cells lose independence and cannot survive alone (1). 2. Lower surface-area-to-volume ratio requires complex transport systems and slower regeneration (1).

3. Two cuboidal organisms X and Y have side lengths L_X = 2 mm and L_Y = 8 mm respectively. (a) Calculate the surface area (SA = 6L²), volume (V = L³), and surface-area-to-volume ratio (SA ÷ V) of each organism [6 marks]. (b) Explain using your answers why organism Y cannot rely on simple diffusion across its body surface for oxygen supply [4 marks].

10 marks
Solution unlocks after you try

(a) For organism X (L = 2 mm): SA_X = 6 × 2^2 = 24 mm² (1); V_X = 2^3 = 8 mm³ (1); Ratio_X = 24 ÷ 8 = 3.0 mm⁻¹ (1).
For organism Y (L = 8 mm): SA_Y = 6 × 8^2 = 384 mm² (1); V_Y = 8^3 = 512 mm³ (1); Ratio_Y = 384 ÷ 512 = 0.75 mm⁻¹ (1).
(b) Organism Y has a four-times lower surface-area-to-volume ratio (0.75 mm⁻¹ vs 3.0 mm⁻¹) and a long internal diffusion distance (4 mm to the core), so oxygen diffusing through the outer skin is consumed long before reaching the deep internal tissues; hence Y requires a specialized respiratory and circulatory system (4).
