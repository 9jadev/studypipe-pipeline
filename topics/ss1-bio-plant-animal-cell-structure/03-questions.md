## Lesson 01 · Discovering the Cell: Microscopes, Cell Theory and Prokaryotes vs Eukaryotes

### Part 1 · 12 Interactive MCQs (`window.LESSON_QUIZ`)

1. **[WAEC-style · Understand]** At Mile 12 Market, Chinedu tells Mama Ngozi that Robert Hooke "saw cells" in a thin slice of cork in 1665. Which statement best describes what Hooke actually saw?
   - **A.** Living cells, each with a clear nucleus
   - **B.** The jelly-like living material he called "sarcode"
   - **C.** The empty box-like walls of dead cork cells ✓
   - **D.** Bacteria swimming between the cork fibres
   - *Explanation:* Cork is dead bark tissue, so Hooke saw only the empty walls that once surrounded living cells; he named these box-like spaces "cells". Option B is the main trap: the living jelly ("sarcode") was described by Félix Dujardin in 1835, about 170 years later, not by Hooke.

2. **[NECO-style · Remember]** Which scientist first described the living, jelly-like contents of cells and named this material "sarcode"?
   - **A.** Félix Dujardin ✓
   - **B.** Matthias Schleiden
   - **C.** Theodor Schwann
   - **D.** Robert Hooke
   - *Explanation:* Dujardin (1835) showed that cells contain living material, not just walls, and called it sarcode. Schleiden (1838) and Schwann (1839) are remembered for stating that plants and animals respectively are made of cells; Hooke only named the empty compartments in dead cork.

3. **[JAMB-style · Understand]** The cell theory grew from the work of Schleiden on plants (1838) and Schwann on animals (1839). Which of the following is NOT a statement of the cell theory?
   - **A.** Every organism consists of one cell or of many cells.
   - **B.** Organisms are built from cells, and cells carry out their life processes.
   - **C.** New cells form only when existing cells divide.
   - **D.** Every cell contains a nucleus that controls its activities. ✓
   - *Explanation:* The cell theory makes no claim that every cell has a nucleus, and such a claim would be false: bacteria (prokaryotes) have no true nucleus, and a mature human red blood cell has lost its nucleus. Options A, B and C are the three accepted ideas (C is credited to Virchow, 1855). Candidates often pick C as "not part of it" because it was added later, but it is part of the modern theory.

4. **[WAEC-style · Remember]** Which of the following best defines an organelle?
   - **A.** The smallest unit of a living organism that can carry out all life processes
   - **B.** A tiny part inside the cytoplasm that is specialised to do one particular job for the cell ✓
   - **C.** A group of similar cells working together to perform one function
   - **D.** The jelly-like fluid that fills the space between the nucleus and the membrane
   - *Explanation:* An organelle is a specialised part *inside* a cell (e.g. mitochondrion, chloroplast) with a definite job. Option A is the definition of a *cell*, the commonest mix-up; C defines a tissue; D describes cytoplasm, which is not itself an organelle.

5. **[NECO-style · Understand]** Dr Amaka shows her class a single *Amoeba* in a drop of pond water from Badagry. On its own it feeds, respires, excretes, responds to light and divides. This observation best supports the idea that the cell is
   - **A.** the basic unit of life: the smallest part of an organism that is both its building block (structure) and its working unit (function) ✓
   - **B.** always one part of a larger tissue
   - **C.** made only of organelles, with no cytoplasm
   - **D.** a building block (structure) of life but not a working unit (function)
   - *Explanation:* One *Amoeba* is one cell, yet it carries out every life process, so the cell is both the building block *and* the working unit of life. Option D is the trap: the evidence here is all about functions (feeding, respiring), so it shows the cell is a working unit as well as a building block. Option B is wrong because *Amoeba* is free-living, not part of a tissue.

6. **[WAEC Practical · Apply]** At the LUTH teaching laboratory, a student uses a microscope with a ×8 eyepiece and a ×40 objective. What is the total magnification?
   - **A.** ×48
   - **B.** ×5
   - **C.** ×3200
   - **D.** ×320 ✓
   - *Explanation:* Total magnification = eyepiece × objective = 8 × 40 = ×320. Option A (×48) comes from *adding* the two powers, the classic examiner trap; lenses in series multiply each other's effect. Option B divides 40 by 8, and C has a misplaced zero.

7. **[WAEC Practical · Understand]** Having focused an onion peel on the ×4 objective, Chinedu turns to the ×40 objective. Which knob should he now use to focus, and why?
   - **A.** The coarse adjustment knob, because it brings the image into view faster
   - **B.** The fine adjustment knob, because the ×40 objective sits very close to the slide and could crack it if moved a lot ✓
   - **C.** The coarse adjustment knob, because the ×40 lens needs a large movement to focus
   - **D.** The fine adjustment knob, because it changes the magnification of the eyepiece
   - *Explanation:* The high-power objective has a very short working distance, so only the small movements of the fine knob are safe; large coarse-knob turns can drive the lens into the slide and damage both. Option D is wrong because no focusing knob changes magnification; magnification is set by the lenses chosen.

8. **[WAEC-style · Apply]** At ×100, the field of view of a microscope is 1.2 mm in diameter. Fifteen cheek cells lie end to end across it. What is the approximate length of one cheek cell?
   - **A.** 18 µm
   - **B.** 8 µm
   - **C.** 80 µm ✓
   - **D.** 800 µm
   - *Explanation:* Cell length = field diameter ÷ number of cells = 1.2 mm ÷ 15 = 0.08 mm; 0.08 mm × 1000 = 80 µm. Option B (8 µm) comes from multiplying by 100 instead of 1000 when converting; remember 1 mm = 1000 µm. Option A comes from multiplying 1.2 by 15.

9. **[NECO-style · Apply]** A photomicrograph of a ugu leaf cell taken at ×450 shows the cell as 18 mm long. What is the actual length of the cell?
   - **A.** 4 µm
   - **B.** 40 µm ✓
   - **C.** 400 µm
   - **D.** 8100 µm
   - *Explanation:* Actual size = image size ÷ magnification = 18 mm ÷ 450 = 0.04 mm; 0.04 mm × 1000 = 40 µm. Option D comes from *multiplying* 18 × 450 = 8100, which makes the real cell larger than its photograph, which is impossible. Options A and C are conversion slips by a factor of ten.

10. **[JAMB-style · Apply]** A cheek cell with an actual length of 0.07 mm is drawn 28 mm long in a practical notebook. What is the magnification of the drawing?
    - **A.** ×400 ✓
    - **B.** 400 mm
    - **C.** ×40
    - **D.** ×0.0025
    - *Explanation:* Magnification = drawing length ÷ actual length = 28 mm ÷ 0.07 mm = ×400. Both lengths are in mm, so the units cancel and the answer has *no unit*; option B (400 mm) loses the mark for attaching a unit. Option D (0.07 ÷ 28 = 0.0025) divides the wrong way round.

11. **[WAEC-style · Analyse]** A swab from a hospital ward at LUTH contains cells with a cell wall, ribosomes, and a single loop of circular DNA lying free in the cytoplasm. No nuclear membrane or mitochondria can be found. The cells are most likely
    - **A.** yeast cells
    - **B.** onion epidermal cells
    - **C.** bacteria ✓
    - **D.** human red blood cells
    - *Explanation:* Circular DNA free in the cytoplasm, no nuclear membrane and no membrane-bound organelles such as mitochondria are the marks of a prokaryote, i.e. a bacterium. Yeast (A) and onion cells (B) also have cell walls, but they are eukaryotes with a nucleus and mitochondria; the wall alone cannot identify a prokaryote. Red blood cells (D) have no wall.

12. **[JAMB-style · Analyse]** Which structure is found in BOTH prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells?
    - **A.** Nuclear membrane
    - **B.** Mitochondria
    - **C.** Endoplasmic reticulum
    - **D.** Ribosomes ✓
    - *Explanation:* All cells make proteins, so both prokaryotes and eukaryotes have ribosomes; prokaryotic ribosomes are smaller. A nuclear membrane, mitochondria and endoplasmic reticulum are membrane-bound structures found only in eukaryotes. Ribosomes themselves have no membrane, which is why prokaryotes can have them.

### Part 2 · Home Investigation & 30-Mark Written Paper (Lesson 01)

**Part A · Home Investigation:** *Build a water-drop lens and measure its magnification.*
You need: a clear plastic sachet-water bag or cling film, a drop of clean water, a 30 cm ruler, a printed newspaper or a page of your exercise book, and the thin skin from an onion bulb (peeled off with your fingers, no blade needed).
1. Stretch the clear plastic flat over the newspaper and place one small, round drop of water on it.
2. Choose a large printed headline letter, 5–10 mm tall (e.g. a capital "N" from a newspaper headline). Measure its real height with the ruler, in mm.
3. Look straight down through the drop, hold the ruler beside it and estimate the height of the letter as it appears through the drop.
4. Calculate magnification = apparent height ÷ real height. Repeat with two more letters and find the average.
5. Lay a piece of onion skin under the plastic and look at it through the drop. Sketch what you see.
6. Wash your hands after handling the onion and wipe up any spilled water.

Sample results table:

| Letter | Real height (mm) | Apparent height through drop (mm) | Magnification |
|---|---|---|---|
| N | 6.0 | 15.0 | ×2.5 |
| E | 8.0 | 20.0 | ×2.5 |
| A | 10.0 | 22.5 | ×2.25 |
| **Average** | | | **×2.4** (to 2 s.f.) |

*What a good answer shows:* magnification found by division (not subtraction) and written with no unit; an average across at least three readings; a note that the onion skin looks like a faint pattern of tiny boxes, too small to see clearly because the drop magnifies only about ×2 to ×3, which is why Hooke and later scientists needed compound microscopes (two lenses whose powers *multiply*). A strong answer also states one source of error, e.g. parallax when reading the ruler beside a curved drop.

**Part B · Written Paper (30 marks)**

**b1 · Definitions (3 marks)**
(a) Define a *cell*. (b) Define an *organelle*. (c) Name one organelle found in a eukaryotic cell but never in a prokaryotic cell.
*Model solution:* (a) A cell is the basic unit of life: the smallest part of an organism that is both its building block (structure) and its working unit (function). (1) (b) An organelle is a tiny part inside the cytoplasm that is specialised to do one particular job for the cell. (1) (c) Any one membrane-bound organelle: nucleus / mitochondrion / chloroplast / endoplasmic reticulum / Golgi body. (1)
*Mark scheme note:* Equivalent wordings that keep the key ideas are credited. "Building block of life" alone is too vague for (a); both the structure idea and the function idea are expected. Ribosome is NOT accepted in (c), since prokaryotes also have ribosomes. Cell wall is not accepted, since bacteria have walls.

**b2 · The Cell Theory and Its Founders (4 marks)**
(a) State two ideas of the cell theory. (b) State the contribution of Schleiden and of Schwann to the cell theory.
*Model solution:* (a) Any two of: every organism consists of one cell or of many cells (1); organisms are built from cells, and cells carry out their life processes (1); new cells form only when existing cells divide (1). (Maximum 2.) (b) Schleiden (1838) stated that all plants are made of cells (1); Schwann (1839) stated that all animals are made of cells (1).
*Mark scheme note:* Equivalent wordings that keep the key ideas are credited. Candidates who swap Schleiden (plants) and Schwann (animals) lose both marks in (b). "Cells are small" or "cells have a nucleus" are not statements of the cell theory and score zero.

**b3 · Correcting Coach Danjuma (3 marks)**
Coach Danjuma says, "Robert Hooke discovered living cells in 1665." Explain why this statement is incorrect, and state who first described the living contents of cells.
*Model solution:* Hooke looked at thin slices of cork, which is dead plant tissue. (1) He saw only the empty, box-like walls left behind by dead cells and named these spaces "cells"; he did not see any living contents. (1) The living jelly inside cells was first described by Félix Dujardin in 1835, who called it "sarcode". (1)
*Mark scheme note:* "Hooke used a weak microscope" does not answer the question; the key point is that cork is DEAD. Writing "Dujardin discovered the nucleus" or naming Schwann for the living contents scores zero for the third mark.

**b4 · The Compound Microscope (3 marks)**
(a) State the function of the fine adjustment knob. (b) A microscope at a Kano secondary school has a ×12 eyepiece and a ×40 objective. Calculate the total magnification.
*Model solution:* (a) It moves the stage or tube through very small distances to bring the image into sharp focus, especially at high power. (1) (b) Total magnification = eyepiece × objective (1) = 12 × 40 = ×480. (1)
*Mark scheme note:* Adding the powers (12 + 40 = 52) scores zero in (b). "It focuses the image" without the idea of small or fine movement is not credited in (a).

**b5 · Cell Size from the Field of View (5 marks)**
Chinedu examines a ugu leaf peel at ×100. The field of view is 1.8 mm in diameter and 24 cells lie end to end across it.
(a) Calculate the length of one cell in mm. (b) Convert your answer to µm. (c) When he switches to ×400, the field diameter becomes 0.45 mm. How many of the same cells would now fit across the field?
*Model solution:* (a) Cell length = field diameter ÷ number of cells (1) = 1.8 mm ÷ 24 = 0.075 mm. (1) (b) 0.075 mm × 1000 = 75 µm. (1) (c) Number of cells = 0.45 mm ÷ 0.075 mm (1) = 6 cells. (1)
*Mark scheme note:* Many candidates think the cell "gets bigger" at ×400 and change its length; the cell's real size does not change, only the field of view shrinks (1.8 × 100 ÷ 400 = 0.45 mm). Multiplying by 100 instead of 1000 in (b) loses the mark. Units must be shown in (a) and (b).

**b6 · Actual Size and Drawing Magnification (5 marks)**
(a) A bacterium from a LUTH laboratory culture appears 10 mm long in an electron micrograph taken at ×5000. Calculate its actual length in mm and in µm. (b) A yeast cell of actual length 8 µm is drawn 32 mm long. Calculate the magnification of the drawing.
*Model solution:* (a) Actual size = image size ÷ magnification (1) = 10 mm ÷ 5000 = 0.002 mm (1) = 0.002 × 1000 = 2 µm. (1) (b) Convert to the same unit: 8 µm = 0.008 mm. (1) Magnification = 32 mm ÷ 0.008 mm = ×4000. (1)
*Mark scheme note:* In (b), dividing 32 by 8 without converting units gives ×4, a common and heavily penalised error. Writing "4000 mm" loses the final mark: magnification has no unit.

**b7 · Prokaryotes versus Eukaryotes (4 marks)**
Using a table, state four structural differences between a prokaryotic cell and a eukaryotic cell.
*Model solution:*

| Feature | Prokaryotic cell | Eukaryotic cell |
|---|---|---|
| Nuclear membrane | Absent; DNA lies free in the cytoplasm | Present; DNA enclosed in a true nucleus (1) |
| Membrane-bound organelles (mitochondria, ER, Golgi) | Absent | Present (1) |
| Ribosomes | Smaller | Larger (1) |
| DNA | Single, circular | Linear chromosomes (1) |
| Size (alternative row) | Usually smaller | Usually larger |

*Mark scheme note:* Each mark needs a matched pair on the same row; listing prokaryote features in one paragraph and eukaryote features in another scores at most half. "Prokaryotes have no ribosomes" is wrong and is penalised. "Cell wall present | absent" is not accepted, because plant and fungal eukaryotic cells also have walls.

**b8 · Practical: Viewing an Onion Peel (3 marks)**
A student mounted a thin onion epidermis in a drop of iodine solution and viewed it under a compound microscope. (a) State two precautions the student took. (b) Explain why the student began observation with the low-power (×4) objective.
*Model solution:* (a) Any two, in the past tense: the cover slip was lowered gently at an angle to avoid trapping air bubbles (1); the peel was spread flat so it was one cell layer thick (1); excess stain was blotted off with filter paper before viewing (1); the high-power objective was focused only with the fine knob so the slide was not cracked (1). (Maximum 2.) (b) The low-power objective gives a wider field of view, making it easier to find and centre the specimen before switching to higher power. (1)
*Mark scheme note:* Precautions written as instructions ("lower the cover slip…") or in the future tense lose the marks in (a). General laboratory rules ("wore a lab coat") are not accepted as precautions specific to this experiment.

## Lesson 02 · Inside the Cell: Organelles, Plant vs Animal Cells

### Part 1 · 12 Interactive MCQs (`window.LESSON_QUIZ`)

1. **[WAEC-style · Apply]** Coach Danjuma trains sprinters at the Teslim Balogun Stadium. A thigh-muscle cell of one of his athletes contracts many times a minute during a race. Which organelle would you expect to find in unusually large numbers in this cell?
   - **A.** Mitochondria ✓
   - **B.** Lysosomes
   - **C.** Centrioles
   - **D.** Chloroplasts
   - *Explanation:* Muscle contraction needs a constant supply of energy. Mitochondria carry out aerobic respiration and release that energy as ATP, so very active cells such as muscle cells are packed with them. Lysosomes hold digestive enzymes for breaking down worn-out parts; they do not supply energy. Chloroplasts are never found in animal cells.

2. **[JAMB-style · Remember]** Which of the following cell structures is NOT bounded by any membrane?
   - **A.** Lysosome
   - **B.** Mitochondrion
   - **C.** Ribosome ✓
   - **D.** Golgi body
   - *Explanation:* A ribosome is a tiny granule of RNA and protein with no membrane at all, which is why ribosomes are found even in prokaryotes. The lysosome and Golgi body each have one membrane; the mitochondrion has two (an outer membrane and an inner one folded into cristae).

3. **[NECO-style · Understand]** In which of the following pairs is EACH organelle bounded by a double membrane?
   - **A.** Nucleus and lysosome
   - **B.** Mitochondrion and endoplasmic reticulum
   - **C.** Chloroplast and ribosome
   - **D.** Nucleus and chloroplast ✓
   - *Explanation:* The nucleus (nuclear envelope with pores), the mitochondrion and the chloroplast are the three double-membrane organelles. Option A fails because the lysosome has a single membrane; B fails because the ER is single-membraned; C fails because the ribosome has no membrane.

4. **[WAEC-style · Analyse]** At Mama Ngozi's stall, Chinedu drew up this comparison table for his homework. Which row contains an error?
   - **A.** Cell wall: plant cell present | animal cell absent
   - **B.** Food stored as: plant cell starch | animal cell glycogen
   - **C.** Position of nucleus: plant cell central | animal cell at the side ✓
   - **D.** Centrioles: plant cell absent | animal cell present
   - *Explanation:* The row is reversed. In a mature plant cell the large central sap vacuole pushes the nucleus and cytoplasm to the side; in an animal cell the nucleus is usually central. Rows A, B and D are all correct. Row D is a common worry, but centrioles are absent from flowering-plant cells and present in animal cells.

5. **[WAEC-style · Understand]** Mama Ngozi's nephew soaked ugu leaves in salty water. The salt solution passed straight through the outer layer of each leaf cell and reached the layer beneath it. Which statement correctly explains this?
   - **A.** The cell membrane is fully permeable, while the cell wall is selectively permeable.
   - **B.** The cellulose cell wall is fully permeable; it is the cell membrane beneath it that controls entry and exit. ✓
   - **C.** The cell wall is made of lipoprotein, which dissolves in salt water.
   - **D.** The tonoplast lies outside the cell wall and lets salt in.
   - *Explanation:* The cell wall is non-living cellulose with large gaps, so it is fully permeable and is not a membrane. The living lipoprotein cell membrane is selectively permeable and is the real "gatekeeper". Option A reverses the two; C gives the wall the wrong chemical make-up; D puts the tonoplast in the wrong place (it surrounds the sap vacuole, deep inside the cell).

6. **[NECO-style · Apply]** A cell in the pancreas makes a digestive enzyme (a protein) and releases it out of the cell. Which sequence correctly traces the enzyme from where it is made to where it leaves?
   - **A.** Nucleus → ribosome → lysosome → cell membrane
   - **B.** Ribosome → rough endoplasmic reticulum → Golgi body → cell membrane ✓
   - **C.** Golgi body → rough endoplasmic reticulum → ribosome → cell membrane
   - **D.** Ribosome → mitochondrion → Golgi body → cell wall
   - *Explanation:* Proteins are built on ribosomes attached to the rough ER, carried along the ER channels, sent in vesicles to the Golgi body to be modified and packaged, and released when the vesicle fuses with the cell membrane. Option C runs the route backwards. The nucleus holds the instructions but does not make the protein, and an animal cell has no cell wall (option D).

7. **[JAMB-style · Analyse]** Dr Amaka examines electron micrographs of a cell from a salivary gland, which secretes large amounts of the enzyme amylase. Which pair of organelles would be most abundant in this cell?
   - **A.** Rough endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi bodies ✓
   - **B.** Chloroplasts and sap vacuoles
   - **C.** Centrioles and lysosomes
   - **D.** Smooth endoplasmic reticulum and chloroplasts
   - *Explanation:* Amylase is a protein made for export, so a secretory cell needs lots of rough ER (ribosomes making and transporting protein) and many Golgi bodies (packaging and secreting it). Centrioles work in cell division, and lysosomes digest worn-out parts, so neither is linked to secretion. Chloroplasts are not found in human cells.

8. **[WAEC-style · Apply]** An electron micrograph at a magnification of ×40 000 shows a chloroplast with an image length of 200 mm. What is the actual length of the chloroplast?
   - **A.** 0.5 µm
   - **B.** 50 µm
   - **C.** 5 mm
   - **D.** 5 µm ✓
   - *Explanation:* Actual size = image size ÷ magnification = 200 mm ÷ 40 000 = 0.005 mm. Convert: 0.005 mm × 1000 = 5 µm. Option C is 0.005 mm wrongly written as 5 mm (the mm-to-µm conversion was skipped, so it is 1000 times too large); option B is a ×10 slip (50 µm) and option A a ÷10 slip (0.5 µm). Always change mm to µm (× 1000) after dividing.

9. **[WAEC Practical · Apply]** Chinedu's school microscope has a ×10 eyepiece and a ×10 objective. A mitochondrion in a cheek cell is 0.8 µm wide. The unaided eye can just make out a detail of about 0.1 mm. What is the width of the mitochondrion's image, and can he see it?
   - **A.** 8 µm; visible
   - **B.** 0.08 mm; not visible ✓
   - **C.** 0.8 mm; visible
   - **D.** 0.008 mm; not visible
   - *Explanation:* Total magnification = eyepiece × objective = 10 × 10 = ×100. Image size = actual size × magnification = 0.8 µm × 100 = 80 µm. Convert: 80 µm ÷ 1000 = 0.08 mm. Since 0.08 mm is less than 0.1 mm, the image is too small to see. Option A (and D, which is the same 8 µm written in mm) uses only one lens, 0.8 × 10, instead of the total magnification 10 × 10 = ×100; option C slips a factor of ten in the conversion.

10. **[JAMB-style · Analyse]** Chinedu says, "If I keep turning to higher-power lenses on the school light microscope, I will eventually see ribosomes." Which statement best explains why he is wrong?
    - **A.** Ribosomes are found only in prokaryotes, not in the cells used on school slides.
    - **B.** Ribosomes are destroyed by the stain used on slides.
    - **C.** Ribosomes are hidden behind the cell wall in every cell.
    - **D.** A light microscope cannot separate details smaller than about 0.2 µm, and a ribosome (about 0.025 µm) is about 8 times smaller than that limit. ✓
    - *Explanation:* Magnifying a blurred image only makes a bigger blur. The limit is resolving power: about 0.2 µm for a light microscope, and 0.2 µm ÷ 0.025 µm = 8, so a ribosome is about eight times below the limit. An electron microscope is needed. Option A is false because ribosomes are found in both prokaryotes and eukaryotes. Option C is false because animal cells have no wall and the wall is not opaque anyway.

11. **[WAEC-style · Remember]** An organelle has a double membrane and contains stacks called grana surrounded by a fluid stroma. Which option names the organelle and its function correctly?
    - **A.** Mitochondrion; aerobic respiration
    - **B.** Golgi body; packaging and secretion
    - **C.** Chloroplast; photosynthesis ✓
    - **D.** Nucleus; control of cell activities
    - *Explanation:* Grana (stacks of thylakoids holding chlorophyll) in a stroma are found only in the chloroplast, which carries out photosynthesis. The mitochondrion also has two membranes, but its inner membrane is folded into cristae around a matrix, not grana in a stroma.

12. **[NECO-style · Understand]** Which of the following statements about cell structures is correct?
    - **A.** The tonoplast is a single membrane that surrounds the sap vacuole of a plant cell. ✓
    - **B.** Ribosomes are surrounded by a double membrane.
    - **C.** The cell wall is a selectively permeable lipoprotein membrane.
    - **D.** Cilia and flagella arise from mitochondria.
    - *Explanation:* The large central vacuole of a plant cell is bounded by one membrane, the tonoplast. Ribosomes have no membrane (B); the cell wall is cellulose, fully permeable and not a membrane (C). Cilia and flagella arise from basal bodies, not mitochondria (D). Several online keys print D, but it is wrong.

### Part 2 · Home Investigation & 30-Mark Written Paper (Lesson 02)

**Part A · Home Investigation:** *Wall or no wall? Starch or no starch?*

You will model the job of the cell wall and test where plant and animal tissues store food, using items found in most Nigerian homes.

*You need:* two small transparent polythene bags (the "pure water" type), a small rigid plastic cup or empty tomato-paste tin with smooth edges, water, a teaspoon, tincture of iodine from the first-aid box (or povidone-iodine diluted 1 : 5 with water), a slice of raw yam, a pinch of garri, a small piece of cooked chicken or beef, and a white saucer.

*Safety:* An adult must cut the yam. Iodine stains skin and clothing and must not be swallowed. Wash your hands afterwards, and do not eat any food that has been tested.

*Steps:*
1. **Wall model.** Fill both bags with water to the same level and tie them. Leave bag 1 on the table. Push bag 2 snugly into the cup or tin. Press each bag gently with the same finger pressure and record what happens to its shape.
2. Undo bag 2 inside the cup, add three more teaspoons of water, retie it, and press again. Note whether it swells outward or holds its shape.
3. **Food-store test.** Put the yam slice, the garri and the meat on the white saucer, well apart. Add two drops of iodine to each and wait one minute. Record the colour.
4. Explain your results using the words *cellulose wall*, *cell membrane*, *starch* and *glycogen*.

*Sample results table:*

| Test | Item | Observation | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Press | Bag alone (animal-cell model) | Bulges and changes shape easily; would split if overfilled | Membrane alone is flexible and gives little support |
| Press | Bag in cup (plant-cell model) | Keeps the shape of the cup; firm when extra water added | Rigid wall resists swelling: support and turgidity |
| Iodine | Yam slice | Brown → blue-black | Starch present (plant food store) |
| Iodine | Garri (cassava) | Brown → blue-black | Starch present |
| Iodine | Cooked meat | Stays yellow-brown | No starch; animal cells store glycogen instead |

*A good answer shows:* the bag in the cup represents the plant cell (the cup is the cellulose wall and the bag is the membrane), while the bag alone represents an animal cell such as a red blood cell; the rigid wall stops the plant cell bursting when water enters, which links to Mama Ngozi's crisp ugu; plant cells store starch and animal cells store glycogen, not starch; the model is limited because a real cell wall is fully permeable (unlike a plastic cup) and the real membrane is selectively permeable; results are recorded in a ruled table with honest colours.

**Part B · Written Paper (30 marks)**

**b1 · Plant cell vs animal cell (4 marks)**
Chinedu compares a cell from an ugu leaf with a white blood cell from Dr Amaka's slide. In a table, state **four** differences between a typical plant cell and a typical animal cell.
*Model solution:*

| Feature | Plant cell | Animal cell |
|---|---|---|
| Cell wall | Cellulose cell wall present | Absent; only a cell membrane (1) |
| Chloroplasts | Present (in green cells) | Absent (1) |
| Vacuole | Large, central, permanent sap vacuole with tonoplast | Small, temporary or absent (1) |
| Centrioles | Absent (flowering plants) | Present (1) |

Accept also: stored food starch | glycogen; nucleus at the side | central; regular shape | irregular shape. Any four, 1 mark each.
*Mark scheme note:* No mark for a row in which only one column is filled, or for vague pairs such as "big | small" with no feature named. Candidates who write "plant cells have mitochondria, animal cells do not" lose the mark (both have mitochondria). Do not credit "plant cells have a membrane, animal cells do not."

**b2 · Cell wall vs cell membrane (3 marks)**
State **three** differences between the cell wall and the cell membrane, in a matched table.
*Model solution:*

| Feature | Cell wall | Cell membrane |
|---|---|---|
| Chemical nature | Cellulose | Lipoprotein (lipid and protein) (1) |
| Permeability | Fully (freely) permeable | Selectively (partially) permeable (1) |
| Nature | Rigid and non-living | Flexible and living (1) |

Accept also: found in plant cells only | found in all cells.
*Mark scheme note:* "Permeable | impermeable" scores zero: the membrane is not impermeable. Candidates often say the wall "controls what enters the cell". That is the membrane's job and is penalised.

**b3 · The mitochondrion (4 marks)**
(a) Describe the structure of a mitochondrion. (3 marks)
(b) State its function. (1 mark)
*Model solution:* (a) It is a rod-shaped or oval organelle bounded by a double membrane (1). The inner membrane is folded inward to form cristae, which give a large surface area (1). The space inside is filled with a fluid matrix containing enzymes (1). (b) It is the site of aerobic respiration, releasing energy in the form of ATP (1).
*Mark scheme note:* "Produces energy" is penalised: energy is released, not produced. "Single membrane" loses the first mark. Do not accept "grana" or "stroma" for a mitochondrion.

**b4 · Chloroplast and nucleus (4 marks)**
(a) Describe the structure of a chloroplast and state its function. (2 marks)
(b) State **two** functions of the nucleus. (2 marks)
*Model solution:* (a) It is a double-membraned organelle containing stacks of membranes called grana, which hold chlorophyll, set in a fluid stroma (1). It is the site of photosynthesis (1). (b) It controls all the activities of the cell (1). It contains the chromosomes (DNA) that carry hereditary information, and the DNA is copied there before cell division (1).
*Mark scheme note:* "Chloroplasts are found in all plant cells" is not credited: onion bulb and root cells lack them. For the nucleus, "the brain of the cell" alone is too vague to score.

**b5 · The route of a secreted protein (4 marks)**
A cell lining Chinedu's stomach releases the enzyme pepsin (a protein). Trace the route of pepsin from where it is made to where it leaves the cell, naming the organelle at each stage and what happens there.
*Model solution:* Pepsin is made (synthesised) from amino acids on ribosomes (1). The ribosomes are attached to the rough endoplasmic reticulum, which carries the protein through its channels (1). Vesicles take the protein to the Golgi body, where it is modified and packaged into secretory vesicles (1). The vesicles move to the cell membrane, fuse with it and release the enzyme out of the cell (secretion/exocytosis) (1).
*Mark scheme note:* The order must be correct. Putting the Golgi body before the ER scores at most 2. "The nucleus makes the protein" is wrong: the nucleus holds the instructions, but ribosomes make the protein. Do not credit "passes through the cell wall" because animal cells have no wall.

**b6 · Classifying organelles by membranes (3 marks)**
Sort the following into organelles with **two** membranes, **one** membrane and **no** membrane: ribosome, lysosome, nucleus, Golgi body, mitochondrion, centriole.
*Model solution:* Two membranes: nucleus, mitochondrion (1). One membrane: lysosome, Golgi body (1). No membrane: ribosome, centriole (1). Each group must be fully correct to score its mark.
*Mark scheme note:* The most common error is giving the ribosome one membrane, or giving the lysosome two. Candidates who add "cell wall" to any membrane group are penalised: the wall is not a membrane.

**b7 · Calculation: size and visibility (5 marks)**
Dr Amaka shows an electron micrograph of a liver cell taken at ×25 000.
(a) The nucleus has an image diameter of 250 mm. Calculate its actual diameter in micrometres. (2 marks)
(b) A Golgi body on the same micrograph has an image length of 30 mm. Calculate its actual length in micrometres. (1 mark)
(c) The nucleus is viewed with a school light microscope at a total magnification of ×100. Calculate the size of its image in millimetres and state whether it can be seen, given that the eye can just make out about 0.1 mm. (2 marks)
*Model solution:* (a) Actual size = image size ÷ magnification = 250 mm ÷ 25 000 = 0.01 mm (1); 0.01 mm × 1000 = 10 µm (1). (b) 30 mm ÷ 25 000 = 0.0012 mm = 1.2 µm (1). (c) Image = actual × magnification = 10 µm × 100 = 1000 µm = 1 mm (1). 1 mm is greater than 0.1 mm, so the nucleus is visible, which is why it can be labelled on a Paper 3 drawing (1).
*Mark scheme note:* Answers without units, or with the wrong unit (e.g. "10 mm"), lose the answer mark. Multiplying instead of dividing in (a) scores zero. Magnification itself has no unit, so do not write "×25 000 mm".

**b8 · Predicting organelles from function (3 marks)**
For each cell below, name the organelle you would expect to find in large numbers and give a reason.
(a) A palisade cell in an ugu leaf exposed to bright sunlight at Mile 12 Market. (1 mark)
(b) A white blood cell at LUTH that engulfs and digests bacteria. (1 mark)
(c) A sperm cell that swims using its tail (flagellum). (1 mark)
*Model solution:* (a) Chloroplasts, to absorb light for photosynthesis (1). (b) Lysosomes, whose digestive enzymes break down the engulfed bacteria (1). (c) Mitochondria (in the middle piece), to release energy (ATP) for the beating flagellum (1).
*Mark scheme note:* The organelle and the reason must both be correct for the mark. In (c), candidates who say the flagellum "arises from" the mitochondria are wrong; it arises from a basal body. The mitochondria only supply the energy. "Vacuole" for (b) is not accepted.

## Lesson 03 · Forms of Cells and Levels of Organisation

### Part 1 · 12 Interactive MCQs (`window.LESSON_QUIZ`)

1. **[WAEC-style · Remember]** At the LUTH lab, Dr Amaka shows Chinedu a green thread from a pond. Under the microscope it is an unbranched chain of identical cylindrical cells, each with ribbon-shaped chloroplasts coiled like a spring. The organism is
   - **A.** *Volvox*
   - **B.** *Chlamydomonas*
   - **C.** *Spirogyra* ✓
   - **D.** *Hydra*
   - *Explanation:* An unbranched filament of similar cells with spiral ribbon chloroplasts is the mark of *Spirogyra*, the filamentous form. *Volvox* (the main distractor) is also many-celled and green, but its cells form a hollow sphere, not a thread, and each cell has a cup-shaped chloroplast like *Chlamydomonas*.

2. **[NECO-style · Understand]** A drop of green drain water from beside Mile 12 Market contains a single-celled organism that is spindle-shaped, swims with one long flagellum, has a red eyespot near the front and contains many chloroplasts. Which organism is it, and which form of existence does it show?
   - **A.** *Euglena*; free-living single cell ✓
   - **B.** *Chlamydomonas*; free-living single cell
   - **C.** *Euglena*; colony
   - **D.** *Paramecium*; free-living single cell
   - *Explanation:* Spindle shape, a single flagellum, an eyespot and chloroplasts identify *Euglena*, which lives alone as one complete cell. *Chlamydomonas* (main distractor) is also green and free-living, but it has **two** flagella, one cup-shaped chloroplast and a cellulose wall. *Paramecium* is slipper-shaped and covered in cilia, with no chloroplasts.

3. **[WAEC-style · Understand]** Coach Danjuma insists that *Volvox* is a tissue "because it is made of many cells". The best reason for classifying *Volvox* as a colony and not a tissue is that its cells
   - **A.** are joined to one another by cytoplasmic strands
   - **B.** contain chloroplasts and make their own food
   - **C.** are arranged in a hollow sphere
   - **D.** are alike, and each carries out nearly all life processes by itself ✓
   - *Explanation:* What separates a colony from a tissue is the lack of division of labour: every *Volvox* cell looks alike and is nearly self-sufficient. In a tissue, by contrast, the jobs are shared out: each cell does one task well and could not survive on its own. Option A (main distractor) is a true feature of *Volvox*, but being linked by strands does not make the cells specialised, so it is not the reason it is a colony. B and C are true but do not separate a colony from a tissue.

4. **[JAMB-style · Analyse]** Which pair of organisms shows the colonial level and the tissue level of organisation respectively?
   - **A.** *Spirogyra* and *Chlamydomonas*
   - **B.** *Volvox* and *Hydra* ✓
   - **C.** *Hydra* and *Volvox*
   - **D.** *Paramecium* and *Hydra*
   - *Explanation:* *Volvox* is a colony (a hollow ball of similar cells); *Hydra* is at the tissue level, with two specialised cell layers, the ectoderm and endoderm. Option C (main distractor) names the right organisms in the wrong order; read "respectively" carefully. *Paramecium* is a single free-living cell, not a colony, and *Chlamydomonas* is a single cell, not a tissue.

5. **[WAEC-style · Apply]** Chinedu puts two jars of pond water rich in rotting leaves and dissolved organic matter in a dark cupboard at home. After three weeks the *Chlamydomonas* in one jar have died, but the *Euglena* in the other jar are still alive. The most likely explanation is that *Euglena*
   - **A.** photosynthesises using light stored in its eyespot
   - **B.** engulfs other organisms with pseudopodia, as *Amoeba* does
   - **C.** switches to saprophytic nutrition, absorbing dissolved organic food through its surface ✓
   - **D.** carries out holozoic nutrition through its oral groove
   - *Explanation:* In light *Euglena* is autotrophic, but in darkness its chloroplasts cannot work and it survives by **saprophytic** nutrition, absorbing dissolved organic substances. *Chlamydomonas* is only autotrophic, so it dies without light. Option D (main distractor) describes *Paramecium*: *Euglena* has no oral groove and is not described as holozoic. The eyespot detects light; it cannot store it (A), and pseudopodia belong to *Amoeba* (B).

6. **[NECO-style · Understand]** Which of the following pairs of organisms are both holozoic?
   - **A.** *Amoeba* and *Paramecium* ✓
   - **B.** *Chlamydomonas* and *Spirogyra*
   - **C.** *Euglena* in light and *Amoeba*
   - **D.** *Volvox* and *Paramecium*
   - *Explanation:* Holozoic organisms take in solid food and digest it inside the body: *Amoeba* surrounds food with pseudopodia to form a food vacuole, and *Paramecium* sweeps food into its oral groove with cilia. Option C (main distractor) fails because *Euglena* in light is autotrophic. *Chlamydomonas*, *Spirogyra* and *Volvox* all photosynthesise (autotrophic).

7. **[JAMB-style · Remember]** Which of the following shows the correct sequence of levels of organisation from the simplest to the most complex?
   - **A.** Cell → organ → tissue → system → organism
   - **B.** Tissue → cell → organ → system → organism
   - **C.** Cell → tissue → system → organ → organism
   - **D.** Cell → tissue → organ → system → organism ✓
   - *Explanation:* Cells of one kind form a tissue; tissues together form an organ; organs working together form a system; systems together make the organism. Option C (main distractor) swaps organ and system: a system (e.g. the digestive system) is **made of** organs (stomach, liver), so it must come after the organ.

8. **[JAMB-style · Analyse]** Which list is arranged in order of increasing complexity?
   - **A.** Palisade cell, chloroplast, leaf, palisade mesophyll, ugu plant, shoot system
   - **B.** Chloroplast, palisade cell, palisade mesophyll, leaf, shoot system, ugu plant ✓
   - **C.** Chloroplast, palisade mesophyll, palisade cell, shoot system, leaf, ugu plant
   - **D.** Ugu plant, shoot system, leaf, palisade mesophyll, palisade cell, chloroplast
   - *Explanation:* Chloroplast = organelle → palisade cell = cell → palisade mesophyll = tissue → leaf = organ → shoot system = system → ugu plant = organism. Option D (main distractor) is the correct ladder written backwards (decreasing complexity). Option C puts the tissue (palisade mesophyll) before the cell (palisade cell) and the system before the organ; option A starts with the cell instead of the organelle and swaps organ/tissue and organism/system.

9. **[WAEC-style · Apply]** Chinedu cuts a cube of agar with sides of 8 mm. What is its surface area to volume ratio?
   - **A.** 1.33 mm⁻¹
   - **B.** 8.0 mm⁻¹
   - **C.** 0.75 mm⁻¹ ✓
   - **D.** 48 mm⁻¹
   - *Explanation:* SA = 6L² = 6 × 8² = 6 × 64 = 384 mm². V = L³ = 8³ = 512 mm³. SA:V = SA ÷ V = 384 ÷ 512 = 0.75 mm⁻¹ (check: 6/L = 6/8 = 0.75 mm⁻¹). Option A (main distractor) is V ÷ SA = 512 ÷ 384 = 1.33, the ratio upside down. Option D is 6 × L, which multiplies instead of dividing.

10. **[JAMB-style · Apply]** Two agar cubes have sides of 0.5 mm and 10 mm. How many times greater is the surface area to volume ratio of the smaller cube than that of the larger cube?
    - **A.** 0.05
    - **B.** 400
    - **C.** 5
    - **D.** 20 ✓
    - *Explanation:* Small cube: SA:V = 6/L = 6/0.5 = 12 mm⁻¹ (SA = 6 × 0.5² = 1.5 mm², V = 0.5³ = 0.125 mm³, 1.5 ÷ 0.125 = 12 mm⁻¹). Large cube: SA:V = 6/L = 6/10 = 0.6 mm⁻¹ (SA = 600 mm², V = 1000 mm³). Comparison = 12 ÷ 0.6 = 20 times. Because SA:V = 6/L, the ratio scales by the side ratio, 10 ÷ 0.5 = 20. Option B (main distractor) is (10 ÷ 0.5)² = 400, which compares surface areas only, not SA:V. Option A is the comparison written the wrong way round (0.6 ÷ 12).

11. **[WAEC Practical · Analyse]** In the cube bench, a yam cube soaked in dye records a surface area to volume ratio of 0.4 mm⁻¹. Using SA:V = 6/L, what is the length of one side of the cube?
    - **A.** 15 mm ✓
    - **B.** 2.4 mm
    - **C.** 0.067 mm
    - **D.** 37.5 mm
    - *Explanation:* SA:V = 6/L, so L = 6 ÷ SA:V = 6 ÷ 0.4 mm⁻¹ = 15 mm. Check: SA = 6 × 15² = 1350 mm², V = 15³ = 3375 mm³, 1350 ÷ 3375 = 0.4 mm⁻¹. Option B (main distractor) multiplies 6 × 0.4 = 2.4 instead of dividing. Option C is 0.4 ÷ 6, the formula inverted.

12. **[NECO-style · Understand]** A goat needs a heart and blood vessels to supply its body with oxygen, but a *Paramecium* does not. The main reason is that the goat
    - **A.** has more mitochondria in each of its cells than *Paramecium*
    - **B.** has a small surface area to volume ratio, so diffusion alone is too slow to reach cells deep inside its body ✓
    - **C.** lives on land, where there is less oxygen than in water
    - **D.** has a large surface area to volume ratio, so it loses oxygen too quickly
    - *Explanation:* As an organism gets bigger, its volume grows faster than its surface, so SA:V falls. Most goat cells are far from the body surface and diffusion over such distances is far too slow, so a transport system (heart, blood, vessels) is needed. *Paramecium*'s large SA:V and tiny size mean diffusion across its membrane is enough. Option D (main distractor) has the ratio the wrong way round: a large animal has a **small** SA:V. Air actually holds more oxygen than water, so C is false.

### Part 2 · Home Investigation & 30-Mark Written Paper (Lesson 03)

**Part A · Home Investigation:** *Why does a big piece of yam stay pale inside?* Modelling surface area to volume ratio with yam and zobo.

*Materials:* one piece of raw white yam (or Irish potato), a ruler marked in mm, a table knife and chopping board (an adult should do or supervise all cutting), a bowl of strong cooled zobo (hibiscus) drink as the dye, a spoon, a clock or phone timer, and a clean plate.

*Steps:*
1. With an adult's help, cut three cubes of yam with sides of 1.5 cm, 2.5 cm and 3.5 cm. Measure each side with the ruler and trim until all edges are equal.
2. Calculate SA = 6L², V = L³ and SA:V for each cube before starting, and record them.
3. Put all three cubes into the zobo at the same time, so that each is fully covered. Leave them for 30 minutes.
4. Lift the cubes out with the spoon, rinse them quickly in clean water and pat dry.
5. With the adult, cut each cube through the middle. Measure the side of the pale (undyed) square in the centre and estimate what fraction of the cut face is still pale.
6. Wash your hands, throw the yam away (do not eat it) and clean the board.

*Sample results table:*

| Side L (cm) | SA = 6L² (cm²) | V = L³ (cm³) | SA:V (cm⁻¹) | Dye depth from each face (cm) | Side of pale centre (cm) | Pale area of cut face (approx.) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.5 | 13.5 | 3.375 | 4.0 | 0.3 | 0.9 | about 36% |
| 2.5 | 37.5 | 15.625 | 2.4 | 0.3 | 1.9 | about 58% |
| 3.5 | 73.5 | 42.875 | 1.7 | 0.3 | 2.9 | about 69% |

*What a good answer shows:* (1) correct SA, V and SA:V with units (cm², cm³, cm⁻¹), worked from 6L², L³ and 6/L; (2) the dye travels about the same distance into every cube in the same time, so the **larger** cube, with the **smaller** SA:V, keeps a larger pale centre; (3) the link to living things: an *Amoeba* or *Paramecium* is like the smallest cube, with every part close to the surface, so diffusion is enough, while Chinedu's body is like a huge cube whose inner cells diffusion could never reach in time, so he needs blood, a heart and lungs; (4) a fair-test statement (same dye, same time, cubes added together) and one source of error (cubes not perfectly cubic, or the pale edge hard to judge).

**Part B · Written Paper (30 marks)**

**b1 · Levels of organisation (4 marks)**
(a) Arrange the following levels in order from the simplest to the most complex: organ, organism, cell, system, tissue, organelle. (b) Give one named example of each of the following: a free-living organism that exists as a single cell; a tissue; an organ.
*Model solution:* (a) Organelle → cell → tissue → organ → system → organism (1). (b) Single-celled organism: *Euglena*, *Paramecium*, *Amoeba* or *Chlamydomonas* (1); tissue: epithelium, blood, or the two cell layers of *Hydra* (1); organ: onion bulb, heart, leaf or kidney (1).
*Mark scheme note:* The mark in (a) is only for a fully correct order; candidates often put "system" before "organ". *Volvox* is **not** accepted as a tissue. "Onion cell" is a cell, not an organ; the onion **bulb** is the organ.

**b2 · Colony, filament or tissue? (4 marks)**
From a pond survey, Dr Amaka describes three organisms without naming them.
- Organism P: a ball of identical green cells; each cell is able to feed and move on its own.
- Organism Q: an unbranched thread of identical cells joined end to end.
- Organism R: a small two-layered animal whose cells do different jobs.

(a) Classify each of P, Q and R as a colony, a filament or a tissue-level organism, justifying each choice from the description. (3 marks) (b) State the feature that separates the tissue level from the other two. (1 mark)
*Model solution:* (a) P is a colony: its cells are all alike and grouped in a ball, and each one can feed and move by itself, so the cells hardly share out the work (1). Q is a filament: its identical cells are joined end to end in a single unbranched chain, again with no specialised cells (1). R is at the tissue level: its cells are arranged in two layers and do different jobs, so they are specialised (1). (b) Division of labour: tissue cells are specialised and depend on one another, so they cannot live apart, whereas the cells of a colony or filament are alike and each is nearly self-sufficient (1).
*Mark scheme note:* A classification with no justification from the description scores zero for that organism. "It has many cells" is not a justification, because all three are many-celled. Calling Q a tissue "because the cells are joined" is penalised: being joined does not make cells specialised. Correctly naming the organisms (*Volvox*, *Spirogyra*, *Hydra*) is welcome but not required and earns no extra mark.

**b3 · Nutrition as a property of cells (4 marks)**
(a) Explain the terms *autotrophic* and *holozoic* nutrition, naming one organism from the pond survey for each. (b) Chinedu kept one jar of *Euglena* and one jar of *Chlamydomonas*, both in pond water rich in rotting leaves and dissolved organic matter. On a sunny windowsill both survived; after three weeks in a dark cupboard only the *Euglena* were alive. Explain how each organism obtains food in light and in darkness, and hence why only one survived.
*Model solution:* (a) Autotrophic: the organism makes its own food from simple substances (carbon dioxide and water) using light energy trapped by chlorophyll, e.g. *Chlamydomonas* or *Spirogyra* (1). Holozoic: the organism takes in solid food, then digests and absorbs it, e.g. *Amoeba* (by pseudopodia, forming a food vacuole) or *Paramecium* (through the oral groove) (1). (b) In light both organisms photosynthesise with their chloroplasts, so both are autotrophic and survive on the windowsill (1). In darkness the chloroplasts cannot make food: *Euglena* switches to absorbing the dissolved organic substances in the water through its surface (saprophytic nutrition) and lives, but *Chlamydomonas* can feed only autotrophically, so it starves and dies (1).
*Mark scheme note:* A definition without a correct example loses the mark in (a). Candidates often call *Euglena* "holozoic in darkness"; the accepted term is **saprophytic**. "Feeds on dead things" without "absorbs dissolved organic matter" is too vague.

**b4 · The price of being complex (4 marks)**
State two advantages and two disadvantages to an organism such as Chinedu of being built from many specialised cells rather than one cell.
*Model solution:* Advantages (any two): division of labour, so each job is done more efficiently by specialised cells (1); the organism can grow to a large size (1); it can live in a wider variety of habitats, including dry land (1). Disadvantages (any two): specialised cells cannot survive on their own (1); damage to one organ or system can affect the whole body (1); a transport system is needed because the SA:V is small (1); more food is needed to keep the many cells alive (1). Maximum 2 + 2.
*Mark scheme note:* Two advantages and two disadvantages are each capped at 2 marks; extra points on one side do not make up for a missing side. "It is bigger" must be stated as an advantage (larger size), not left as a bare fact.

**b5 · Surface area to volume ratio calculation (5 marks)**
Dr Amaka models a *Paramecium* as a cube of side 0.2 mm and a young catfish from a Lagos fish pond as a cube of side 40 mm. (a) Calculate the surface area, volume and SA:V of the *Paramecium* model. (b) Calculate the SA:V of the catfish model. (c) How many times greater is the SA:V of the *Paramecium* than that of the catfish?
*Model solution:* (a) SA = 6L² = 6 × (0.2)² = 6 × 0.04 = 0.24 mm² (1). V = L³ = (0.2)³ = 0.008 mm³ (1). SA:V = 0.24 ÷ 0.008 = 30 mm⁻¹ (check: 6/0.2 = 30) (1). (b) SA:V = 6/L = 6/40 = 0.15 mm⁻¹ (or SA = 9600 mm², V = 64 000 mm³, 9600 ÷ 64 000 = 0.15 mm⁻¹) (1). (c) 30 ÷ 0.15 = 200 times greater (1).
*Mark scheme note:* Units are required: mm², mm³ and mm⁻¹. Writing 0.2² as 0.4 (doubling instead of squaring) is the most common error. Dividing V by SA gives the reciprocal and scores zero for that step. Allow error carried forward in (c).

**b6 · Why the catfish needs blood (3 marks)**
Using your answers to b5, explain why the *Paramecium* can rely on diffusion alone, but the catfish needs gills, a heart and blood vessels.
*Model solution:* The *Paramecium* has a large SA:V (30 mm⁻¹) and every part of its cytoplasm is very close to its surface, so diffusion across the membrane supplies oxygen and removes waste fast enough (1). The catfish has a small SA:V (0.15 mm⁻¹); its surface is too small for its volume and most cells lie far from the surface, so diffusion would be far too slow (1). It therefore needs a specialised exchange surface (gills) and a transport system (heart, blood and vessels) to carry materials to and from every cell (1).
*Mark scheme note:* The answer must use the calculated values or say "large / small SA:V". Saying "the fish is big" without linking size to SA:V and diffusion distance scores at most 1. Do not credit "Paramecium does not need oxygen".

**b7 · Features to draw for named specimens (4 marks)**
In a WAEC Paper 3 practical, specimen P (*Paramecium*) and specimen Q (*Euglena*) are provided on prepared slides. State two features you would show and label in a drawing of each specimen that help to identify it.
*Model solution:* *Paramecium* (any two): slipper shape with cilia around the edge (1); oral groove (1); macronucleus and micronucleus (1); contractile vacuole or food vacuoles (1). Maximum 2. *Euglena* (any two): spindle shape with one long flagellum (1); eyespot (stigma) near the front (1); chloroplasts (1); gullet/reservoir or contractile vacuole or nucleus (1). Maximum 2.
*Mark scheme note:* Features must be ones that identify the specimen: "nucleus" or "cytoplasm" alone are found in both and score only as a fourth choice for *Euglena*. Do not credit "flagella" (plural) for *Euglena*, or "pseudopodia" for *Paramecium*. In the drawing itself, examiners still expect a title, magnification, ruled horizontal label lines and no shading.

**b8 · Precautions in the pond-water survey (2 marks)**
State two precautions Chinedu took when collecting and examining the drain water near Mile 12 Market under the microscope.
*Model solution:* (any two) He wore gloves and washed his hands with soap after handling the drain water (1). He lowered the cover slip at an angle of about 45° to avoid trapping air bubbles (1). He focused first with the low-power objective before turning to high power (1). He kept the microscope out of direct sunlight / did not use the mirror to look at the sun (1). Maximum 2.
*Mark scheme note:* Precautions must be written in the **past tense** ("I washed my hands", not "wash your hands"). General statements such as "I was careful" or "I avoided errors" score zero.

## Lesson 04 · The Cell and Its Environment: Diffusion, Osmosis and Active Transport

### Part 1 · 12 Interactive MCQs (`window.LESSON_QUIZ`)

1. **[WAEC-style · Remember]** Which of the following best describes **active transport**?
   - **A.** The overall spreading of particles from where they are crowded to where they are fewer, until they are evenly spread
   - **B.** The passage of water molecules across a selectively permeable membrane, from the side where water is more plentiful (dilute solution) to the side where it is less plentiful (concentrated solution)
   - **C.** The pumping of molecules or ions by a cell from where they are scarce to where they are already plentiful, which costs energy (ATP) from respiration ✓
   - **D.** Movement of dissolved substances through the fully permeable cellulose cell wall without a membrane
   - *Explanation:* Active transport moves substances **against** their concentration gradient (from low to high), so the cell must spend energy as ATP from respiration. Option A is diffusion and option B is osmosis; both are passive and use no ATP. Option D is wrong because the cellulose wall does not control entry; the selectively permeable membrane does.

2. **[NECO-style · Understand]** Which of these is an example of **active transport** in a living organism?
   - **A.** Root hair cells of a maize plant taking in nitrate ions from soil water that is more dilute than the cell sap ✓
   - **B.** Carbon dioxide passing out of a red blood cell into the air spaces of the lungs
   - **C.** Water entering the root hair of a cassava plant from wet soil
   - **D.** The smell of fried akara spreading across a kitchen in Ibadan
   - *Explanation:* The ions are moving from the dilute soil water (low concentration) into the more concentrated cell sap (high concentration), which is against the gradient, so energy from respiration is needed. Options B and D are diffusion (higher to lower concentration), and option C is osmosis (water moving into the more concentrated cell sap). The commonest wrong choice is C, because it is also "uptake by a root", but water uptake is passive.

3. **[JAMB-style · Analyse]** At a research farm in Ibadan, roots of tomato seedlings took up potassium ions from a solution that was more dilute than their cell sap. When a chemical that stops respiration was added, potassium uptake stopped almost at once, but water uptake continued. The best conclusion is that
   - **A.** potassium ions enter the root cells by osmosis
   - **B.** the chemical made the cell membranes fully permeable
   - **C.** water uptake also needs energy from the mitochondria
   - **D.** potassium uptake is by active transport, which depends on ATP from respiration, while water enters passively by osmosis ✓
   - *Explanation:* Uptake against a gradient that stops when respiration is blocked is the classic evidence for active transport: no respiration means no ATP, and no ATP means no pumping. Water uptake carried on, so it does not need ATP; it is osmosis. Option A is wrong because osmosis refers only to water molecules, and option C is contradicted by the data (water uptake did not stop).

4. **[WAEC-style · Understand]** At the LUTH teaching lab, Dr Amaka places a drop of human blood in a 3% sodium chloride solution and views it under the microscope after ten minutes. The red blood cells will appear
   - **A.** swollen and then burst, because water has entered them
   - **B.** shrunken with crinkled, wrinkled edges (crenation), because water has left them by osmosis ✓
   - **C.** plasmolysed, with the membrane pulled away from the cell wall
   - **D.** unchanged, because 3% salt is isotonic with blood
   - *Explanation:* 3% NaCl is far more concentrated than blood plasma (which is isotonic with 0.9% NaCl), so the solution is **hypertonic**. Water leaves the red cells by osmosis and they shrink and crenate. Option C is the main trap: "plasmolysis" applies to plant cells, where the protoplast pulls away from a cell wall; a red cell has no wall, so the correct term is crenation. Option A describes a hypotonic solution and option D confuses 3% with 0.9%.

5. **[WAEC Practical · Analyse]** Chinedu adds a drop of blood to each of three test tubes containing salt solutions P, Q and R. Under the microscope: in P the red cells have burst (haemolysis); in Q they keep their normal disc shape; in R they are shrunken and crenated. Which arrangement shows the solutions in order of **increasing** salt concentration?
   - **A.** R, Q, P
   - **B.** Q, P, R
   - **C.** P, R, Q
   - **D.** P, Q, R ✓
   - *Explanation:* Bursting in P means water rushed in, so P is the most dilute (hypotonic). Q causes no change, so it is isotonic. Shrinking in R means water left the cells, so R is the most concentrated (hypertonic). Increasing concentration is therefore P → Q → R. Option A is the same order reversed, a common slip by candidates who think "burst" means "too much salt".

6. **[WAEC-style · Apply]** A yam cube with an initial mass of 4.00 g was left in distilled water for 40 minutes. Its final mass was 4.62 g. What is the percentage change in mass?
   - **A.** +15.5% ✓
   - **B.** +13.4%
   - **C.** +0.62%
   - **D.** −15.5%
   - *Explanation:* $\%\ \text{change} = \dfrac{\text{final} - \text{initial}}{\text{initial}} \times 100 = \dfrac{4.62 - 4.00}{4.00} \times 100 = \dfrac{0.62}{4.00} \times 100 = +15.5\%$. The cube gained mass because water entered the cells by osmosis from the hypotonic distilled water. Option B divides by the **final** mass ($0.62 \div 4.62 \times 100 = 13.4\%$), which is the most common error; the formula always divides by the **initial** mass. Option C forgets to divide by the initial mass, and D has the wrong sign.

7. **[NECO-style · Apply]** A potato strip from Jos with an initial mass of 3.20 g showed a **−7.5%** change in mass after 30 minutes in a sucrose solution. What was its final mass?
   - **A.** 3.44 g
   - **B.** 2.96 g ✓
   - **C.** 3.13 g
   - **D.** 2.45 g
   - *Explanation:* $\text{Change in mass} = \dfrac{\%\ \text{change}}{100} \times \text{initial} = \dfrac{-7.5}{100} \times 3.20 = -0.24\text{ g}$. $\text{Final mass} = \text{initial} + \text{change} = 3.20 - 0.24 = 2.96\text{ g}$. The strip lost mass, so the sucrose solution was hypertonic to the cell sap. Option A adds the change instead of subtracting it, option C subtracts 0.075 g instead of 7.5% of 3.20 g, and option D subtracts 0.75 g.

8. **[WAEC Practical · Analyse]** In a potato-strip experiment, a student forgot to blot the strip taken from the 0.0 mol dm⁻³ tube (distilled water) before its final weighing, although all the other strips were blotted properly. How would this error affect the result for that tube?
   - **A.** The % gain in mass would be recorded as smaller than its true value
   - **B.** The % change would become negative
   - **C.** It would have no effect, because the extra water is outside the cells
   - **D.** The % gain in mass would be recorded as larger than its true value ✓
   - *Explanation:* Water clinging to the surface of an unblotted strip is weighed along with the strip, so the final mass is too high. That makes $(\text{final} - \text{initial})$ too large, and the calculated % gain is larger than the true gain from osmosis. Option C is the trap: the water is outside the cells, but the balance still weighs it, which is exactly why strips are blotted in the same way before every weighing.

9. **[WAEC Practical · Apply]** Cocoyam discs were placed in sucrose solutions. Part of the results is shown below.

   | Concentration (mol dm⁻³) | 0.15 | 0.25 | 0.35 | 0.45 |
   |---|---|---|---|---|
   | % change in mass | +6.2 | +3.0 | −2.0 | −6.4 |

10. **[JAMB-style · Analyse]** A graph of % change in mass (y-axis) against sucrose concentration (x-axis) for cassava strips is a falling line that crosses the x-axis at 0.45 mol dm⁻³. What would happen to a cassava strip placed in a 0.30 mol dm⁻³ sucrose solution?
    - **A.** It would lose mass, because the solution is hypertonic to the cell sap
    - **B.** Its mass would not change, because 0.30 mol dm⁻³ is isotonic with the cell sap
    - **C.** It would gain mass and its cells would become turgid, because the solution is hypotonic to the cell sap ✓
    - **D.** It would gain mass and its cells would burst, because the solution is hypotonic to the cell sap
    - *Explanation:* The line crosses 0% at 0.45 mol dm⁻³, so the cell sap is equivalent to 0.45 mol dm⁻³. 11. **[WAEC-style · Understand]** At Mile 12 Market, Mama Ngozi's ugu leaves went limp an hour after being washed in a bowl of strong salt water. Coach Danjuma says, "The salt soaked into the leaves and made them heavy." Which is the correct explanation?
    - **A.** Salt particles entered the leaf cells by active transport and crushed the vacuoles
    - **B.** The salt water was hypertonic to the cell sap, so water left the leaf cells by osmosis; the cells lost turgor and became plasmolysed, so the leaves wilted ✓
    - **C.** The salt water was hypotonic to the cell sap, so the leaf cells absorbed too much water and burst
    - **D.** Salt dissolved the cellulose cell walls, so the leaves could no longer stand up
    - *Explanation:* Wilting is caused by **water leaving** the cells, not by salt coming in. The salt water is more concentrated than the cell sap (hypertonic), so water moves out by osmosis through the selectively permeable membrane. The vacuoles shrink, the protoplasts pull away from the walls (plasmolysis), turgor is lost and the leaf droops. Option A repeats Coach Danjuma's misconception that salt moves into the cell; option C has the direction of water movement reversed, and plant cells do not burst.

12. **[NECO-style · Apply]** Dr Amaka needs a 250 cm³ bag of 0.9% saline (0.9 g of sodium chloride in every 100 cm³ of solution) for a dehydrated patient at LUTH. What mass of sodium chloride does the bag contain?
    - **A.** 0.9 g
    - **B.** 22.5 g
    - **C.** 2.25 g ✓
    - **D.** 9.0 g
    - *Explanation:* $\text{Mass of NaCl} = \dfrac{0.9\text{ g}}{100\text{ cm}^3} \times 250\text{ cm}^3 = 0.009 \times 250 = 2.25\text{ g}$. **Part B · Written Paper (30 marks)**

**b1 · Diffusion and osmosis (4 marks)**
(a) Define diffusion and give one example of diffusion in a living organism. (b) Define osmosis and give one example of osmosis in a plant.
*Model solution:* (a) Diffusion is the overall spreading of particles from where they are crowded to where they are fewer, until they are evenly spread (1), e.g. oxygen moving from the air sacs (alveoli) of the lungs into red blood cells, or carbon dioxide moving out of a respiring cheek cell (1). (b) Osmosis is the passage of water molecules across a selectively permeable membrane, from the side where water is more plentiful (dilute solution) to the side where it is less plentiful (concentrated solution) (1), e.g. water entering a root hair cell from the soil water, or water leaving ugu cells placed in salty water (1).
*Mark scheme note:* Equivalent wordings that keep the key ideas are credited. For diffusion, the idea of net (overall) movement from more crowded to less crowded is needed. For osmosis, examiners withhold the mark if "water" is missing (writing "molecules move" is diffusion), if the direction is reversed, or if the membrane is not described as selectively (semi-/partially) permeable. "From high to low concentration" with no mention of what is concentrated is ambiguous for osmosis and is not credited. Examples must be from living things for the example marks.

**b2 · Active transport (3 marks)**
Define active transport, give one example in a living organism, and explain why active transport stops when the cells are deprived of oxygen.
*Model solution:* Active transport is the pumping of molecules or ions by a cell from where they are scarce to where they are already plentiful, which costs energy (ATP) from respiration (1). Example: root hair cells absorbing mineral ions (e.g. nitrate or potassium) from dilute soil water, or cells of the small intestine taking up glucose against its gradient (1). Without oxygen, aerobic respiration in the mitochondria stops, so no (or very little) ATP is produced to supply the energy needed for the pumping (1).
*Mark scheme note:* Equivalent wordings that keep the key ideas are credited. The direction (from scarce to plentiful, i.e. against the concentration gradient) and the use of energy/ATP are both needed for the definition mark. Candidates often give water uptake by roots as an example of active transport, which scores zero (it is osmosis). "Oxygen is needed for energy" without linking to respiration or ATP gets no credit.

**b3 · Cells in three kinds of solution (6 marks)**
Copy and complete the table to describe what happens to a plant cell and to a human red blood cell when each is placed in the solutions shown.

| Solution | Plant cell (e.g. onion or ugu cell) | Red blood cell |
|---|---|---|
| Hypotonic (e.g. distilled water) | | |
| Isotonic | | |
| Hypertonic (e.g. strong salt solution) | | |

*Model solution:*

| Solution | Plant cell | Red blood cell |
|---|---|---|
| Hypotonic | water enters by osmosis; vacuole swells; cell becomes **turgid** but does not burst (1) | water enters; cell swells and **bursts (haemolysis)** (1) |
| Isotonic | no net water movement; cell stays **normal / flaccid** (1) | no net water movement; cell keeps its **normal** biconcave shape (1) |
| Hypertonic | water leaves; vacuole shrinks; protoplast pulls away from the cell wall: **plasmolysed** (1) | water leaves; cell shrinks and becomes wrinkled: **crenation** (1) |

*Mark scheme note:* One mark per correctly completed box. Common errors penalised: writing "plasmolysis" for a red cell (it has no wall to pull away from), writing "bursts" for a plant cell in water, and swapping hypotonic and hypertonic. The technical term alone is credited only if it is correct for that cell type; a description without the term is accepted if it is accurate.

**b4 · Interpreting Chinedu's sachet-water test (4 marks)**
Chinedu put a drop of goat blood and a piece of ugu leaf peel in sachet water. After 15 min the blood looked clear red and no whole cells were seen, while the ugu cells looked swollen and firm. Explain each observation. (4 marks)
*Model solution:* Sachet water is almost pure water, so it is hypotonic to the contents of both the goat's red cells and the ugu cells; water therefore moved into both kinds of cell by osmosis across their selectively permeable membranes (1). The red cells have no cell wall, only a thin, flexible membrane, so they swelled until the membranes tore (haemolysis); their red haemoglobin spread out into the water, which is why the liquid looked clear red and no whole cells could be found (1). Each ugu cell is enclosed by a rigid cellulose cell wall (1). As water entered, the vacuole enlarged and pressed the cytoplasm outward against the wall, which pushed back and stopped further entry, so the cells became turgid (swollen and firm) without bursting (1).
*Mark scheme note:* The answer must explain **both** observations: the "clear red, no whole cells" result must be linked to bursting and the release of haemoglobin, not to the cells "dissolving" or "becoming invisible". Candidates lose marks for saying no water entered the ugu cells (it did), for saying the ugu cell membrane is "stronger", or for giving the wall as the structure that controls water entry (the membrane controls entry; the wall resists swelling). The word "cellulose" or "rigid/strong" is needed for the third mark, and "turgid" (or swollen and firm because the wall resists) for the fourth.

**b5 · Potato-strip practical analysis (6 marks)**
At a school in Jos, Chinedu's cousin Amina cut six Irish potato strips with the same cork borer, blotted and weighed each one, and placed each in a different sucrose solution for 45 minutes. She then blotted and re-weighed them.

| Sucrose concentration (mol dm⁻³) | Initial mass (g) | Final mass (g) | Change in mass (g) | % change in mass |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.00 | 3.10 | 3.52 | +0.42 | ? |
| 0.15 | 2.95 | 3.13 | +0.18 | +6.1 |
| 0.30 | 3.05 | 3.00 | −0.05 | ? |
| 0.45 | 3.00 | 2.79 | −0.21 | −7.0 |
| 0.60 | 2.90 | 2.58 | −0.32 | −11.0 |
| 0.75 | 3.15 | 2.71 | −0.44 | ? |

(a) Calculate the three missing percentage changes, giving each to 1 decimal place. (3 marks) (b) Using a graph of % change against concentration, or by interpolation, estimate the concentration of sucrose that is isotonic with the potato cell sap. Show your working. (2 marks) (c) Explain the result for the 0.75 mol dm⁻³ strip. (1 mark)
*Model solution:* (a) $0.00$: $\dfrac{3.52 - 3.10}{3.10} \times 100 = \dfrac{0.42}{3.10} \times 100 = +13.5\%$ (1). $0.30$: $\dfrac{3.00 - 3.05}{3.05} \times 100 = \dfrac{-0.05}{3.05} \times 100 = -1.6\%$ (1). $0.75$: $\dfrac{2.71 - 3.15}{3.15} \times 100 = \dfrac{-0.44}{3.15} \times 100 = -14.0\%$ (1). (b) % change is zero between 0.15 (+6.1) and 0.30 (−1.6) mol dm⁻³; $\text{fraction} = \dfrac{6.1}{6.1 + 1.6} = \dfrac{6.1}{7.7} = 0.79$ (1); $\text{isotonic concentration} = 0.15 + 0.79 \times 0.15 = 0.15 + 0.12 = 0.27\text{ mol dm}^{-3}$ (accept 0.26–0.28 read from a graph) (1). (c) The 0.75 mol dm⁻³ solution was hypertonic to the cell sap, so water left the potato cells by osmosis; the cells lost water, became flaccid and then plasmolysed, and the strip lost mass (1).
*Mark scheme note:* Percentages without a sign, or calculated by dividing by the final mass (e.g. 0.42 ÷ 3.52), score zero for that row. In (b), quoting 0.30 mol dm⁻³ "because it is the first loss" does not earn the marks; the answer is where % change is **zero**. In (c), "sucrose entered the strip" or "the salt/sugar drew in" is a misconception and is not credited; the answer must say **water left** the cells.

**b6 · Precautions (3 marks)**
State three precautions Amina took in the experiment in b5 to make her results reliable.
*Model solution:* Any three, in the past tense: all the strips were cut with the same cork borer and trimmed to the same length (1); each strip was blotted gently in the same way before every weighing (1); all the strips were left in the solutions for the same time (45 minutes) at the same room temperature (1); the balance was zeroed (tared) before each weighing (1); each strip was fully covered by its solution (1); the tubes were labelled so the strips were not mixed up (1).
*Mark scheme note:* Precautions written in the present or future tense ("cut the strips equally", "ensure you blot") are not credited by WAEC. Vague answers such as "I was careful" or "avoided errors" score zero, and "I used a clean apparatus" is not accepted unless linked to a reason.

**b7 · The wilting ugu (2 marks)**
Explain why ugu leaves washed in a bowl of strong salt water at Mile 12 Market became limp within an hour.
*Model solution:* The salt water was more concentrated than (hypertonic to) the cell sap of the leaf cells, so water moved out of the cells by osmosis through the selectively permeable membrane (1). The vacuoles shrank and the cells lost turgor (became flaccid/plasmolysed), so they could no longer hold the leaves firm and the leaves wilted (1).
*Mark scheme note:* Answers that say "salt entered the leaves" or "salt soaked in and made them heavy" score zero. The loss of turgidity (or plasmolysis) must be linked to the loss of support for the second mark.

**b8 · The saline drip (2 marks)**
(a) Calculate the mass of sodium chloride in a 1.0 dm³ (1000 cm³) bag of 0.9% saline. (b) Explain why Dr Amaka uses 0.9% saline rather than pure water for a drip into a vein.
*Model solution:* (a) $\text{Mass} = \dfrac{0.9\text{ g}}{100\text{ cm}^3} \times 1000\text{ cm}^3 = 9.0\text{ g}$ (1). (b) 0.9% saline is isotonic with blood plasma, so red cells neither gain nor lose water and keep their normal shape; pure water would be hypotonic, so water would enter the red cells by osmosis and they would swell and burst (haemolysis) (1).
*Mark scheme note:* In (a), 0.9 g (forgetting to scale up from 100 cm³) or an answer without a unit is not credited. In (b), both the word "isotonic" (or "same concentration as blood") and the consequence of pure water (haemolysis) are needed for the mark; "water would dilute the blood" alone is not enough.

   - **C.** ×12
   - **D.** ×4.32
   - *Explanation:* $M = \dfrac{\text{drawing length}}{\text{actual length}} = \dfrac{72\ \text{mm}}{0.06\ \text{mm}} = 1200$, so $M = \times 1200$ (no unit, because mm cancel). Both lengths are already in mm. Option D is the main distractor: it comes from **multiplying** 72 × 0.06 instead of dividing. Options B and C come from slipping a decimal place.

9. **[WAEC Practical · Apply]** Chiamaka's drawing of an onion epidermal cell is 54 mm long, and she has written "Mag. ×300" under it. What is the actual length of the cell?
   - **A.** 0.018 mm
   - **B.** 1.8 mm
   - **C.** 0.18 mm ✓
   - **D.** 16 200 mm
   - *Explanation:* $\text{actual length} = \dfrac{\text{drawing length}}{M} = \dfrac{54\ \text{mm}}{300} = 0.18\ \text{mm}$ (= 180 µm). Option D is the main distractor: it multiplies the drawing length by the magnification (54 × 300) instead of dividing. Options A and B are decimal-place slips.

10. **[WAEC Practical · Analyse]** A question paper says: "Draw a piece of onion scale leaf (Specimen C), 1.2 cm long, making your drawing 8–10 cm long." Which magnification should Emeka use so that his drawing meets the size instruction?
   - **A.** ×5
   - **B.** ×6
   - **C.** ×9
   - **D.** ×7.5 ✓
   - *Explanation:* drawing length $= M \times \text{actual length}$. For ×7.5: $7.5 \times 1.2\ \text{cm} = 9.0\ \text{cm}$, which lies between 8 and 10 cm. Check the others: ×5 gives 6.0 cm and ×6 gives 7.2 cm (both too small); ×9 gives 10.8 cm (too large). Option B is the main distractor: 7.2 cm would satisfy the common 6–8 cm instruction but **not** this paper's 8–10 cm instruction, so always read the size the question asks for.

11. **[WAEC-style · Understand]** Coach Danjuma tells Chinedu to label chloroplasts on his onion epidermis drawing "because all plant cells make food". Why is this advice wrong?
   - **A.** Onion bulb scales form below the soil, where no light reaches them, so their cells never develop chloroplasts ✓
   - **B.** Iodine destroys chloroplasts during staining
   - **C.** Chloroplasts are found only in animal cells
   - **D.** Chloroplasts are present but are too small to see with any microscope
   - *Explanation:* Chloroplasts develop in plant cells that are exposed to light. The fleshy scale leaves of an onion bulb grow underground in darkness, so their cells have none, just like root cells. Option D is the main distractor: chloroplasts are large enough to be seen with a light microscope (for example in *Spirogyra* or a leaf cell); the onion cells simply do not have them. Iodine does not destroy chloroplasts (B), and animal cells never have chloroplasts (C).

12. **[WAEC-style · Remember]** Why should mitochondria NOT be labelled on a drawing of a cheek cell made from a school light-microscope slide?
   - **A.** Cheek cells do not contain mitochondria
   - **B.** On an iodine- or methylene-blue-stained slide at ×400, mitochondria are too small and unstained to be made out clearly, so they are not visible on the slide ✓
   - **C.** Methylene blue dissolves the mitochondria
   - **D.** Mitochondria are found only in plant cells
   - *Explanation:* You must label only what you can see. Keep two cases apart. Ribosomes (about 0.025 µm) are below the light microscope's resolving limit of about 0.2 µm, so no light microscope can ever show them. Mitochondria and Golgi bodies are larger (a few µm), but iodine and methylene blue do not colour them, so at ×400 they cannot be picked out; special stains or an electron microscope are needed. Option A is the main distractor: cheek cells **do** have mitochondria, as all living animal cells do; they are simply not visible. Mitochondria are found in both plant and animal cells (D is wrong), and the stain does not dissolve them (C).

### Part 2 · Home Investigation & 30-Mark Written Paper (Lesson 05)

**Part A · Home Investigation:** *Drawing to size at home.* You will practise the nine drawing rules and the magnification formula using foods from your mother's kitchen.

- **Materials:** a clean 30 cm ruler, a sharp HB pencil, an eraser, plain A4 paper, one cowpea (beans) seed, one groundnut kernel, one maize grain, one onion bulb and a hand lens (magnifying glass) if you have one.

- **Steps:**

**b5 · Explaining the practical (4 marks)**

**b6 · Magnification calculations (5 marks)**
(a) A cheek cell 0.08 mm across is drawn 64 mm across. Calculate the magnification of the drawing. (2)
