Blood Vessels, Capillary Exchange, the Lymphatic System & Disorders

How does blood climb a full metre uphill from your ankles at near-zero pressure — and how does fluid leave capillaries to nourish every cell without losing a single red blood cell?

  • 01Compare the structure and function of arteries, veins, and capillaries (wall layers tunica intima, media, externa; lumen diameter; pressure; valves; pulse).
  • 02Explain using the continuity equation (A1v1 = A2v2) why blood flows slowest in capillary beds (~0.03 cm/s) and how one-cell-thick squamous endothelium adapts capillaries for exchange.
  • 03Calculate Starling Net Filtration Pressure (NFP = Pc − πc) to explain ultrafiltration (+10 mmHg) at the arterial end and osmotic reabsorption (−10 mmHg) at the venous end.
  • 04Compare the composition of whole blood, tissue fluid, and lymph, and state the three core functions of the lymphatic system (fluid return to subclavian veins, fat absorption via lacteals, and lymphocyte defence in lymph nodes).
  • 05Explain the causes and prevention of oedema (elephantiasis / filariasis and kwashiorkor), atherosclerosis, coronary thrombosis, hypertension, stroke, and varicose veins.

3D Starling Capillary & Vessel Bench: Measuring NFP = Pc − πc

Step along five positions of a systemic capillary bed (0%, 25%, 50%, 75%, 100% from arterial to venous end). Measure outward Hydrostatic Pressure Pc (35.0 → 15.0 mmHg) against inward Colloid Osmotic Pressure πc (25.0 mmHg) to plot Net Filtration Pressure NFP = Pc − πc.

Haemodynamic Continuity Principle ($A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2$): Students often think blood flows fast in capillaries because each capillary is narrow (8 µm). In reality, the aorta (A_1 = 4.5 cm²) branches into billions of parallel capillaries whose combined total cross-sectional area ($A_2 \approx 4,500\text{ cm}^2$) is 1,000× larger! Therefore velocity drops 1,000-fold from 35 cm/s to 0.035 cm/s, giving red blood cells 1–2 full seconds to exchange gases and nutrients.

STEP 01

Test arterial end (0%)

At 0% (arteriole entrance), P_c = 35.0 mmHg exceeds π_c = 25.0 mmHg, giving NFP = +10.0 mmHg (ultrafiltration outward).

STEP 02

Locate midpoint (50%)

At 50% along the capillary, P_c = 25.0 mmHg equals π_c = 25.0 mmHg, so NFP = 0.0 mmHg (dynamic equilibrium).

STEP 03

Test venous end (100%)

At 100% (venule exit), P_c = 15.0 mmHg is less than π_c = 25.0 mmHg, giving NFP = −10.0 mmHg (osmotic reabsorption inward).

STEP 04

Test Kwashiorkor (πc = 15)

Switch to the Kwashiorkor condition to see why low plasma albumin prevents venous reabsorption and causes oedema.

Arteries vs Veins vs Capillaries, Tissue Fluid, Lymph & Disorders

Let’s master the WAEC comparison tables for blood vessels, the Starling mechanism of tissue fluid formation, the lymphatic system, and cardiovascular disorders.

1 · Arteries, Veins & Capillaries Compared (WAEC Classic Table)

The walls of arteries and veins consist of three concentric coats (tunics): (1) an outer fibrous tunica externa (adventitia) of tough collagen that prevents bursting, (2) a middle tunica media of smooth muscle and elastic fibres, and (3) an inner tunica intima (interna) of smooth squamous endothelium. Capillaries consist only of the one-cell-thick endothelium.

2 · Formation of Tissue Fluid (Ultrafiltration) & Comparison of Blood, Tissue Fluid, and Lymph

Blood inside capillaries never touches body cells directly. Instead, exchange takes place through an intermediate bathing medium called tissue fluid (interstitial fluid) formed by Starling’s forces:

  • At the Arterial End of a Capillary ($P_c = 35\text{ mmHg}$, $\pi_c = 25\text{ mmHg}$): Outward blood hydrostatic pressure ($35\text{ mmHg}$) exceeds the inward colloid osmotic pressure ($25\text{ mmHg}$) of plasma proteins by $\text{NFP} = +10\text{ mmHg}$. This positive pressure forces water, dissolved oxygen, glucose, amino acids, fatty acids, vitamins, mineral salts, and hormones out through tiny endothelial pores by ultrafiltration to form tissue fluid. Red blood cells, platelets, and large plasma proteins (albumin, fibrinogen) are too large to pass through and remain in the blood.
  • At the Venous End of a Capillary ($P_c = 15\text{ mmHg}$, $\pi_c = 25\text{ mmHg}$): Resistance along the capillary drops hydrostatic pressure to $15\text{ mmHg}$ while plasma protein osmotic pull remains $25\text{ mmHg}$ ($\text{NFP} = -10\text{ mmHg}$). Therefore, ~85% of the tissue fluid (carrying metabolic wastes such as $\text{CO}_2$ and urea) is drawn back into the venule by osmosis.
  • Drainage into the Lymphatic System (~15%): The remaining ~15% of tissue fluid (~3.0 L/day) enters blind-ended lymphatic capillaries as lymph, flows through lymph nodes (which filter bacteria and produce lymphocytes/antibodies) and valved lymphatic vessels (propelled by skeletal muscle contractions), and empties back into the blood at the left and right subclavian veins via the thoracic duct and right lymphatic duct. In the small intestine villi, specialised lymph vessels called lacteals absorb fatty acids and glycerol as milky chyle.

Worked examples, step by step

Every calculation is broken down one atomic mathematical step at a time with a plain-English explainer note underneath.

EXAMPLE 1

Starling Net Filtration Pressure (NFP) at Arterial & Venous Ends

In a human skeletal muscle capillary bed, blood hydrostatic pressure is Pc,art = 36 mmHg at the arterial end and drops to Pc,ven = 16 mmHg at the venous end, while plasma protein colloid osmotic pressure remains constant at πc = 25 mmHg. Calculate: (a) the Net Filtration Pressure (NFP) at the arterial end, and (b) the Net Filtration Pressure (NFP) at the venous end, stating the direction of fluid movement at each end.

  1. Part (a) — Subtract πc (25 mmHg) from arterial Pc (36 mmHg): NFP_art = 36 − 25 = 11 mmHg.
  2. Part (b) — Subtract πc (25 mmHg) from venous Pc (16 mmHg): NFP_ven = 16 − 25 = -9 mmHg.
(a) Arterial NFP = +11 mmHg (Ultrafiltration outward)  ·  (b) Venous NFP = −9 mmHg (Osmotic reabsorption inward)
EXAMPLE 2

Capillary Flow Velocity from the Continuity Equation (A₁v₁ = A₂v₂)

Blood leaves the left ventricle through the aorta of cross-sectional area A1 = 4.5 cm² at a mean velocity of v1 = 35 cm/s. When the systemic arteries branch into the systemic capillary beds, the combined total cross-sectional area of all open capillaries is A2 = 4,500 cm². Calculate: (a) the volume flow rate Q = A1 × v1 in cm³/s, and (b) the velocity of blood flow v2 inside the capillaries in cm/s.

  1. Part (a) — Multiply aortic area A1 by aortic velocity v1: Q = 4.5 × 35 = 157.5 cm^3/s.
  2. Part (b) — Divide volume flow rate Q by total capillary area A2: v_2 = 157.5 ÷ 4500 = 0.035 cm/s.
(a) Volume Flow Rate Q = 157.5 cm³/s  ·  (b) Capillary Blood Velocity v₂ = 0.035 cm/s (1,000× slower!)
EXAMPLE 3

Daily Tissue Fluid Ultrafiltration, Venous Reabsorption & Lymphatic Return

Across all systemic capillaries in an adult human, a total volume of Vfilt = 20.0 L of tissue fluid is formed by ultrafiltration every 24 hours. If 85% of this fluid is reabsorbed directly into the venules by osmosis, calculate: (a) the volume of fluid reabsorbed into the venules per day, and (b) the volume of lymph returned to the subclavian veins per day by the lymphatic system.

  1. Part (a) — Multiply total filtered volume (20.0 L) by 0.85 (85%): V_reabs = 20.0 × 0.85 = 17.0 L/day.
  2. Part (b) — Subtract reabsorbed volume from total filtered volume: V_lymph = 20.0 − 17.0 = 3.0 L/day.
(a) Venous Reabsorption = 17.0 L/day  ·  (b) Daily Lymphatic Return = 3.0 L/day

The big idea of Lesson 4: Thick elastic arteries deliver high-pressure blood to one-cell-thick capillaries where huge total cross-sectional area slows blood to $0.035\text{ cm/s}$. Outward hydrostatic pressure ($+10\text{ mmHg}$) filters tissue fluid (without RBCs, platelets, or albumin) at the arterial end, inward osmotic pull ($-10\text{ mmHg}$) reabsorbs 85% at the venous end, and valved veins and lymphatic vessels return blood and the remaining 15% lymph uphill to the heart!

In two sentences: state three components of whole blood that are absent from normal tissue fluid, and state the three main functions of the mammalian lymphatic system.

2 marks
Model answer unlocks after you try

Normal tissue fluid lacks red blood cells (erythrocytes), platelets (thrombocytes), and large plasma proteins such as albumin and fibrinogen because they are too large to pass through capillary endothelial pores (1). 2. (a) Explain how tissue fluid is formed at the arterial end of a capillary bed and reabsorbed at the venous end [5 marks]. (b) State two differences in composition between whole blood and tissue fluid [2 marks]. (c) State three functions of the mammalian lymphatic system [3 marks].

Solution unlocks after you try

(a) At the arterial end of a capillary, blood hydrostatic pressure (35 mmHg) exceeds the inward colloid osmotic pressure of plasma proteins (25 mmHg) by +10 mmHg, forcing water, glucose, amino acids, salts, and O2 out through endothelial pores by ultrafiltration to form tissue fluid (2.5). At the venous end, hydrostatic pressure has dropped to 15 mmHg while plasma protein osmotic pull remains 25 mmHg (−10 mmHg), drawing ~85% of the fluid (with CO2 and urea) back into the blood by osmosis (2.5).
(b) Whole blood contains red blood cells, platelets, and high concentrations of large plasma proteins (albumin, fibrinogen), whereas tissue fluid lacks all three (2).
(c) 1. Drains the remaining ~15% excess tissue fluid and leaked proteins back into the subclavian veins to prevent oedema (1). 2. Absorbs fatty acids and glycerol via lacteals in the intestinal villi (1). 3. Filters bacteria and produces lymphocytes/antibodies inside lymph nodes (1).

3. (a) Explain the physiological cause of oedema in (i) a patient infected with Wuchereria bancrofti (elephantiasis) and (ii) a child with severe kwashiorkor [4 marks]. (b) At the arterial end of a capillary, Pc = 38 mmHg and πc = 26 mmHg; at the venous end, Pc = 16 mmHg and πc = 26 mmHg. Calculate the Net Filtration Pressure at both ends [3 marks]. (c) If 24.0 L/day of fluid filters out of capillaries and 85% is reabsorbed into venules, calculate the daily volume of lymph formed [3 marks].

10 marks
Solution unlocks after you try

(a) (i) Elephantiasis: Adult filarial worms (Wuchereria bancrofti) block lymphatic vessels/nodes so the 15% excess tissue fluid cannot drain back to the blood (2). (ii) Kwashiorkor: Severe dietary protein deficiency reduces liver synthesis of plasma albumin, lowering plasma colloid osmotic pressure (π_c) so water is not reabsorbed at the venous end of capillaries (2).
(b) Arterial end: NFP_art = 38 − 26 = 12 mmHg (+12 mmHg outward) (1.5). Venous end: NFP_ven = 16 − 26 = -10 mmHg (−10 mmHg inward) (1.5).
