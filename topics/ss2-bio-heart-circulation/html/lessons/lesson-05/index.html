Practical Analysis Questions

Answer these three WAEC haematology & Paper 3 questions, then compare with the model answers.

  1. Why do mature mammalian red blood cells have a shorter lifespan (~120 days) than most nucleated body cells?
  2. Why does adding sodium citrate or potassium oxalate to freshly drawn blood in a test tube prevent the blood from clotting?
  3. How do you distinguish the left ventricle from the right ventricle on an unlabelled dissected mammalian heart specimen in WAEC Paper 3?
Model answers unlocks after you try
  1. Because mature mammalian erythrocytes lack a nucleus and ribosomes, they cannot synthesise new proteins or repair worn-out enzymes and membranes, so they wear out after ~120 days and are broken down in the liver and spleen.
  2. Citrate and oxalate ions bind (chelate) free calcium ions ($\text{Ca}^{2+}$) in the plasma; without $\text{Ca}^{2+}$, thromboplastin cannot convert prothrombin into active thrombin.
  3. The left ventricle forms the pointed apex of the heart and has a muscular wall (myocardium) that is ~3 times thicker and firmer when pinched than the thinner, crescent-shaped right ventricular wall.

Composition of Blood, 4-Step Clotting Cascade, ABO/Rhesus Groups & WAEC Paper 3

Let’s bring together the cellular and liquid components of mammalian blood, the enzymatic clotting mechanism, ABO and Rhesus transfusion genetics, and the 9 WAEC Paper 3 biological drawing rules.

1 · Composition of Mammalian Blood & Comparison of Blood Cells

An average adult human has 4.5–5.5 litres of blood (~8% of body mass, pH 7.35–7.45), consisting of:

  • Blood Plasma (~55% by volume): Pale straw-coloured alkaline fluid composed of ~90% water and ~10% dissolved substances — plasma proteins (albumin for colloid osmotic pressure $\pi_c$, globulins/antibodies for immunity, and fibrinogen + prothrombin for clotting), digested nutrients (glucose, amino acids, fatty acids, vitamins), mineral ions ($\text{Na}^+, \text{K}^+, \text{Ca}^{2+}, \text{Cl}^-, \text{HCO}_3^-$), excretory wastes (urea, uric acid, creatinine), hormones, and dissolved gases. Serum is plasma from which fibrinogen and clotting factors have been removed ($\text{Serum} = \text{Plasma} - \text{Fibrinogen}$).
  • Cellular Elements / Corpuscles (~45% by volume): Erythrocytes (red blood cells), leucocytes (white blood cells: granulocytes/phagocytes and agranulocytes/lymphocytes & monocytes), and thrombocytes (platelets).

2 · Eight Functions of Blood & The 4-Step Blood Clotting Cascade

Eight Core Functions of Mammalian Blood: (1) Oxygen transport from lung alveoli to tissues as unstable oxyhaemoglobin ($\text{Hb} + 4\text{O}_2 \rightleftharpoons \text{HbO}_8$); (2) Carbon dioxide transport from tissues to lungs — 70–85% as bicarbonate ions ($\text{HCO}_3^-$) in plasma, 10–20% as carbaminohaemoglobin, and 5–7% dissolved in plasma; (3) Transport of digested food (glucose, amino acids, vitamins) from ileum villi to the liver via the hepatic portal vein; (4) Transport of excretory wastes (urea from liver to kidneys); (5) Transport of hormones from endocrine glands to target organs; (6) Regulation of body temperature by distributing heat from liver and muscles to the skin; (7) Defence against infection via phagocytes and lymphocyte antibodies/antitoxins; and (8) Blood clotting (haemostasis) to prevent blood loss and pathogen entry.

The 4-Step Enzymatic Clotting Cascade:

  1. Step 1 — Release of Thromboplastin: When a blood vessel is cut, platelets adhere to exposed collagen fibres, rupture, and together with damaged tissue cells release the enzyme/activator thromboplastin (thrombokinase).
  2. Step 2 — Conversion of Prothrombin to Thrombin: In the presence of dissolved calcium ions ($\text{Ca}^{2+}$), thromboplastin catalyses the conversion of inactive plasma prothrombin (synthesised in the liver using Vitamin K) into the active enzyme thrombin.
  3. Step 3 — Conversion of Fibrinogen to Fibrin: Active thrombin converts soluble plasma protein fibrinogen into sticky, insoluble threads of fibrin.
  4. Step 4 — Clot Formation & Serum: The insoluble fibrin meshwork traps red blood cells and platelets to form a solid clot (thrombus) that dries into a scab, sealing the wound and leaving pale-yellow serum ($\text{Plasma} - \text{Fibrinogen}$). Inside intact vessels, smooth endothelium and the natural anticoagulant heparin prevent unwanted clotting.

4 · ABO & Rhesus Blood Groups, Genetics & WAEC Paper 3 Rules

ABO Blood Group Compatibility: Red blood cell membranes carry Antigens (agglutinogens A and B) while blood plasma contains naturally occurring Antibodies (agglutinins anti-a and anti-b). Agglutination (fatal clumping) occurs whenever donor RBC antigens react with matching antibodies in the recipient’s plasma:

  • Rhesus ($\text{Rh}$) Factor & Erythroblastosis Fetalis: Individuals with the D antigen on their RBCs are $\text{Rh}^+$; those without it are $\text{Rh}^-$. If an $\text{Rh}^-$ mother carries an $\text{Rh}^+$ fetus (fathered by an $\text{Rh}^+$ man), fetal $\text{Rh}^+$ RBCs crossing into her blood at delivery sensitise her to produce anti-D antibodies. In a second or subsequent $\text{Rh}^+$ pregnancy, maternal anti-D IgG antibodies cross the placenta and haemolyse fetal RBCs (erythroblastosis fetalis / haemolytic disease of the newborn), prevented by injecting the mother with anti-D immunoglobulin (RhoGAM) within 72 hours of birth.
  • Sickle-Cell Anaemia ($Hb^S Hb^S$), Haemophilia & Leukaemia: In sickle-cell anaemia (autosomal recessive mutation replacing glutamic acid with valine in $\beta$-globin), low oxygen tension causes RBCs to crystallise into rigid sickles that block capillaries and haemolyse early. Haemophilia is an X-linked recessive bleeding disorder lacking clotting factor VIII or IX. Leukaemia is cancerous overproduction of immature white blood cells in the bone marrow.
  • 9 Mandatory WAEC Paper 3 Heart Drawing Rules: (1) Use a sharp HB pencil with single, continuous outlines; (2) Draw large (8–12 cm long); (3) Zero shading, zero stippling, zero colouring; (4) Show accurate proportions (left ventricular wall ~3× thicker than right ventricular wall, identified externally by the diagonal coronary sulcus); (5) Use a ruler for all label guide lines; (6) Keep guide lines horizontal, parallel, and never crossing; (7) Guide lines must touch the exact structure with no arrowheads; (8) Write labels horizontally; (9) Write the full Title, View, and Magnification ($M = \text{Drawing} \div \text{Actual}$) below the drawing.

EXAMPLE 1

Haemoglobin Oxygen-Carrying Capacity in 5.0 L of Healthy vs Anaemic Blood

Given that 1.0 g of haemoglobin binds 1.34 mL of oxygen at 100% arterial saturation, calculate: (a) the oxygen-carrying capacity CO₂ per 100 mL (1 dL) of blood for a healthy student with [Hb] = 15.0 g/100 mL, (b) the total volume of oxygen carried in 5.0 L (50 dL) of the student's arterial blood, and (c) the total oxygen in 5.0 L (50 dL) for an anaemic patient with [Hb] = 8.0 g/100 mL.

  1. Part (a) — Multiply 1.34 mL O₂/g by healthy haemoglobin concentration (15.0 g/100 mL): C_O2 = 1.34 × 15.0 = 20.1 mL O2/100 mL.
  2. Part (b) — Multiply CO₂ (20.1 mL/dL) by 50 dL (5.0 L of blood): V_healthy = 20.1 × 50 = 1005 mL O2.
  3. Part (c) — Multiply 1.34 × 8.0 × 50 dL for the anaemic patient: V_anaemic = 10.72 × 50 = 536 mL O2.
(a) Healthy CO₂ = 20.1 mL O₂/100 mL  ·  (b) Healthy Total O₂ = 1,005 mL  ·  (c) Anaemic Total O₂ = 536 mL
EXAMPLE 2

Sickle-Cell Trait Genetic Cross (HbAHbS × HbAHbS)

A man and a woman who are both healthy carriers of the sickle-cell trait (genotype HbAHbS) marry. Out of 4 children, calculate: (a) the percentage probability that a child will have sickle-cell anaemia (HbSHbS), and (b) the percentage probability that a child will be a carrier (HbAHbS).

  1. Part (a) — Divide 1 homozygous HbSHbS outcome by 4 total Punnett square outcomes: P_SS = (1 ÷ 4) × 100 = 25%.
  2. Part (b) — Divide 2 heterozygous HbAHbS outcomes by 4 total outcomes: P_AS = (2 ÷ 4) × 100 = 50%.
(a) Sickle-Cell Anaemia (HbSHbS) = 25% (1/4)  ·  (b) Carrier Trait (HbAHbS) = 50% (1/2)
EXAMPLE 3

WAEC Paper 3 Mammalian Heart Biological Drawing Magnification

During a WAEC Biology Paper 3 practical examination, Amaka measures her dissected goat heart specimen as O = 80 mm (8.0 cm) in length and makes an unshaded HB-pencil drawing of length I = 120 mm (12.0 cm). Calculate the linear magnification M of her biological drawing.

  1. Divide the drawing length I (120 mm) by the actual specimen length O (80 mm): M = 120 ÷ 80 = 1.5.
Linear Magnification M = ×1.5 (written beside the title with no units)

Solution unlocks after you try

(a) Erythrocytes: Biconcave discs; no nucleus at maturity; formed in red bone marrow; transport O₂ as oxyhaemoglobin (2). Leucocytes: Amoeboid (phagocytes) or spherical (lymphocytes); nucleated (lobed or large round nucleus); formed in bone marrow and lymph nodes; engulf bacteria and secrete antibodies (2). 2. (a) Describe the four sequential steps of mammalian blood clotting, stating the exact role of platelets, thromboplastin, calcium ions (Ca²⁺), Vitamin K, prothrombin, thrombin, and fibrinogen [6 marks]. (b) Distinguish between blood plasma and blood serum, and state two reasons why blood does not clot inside healthy intact blood vessels [4 marks].

10 marks
Solution unlocks after you try

(a) 1. Damaged tissue cells and ruptured platelets at a wound release the activator thromboplastin (thrombokinase) (1.5). 2. In the presence of calcium ions (Ca²⁺), thromboplastin converts inactive plasma prothrombin (synthesised in the liver using Vitamin K) into active enzyme thrombin (1.5). 3. Thrombin catalyses the conversion of soluble plasma fibrinogen into insoluble threads of fibrin (1.5). 4. The fibrin mesh traps red blood cells to form a clot (scab) that seals the wound (1.5).
(b) Plasma contains soluble fibrinogen and clotting factors, whereas serum is plasma minus fibrinogen and clotting factors after coagulation (Serum = Plasma − Fibrinogen) (2). Blood does not clot in intact vessels because: (i) smooth endothelial lining prevents platelet adherence/rupture, and (ii) mast cells/basophils secrete the natural anticoagulant heparin (2).

3. (a) Explain why transfusing Blood Group A red blood cells into a Blood Group B patient causes agglutination, whereas Blood Group O⁻ red cells can be transfused safely [4 marks]. (b) Explain how Rhesus incompatibility causes erythroblastosis fetalis and how it is prevented clinically [3 marks]. (c) Two sickle-cell trait carriers (HbAHbS) marry; show by a genetic cross the probability that their child will have sickle-cell anaemia (HbSHbS) [3 marks].

10 marks
Solution unlocks after you try

(a) A Group B recipient has anti-A antibodies in plasma that bind specifically to Antigen A on the donor Group A RBCs, causing fatal clumping (agglutination) (2). Group O⁻ RBCs have no A, B, or Rh-D surface antigens, so anti-A and anti-B antibodies cannot clump them (2).
(b) When an Rh⁻ mother carries a first Rh⁺ fetus, fetal Rh⁺ RBCs entering her blood at birth sensitise her immune system to make anti-D IgG antibodies; in a subsequent Rh⁺ pregnancy, anti-D crosses the placenta and haemolyses fetal RBCs (2). Prevented by injecting the mother with anti-D immunoglobulin (RhoGAM) within 72 hours of delivery (1).
(c) Parental cross: Hb^A Hb^S × Hb^A Hb^S → Gametes: Hb^A, Hb^S × Hb^A, Hb^S → Offspring: 1 Hb^A Hb^A : 2 Hb^A Hb^S : 1 Hb^S Hb^S. Probability of sickle-cell anaemia (Hb^S Hb^S) = 1 ÷ 4 = 0.25 = 25% (3).
