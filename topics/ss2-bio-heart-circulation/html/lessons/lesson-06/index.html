Precautions and sources of error

Precautions (past tense): the chamber was filled from the edge of the cover slip in one smooth flow, without air bubbles · the cells were left to settle for 2 minutes before counting · cells touching the top and left lines were counted, but those touching the bottom and right lines were not · the count was repeated with a second filling and the mean taken.

Sources of error (with direction): counting cells on all four boundary lines counted some twice, so the count was too high · an air bubble or overfilled chamber changed the depth from 0.1 mm; a deeper layer held more cells, so the count was too high (a bubble taking up space made it too low) · too much diluting fluid drawn into the pipette made the blood weaker than 1 in 200, so the count was too low · counting before the cells settled missed cells that were out of focus, so the count was too low.

Practical analysis questions

Answer these five questions, then compare with the model answers.

  1. Why are red cells so much more numerous than white cells?
  2. Why does a mature red cell lack a nucleus?
  3. How can you tell a phagocyte from a lymphocyte?
  4. Why was the blood diluted?
  5. Why must cells on two of the four boundary lines be left out?
Model answers unlocks after you try
  1. Red cells carry oxygen to every cell all the time, so many are needed (about 5 million per mm³); white cells only defend, and about 7000 to 8000 per mm³ is enough.
  2. Losing the nucleus leaves more room for haemoglobin, so each cell can carry more oxygen.
  3. A phagocyte has a lobed (or bean-shaped) nucleus and grainy cytoplasm and swallows bacteria; a lymphocyte has one large round nucleus filling most of the cell and makes antibodies.
  4. Undiluted blood has far too many cells to count; they would overlap. Diluting 1 in 200 spreads them out so they can be counted, and the sum then undoes the dilution.
  5. A cell on a shared line belongs to two squares. Counting the top and left lines but not the bottom and right lines makes sure every cell is counted exactly once.

Plasma, cells and the clotting chain

1 · What blood is made of

Composition: blood is a liquid connective tissue. About 55% is plasma and about 45% is cells; an adult carries roughly 5 to 6 dm³. Plasma is a straw-coloured liquid, about nine-tenths water, carrying dissolved food (amino acids, glucose, glycerol, fatty acids), salts, hormones, urea, most of the carbon dioxide (as hydrogencarbonate), plasma proteins (albumin, fibrinogen, prothrombin, antibodies) and heat. Serum is what remains when fibrinogen and the other clotting proteins have been removed.

3 · How blood clots

thromboplastin released → prothrombin → thrombin → fibrinogen → fibrin → fibrin net traps red cells = clot   calcium ions and vitamin K help

Injured tissue and platelets release thromboplastin (thrombokinase). Helped by vitamin K and calcium ions, it turns inactive prothrombin into active thrombin. Thrombin changes dissolved fibrinogen into solid fibrin threads. The fibrin net snares red cells, and the tangle becomes a clot that later dries into a scab. Clotting stops blood loss and keeps germs out.

Why blood stays liquid inside healthy vessels: the lining is smooth, so no thromboplastin is released, and the anticoagulant heparin is present. In haemophilia, an inherited sex-linked condition, clotting fails.

4 · Functions of blood

Transport (gases, food, hormones, urea, heat) · protection against germs (engulfing them, antibodies) · sealing wounds by clotting · spreading heat to keep body temperature steady · helping keep the pH and water content steady.

4Apply · Worked examples · 15 min

Worked examples, step by step

Try each problem on paper first, then check the working.

EXAMPLE 1

Haemocytometer count

480 red cells were counted in 5 squares, each 0.2 mm × 0.2 mm × 0.1 mm deep, from blood diluted 1 in 200. Find the count per mm³ of whole blood.

  1. One square's volume: 0.2 × 0.2 × 0.1 = 0.004 mm³.
  2. Five squares: 5 × 0.004 = 0.020 mm³.
  3. Diluted count: 480 ÷ 0.020 = 24 000 cells mm⁻³.
  4. Undo the dilution: 24 000 × 200 = 4 800 000 = 4.8 × 10⁶ cells mm⁻³.
4.8 million per mm³, within the normal range of about 5 million.
EXAMPLE 2

Real size of a red cell

On a micrograph at ×2000, a red cell measures 15 mm across. Find its real diameter in µm.

  1. Actual size: actual = image ÷ M = 15 mm ÷ 2000.
  2. Divide: 15 ÷ 2000 = 0.0075 mm.
  3. Convert to µm: 0.0075 × 1000 = 7.5 µm.
Real diameter = 7.5 µm
EXAMPLE 3

Red cells in the whole body

With 5.0 × 10⁶ red cells per mm³ and 5.0 dm³ of blood, how many red cells are there?

  1. Convert the volume: 1 dm³ = 10⁶ mm³, so 5.0 dm³ = 5.0 × 10⁶ mm³.
  2. Multiply: (5.0 × 10⁶) × (5.0 × 10⁶).
  3. Result: 25 × 10¹² = 2.5 × 10¹³.
About 2.5 × 10¹³ red cells
EXAMPLE 4

Model answer: how blood clots

Explain how blood clots at a cut.

  1. Start: thromboplastin is released by platelets and damaged tissue.
  2. Thrombin: with calcium ions and vitamin K, it converts prothrombin to thrombin.
  3. Fibrin: thrombin changes dissolved fibrinogen into solid fibrin threads.
  4. Clot: the fibrin net snares red cells, giving a clot.
thromboplastin → thrombin → fibrin → net traps cells

Part A · Home investigation: counting what you cannot count, and catching what you cannot hold

Model a sample count and a fibrin net. No blood is used.

You need: a flat tray or large plate, a ruler, a pencil and a sheet of paper big enough to cover the tray, a cupful of small grains (millet, beniseed or uncooked rice), an empty plastic water bottle, scissors (adult help), sewing thread or cotton strands, a cup of water, a handful of beans soaked for an hour, and a bowl.

Safety: an adult cuts the bottle; the cut edge is sharp. Do not eat any grains or beans you have used. Mop up spilled water at once so nobody slips.

  1. The count. Rule the paper into equal squares (for example 2 cm × 2 cm). Count how many squares cover the paper. Put it on the tray.
  2. Scatter the grains as evenly as you can over the whole sheet.
  3. Pick five squares spread across the sheet (not side by side). Count the grains in each, following one rule: count grains on the top and left lines, but not those on the bottom and right lines.
  4. Find the mean per square and multiply by the total number of squares to estimate all the grains. If you have time, count every grain to check your estimate.
  5. The clot model. Cut the top third off the bottle and turn it upside down over the bowl as a funnel. Tie or tape 6 to 8 thread strands across the mouth in a criss-cross net.
  6. Pour the water and soaked beans together into the funnel. Record what passes through and what stays.
  7. Write four sentences linking the beans, the water and the threads to red cells, plasma and fibrin.

A good answer shows: the estimate uses the mean of several spread-out squares, not one square, because the grains are never perfectly even; the line rule stops grains on shared lines being counted twice (ignoring it makes the estimate too high); the estimate is compared honestly with a full count if one was made; the bean model is linked correctly (threads = fibrin, beans = red cells, water = plasma or serum); and one limit of the model is stated, for example that real fibrin forms by a chemical chain (thromboplastin → thrombin → fibrin) and is not put in place by hand, or that real cells are far too small and numerous to count without a microscope.

Part B · Written paper (30 marks)

1. Plasma and serum

3 marks

(a) State what plasma is. (1 mark) (b) State what serum is. (1 mark) (c) Give one difference between plasma and serum. (1 mark)

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) Plasma is the pale yellow liquid part of blood, mostly water, in which the blood cells float and many substances are carried dissolved (1). (b) Serum is the liquid left over after blood has clotted (1). (c) Plasma still contains fibrinogen and the other clotting proteins; serum does not, so serum cannot clot (1). Equivalent wordings that keep the key ideas are credited.

Mark scheme note: For (c) "serum has no fibrinogen" earns the mark only if it is set against plasma, which has it. Do not credit "serum has no cells" as the difference: plasma has no cells either.

3. Red cell adaptations and white cell types

5 marks

(a) State three ways in which a red blood cell is adapted to carrying oxygen. (3 marks) (b) Name two kinds of white blood cell and give the job of each. (2 marks)

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) Any three: its biconcave (dimpled disc) shape provides a big surface across which oxygen can move in and out (1); it has no nucleus, leaving more room for haemoglobin (1); it is packed with haemoglobin, which combines loosely with oxygen (1); it is small and flexible enough to squeeze through narrow capillaries (1). (b) Phagocyte: engulfs and digests bacteria (1). Lymphocyte: makes antibodies (1).

Mark scheme note: In (a) each adaptation must be linked to how it helps; a bare "biconcave" with no reason earns no mark. Carbonic anhydrase helps carbon dioxide carriage, not oxygen, so it is not credited here. In (b) the name and the job must match: "lymphocyte engulfs bacteria" scores zero.

4. How blood clots

4 marks

The rider's gash stopped bleeding before anyone stitched it. Explain how blood clots at a wound.

Model solution unlocks after you try

Damaged tissue and platelets at the wound release thromboplastin (thrombokinase) (1). With the help of calcium ions and vitamin K, thromboplastin changes inactive prothrombin into active thrombin (1). Thrombin turns soluble fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin threads (1). The fibrin threads form a net that traps red cells, making a clot that seals the wound and later dries into a scab (1).

Mark scheme note: The steps must be in the right order and with the right pairs; "prothrombin becomes fibrin" scores zero for that step. Writing "the blood dries in the air" earns nothing. Calcium ions must be named for the second mark; "minerals" alone is not enough.

5. Why blood stays liquid in healthy vessels

2 marks

Give two reasons why blood does not normally clot inside healthy blood vessels.

Model solution unlocks after you try

The inner lining of healthy vessels is smooth, so platelets are not broken and no thromboplastin is released (1). Blood contains an anticoagulant, heparin, which prevents clotting (1).

Mark scheme note: "Blood is always moving" alone is not credited. Do not accept "there is no fibrinogen in vessels": fibrinogen is always present in plasma.

6. Haemocytometer count

5 marks

In the UCH lab, a sample was diluted 1 in 200. Red cells counted in 5 haemocytometer squares were 88, 97, 90, 93 and 92. Each square is 0.2 mm × 0.2 mm and the chamber is 0.1 mm deep. (a) Calculate the number of red cells per mm³ of undiluted blood. Show your working. (4 marks) (b) Compare your answer with the typical adult value. (1 mark)

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) Volume of one square = 0.2 × 0.2 × 0.1 = 0.004 mm³ (1). Total counted = 88 + 97 + 90 + 93 + 92 = 460 cells in 5 × 0.004 = 0.020 mm³ (1). Cells per mm³ of diluted blood = 460 ÷ 0.020 = 23 000 (1). Cells per mm³ of whole blood = 23 000 × 200 = 4 600 000 = 4.6 × 10⁶ (1). (b) This is close to, and a little below, the typical value of about 5 million per mm³ (1).

Mark scheme note: Each mark is for a separate step: the volume of one square, the total volume of five squares (with the total count), the diluted count, and undoing the dilution. Candidates who divide by 200 (getting about 115) lose the last mark of (a). A final answer without a unit ("per mm³") loses the last mark of (a).

7. Practical: precautions and errors

4 marks

(a) State two precautions that were taken when filling and counting the haemocytometer. (2 marks) (b) For two sources of error, state whether each made the count too high or too low, and why. (2 marks)

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) Any two, in the past tense: the chamber was filled from the edge of the cover slip in one smooth flow so that no air bubbles were trapped (1); the cells were left to settle for 2 minutes before counting (1); cells touching the top and left lines were counted but those on the bottom and right lines were not (1); the count was repeated with a second filling and the mean was taken (1). (b) Any two: counting cells on all four boundary lines counted some cells twice, so the count was too high (1); drawing too much diluting fluid into the pipette made the blood weaker than 1 in 200, so the count was too low (1); counting before the cells had settled missed cells that were out of focus, so the count was too low (1); an overfilled chamber was deeper than 0.1 mm and held more cells, so the count was too high (1).

Mark scheme note: Precautions written as instructions ("fill the chamber slowly") lose the mark: they must be in the past tense. In (b), an error earns its mark only with both a direction and a reason; "human error" or "the count was wrong" scores zero.

8. Cell size and the rider's blood loss

3 marks

(a) On a micrograph at ×3000, a red cell measures 21 mm across. Calculate its actual diameter in micrometres. (2 marks) (b) The rider's wound has sealed. Explain why he still needs a blood transfusion. (1 mark)

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) Actual size = image size ÷ magnification = 21 mm ÷ 3000 = 0.007 mm (1). 0.007 mm × 1000 = 7.0 µm (1). (b) The clot only stops further loss; it does not replace the red cells (and plasma) he has already lost, so his blood can no longer carry enough oxygen to his tissues (1).

Mark scheme note: In (a) the second mark is for the conversion to µm; "0.007 µm" scores only the first mark. In (b) "because the clot is weak" or "because of germs" scores zero.
