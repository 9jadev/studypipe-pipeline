## Lesson 01 · Why Big Bodies Need a Transport System: Open, Closed, Single and Double Circulation

### Part 1 · 12 Interactive MCQs (`window.LESSON_QUIZ`)

1. **[WAEC-style · Understand]** At the Oja Oba screening tent, Dr Aisha points out that a pond *Amoeba* gets all its oxygen by diffusion through its surface, but Chidi could never live that way. Which reason best explains the difference?
   - **A.** Chidi's cells need no oxygen while he is resting
   - **B.** Most of Chidi's cells lie far from his body surface, and diffusion is far too slow over such distances ✓
   - **C.** Diffusion can only happen in water, and Chidi lives on land
   - **D.** Chidi's body has a smaller total surface area than the *Amoeba*
   - *Explanation:* Diffusion is quick only over tiny distances; in a thick body most cells sit many millimetres or centimetres from the skin, so a pumped supply is needed. A is false: every living cell respires all the time, so it needs oxygen even at rest. C is false: gases diffuse through air as well as water (oxygen diffuses into the alveoli from air). D is the ratio trap: Chidi's *total* surface area is millions of times larger; it is his surface area *per unit volume* (SA : V) that is tiny.

2. **[JAMB-style · Remember]** Which list names the pump, the channels and the medium of the mammalian transport system, in that order?
   - **A.** Blood; heart; blood vessels
   - **B.** Heart; blood; blood vessels
   - **C.** Blood vessels; heart; blood
   - **D.** Heart; blood vessels; blood ✓
   - *Explanation:* The heart pushes (pump), the arteries, capillaries and veins carry (channels), and blood is the fluid that holds the materials (medium). A, B and C contain the right three words but in the wrong slots: A calls blood the pump, B swaps channels and medium, and C makes the vessels the pump.

3. **[WAEC-style · Apply]** A block of agar is cut into a cube of side 0.40 cm. What is its surface area to volume ratio, expressed in mm⁻¹?
   - **A.** 1.5 mm⁻¹ ✓
   - **B.** 15 mm⁻¹
   - **C.** 150 mm⁻¹
   - **D.** 0.67 mm⁻¹
   - *Explanation:* Convert first: 0.40 cm × 10 = 4.0 mm. Then SA : V = 6L² ÷ L³ = 6 ÷ L = 6 ÷ 4.0 mm = 1.5 mm⁻¹. B (15) is 6 ÷ 0.40, which is the ratio in cm⁻¹ wrongly labelled mm⁻¹ (forgot to convert cm to mm). C (150) multiplies 15 cm⁻¹ by 10 instead of dividing; "per mm" must be a smaller number than "per cm". D (0.67) is L ÷ 6, the ratio turned upside down.

4. **[WAEC-style · Apply]** Chidi's class cuts two agar cubes, one of side 3.0 mm and one of side 12 mm. How many times larger is the SA : V of the small cube than that of the large cube?
   - **A.** 16
   - **B.** 64
   - **C.** 4 ✓
   - **D.** 1.5
   - *Explanation:* Small cube: 6 ÷ 3.0 = 2.0 mm⁻¹. Large cube: 6 ÷ 12 = 0.50 mm⁻¹. Ratio: 2.0 ÷ 0.50 = 4. SA : V varies as 1 ÷ L, so making the side four times longer makes the ratio four times smaller. A (16 = 4²) compares surface areas only, and B (64 = 4³) compares volumes only. D (2.0 − 0.50 = 1.5) subtracts the ratios when the question asks "how many times".

5. **[WAEC Practical · Analyse]** In a repeat of the cube bench, the acid moves 2.0 mm into every face of a 12 mm cube in the time allowed. What percentage of the cube's volume has turned colourless?
   - **A.** 70.4% ✓
   - **B.** 33.3%
   - **C.** 29.6%
   - **D.** 42.1%
   - *Explanation:* The pink centre loses 2.0 mm from *both* sides of every edge: 12 − (2 × 2.0) = 8.0 mm. Pink volume = 8.0³ = 512 mm³; whole cube = 12³ = 1728 mm³. Colourless = (1728 − 512) ÷ 1728 × 100 = 70.4%. B (4 ÷ 12) treats a volume as if it were a length. C (512 ÷ 1728 = 29.6%) is the share still *pink*, not the share cleared. D takes the 2.0 mm off only one side (centre 10 mm), forgetting that acid enters from opposite faces.

6. **[WAEC Practical · Analyse]** During the 8-minute wait, one cube lay flat on the floor of the beaker instead of being surrounded by acid. How did this affect its result?
   - **A.** Its % colourless was too high, because the beaker floor warmed the agar
   - **B.** Its % colourless was unaffected, because acid still reached the other five faces
   - **C.** Its % colourless was too low, because the face touching the floor was shielded from the acid ✓
   - **D.** Its % colourless was too high, because extra acid collected under the cube
   - *Explanation:* With one face against the glass, acid could enter through only five faces, so less of the cube cleared and the reading came out too low for its true SA : V. A is wrong: the beaker floor is at room temperature and does not heat the cube. B ignores the lost face: the whole SA counted in SA : V was not exposed. D is wrong: a cube resting on the floor squeezes acid *out* from under it, not in.

7. **[NECO-style · Understand]** Which statement correctly describes the circulation of a cockroach?
   - **A.** Blood stays inside vessels and moves at high pressure
   - **B.** A two-chambered heart pumps blood through gills
   - **C.** In each full circuit, blood goes through the heart two times
   - **D.** Haemolymph is pumped into a body cavity, the haemocoel, where it bathes the organs directly at low pressure ✓
   - *Explanation:* Insects have an open system: the fluid (haemolymph) leaves the vessels and pools around the organs, so pressure and flow are low. A describes a closed system (earthworm, fish, mammal). B describes a fish. C describes double circulation, found in birds and mammals, not insects.

8. **[JAMB-style · Understand]** Which of these animals has a closed, single circulation?
   - **A.** Grasshopper
   - **B.** Catfish ✓
   - **C.** Pigeon
   - **D.** Garden snail
   - *Explanation:* A catfish keeps its blood in vessels (closed) and the blood goes heart → gills → body → heart, passing the heart once per circuit (single). A grasshopper (A) and a snail (D) both have open systems. A pigeon (C) is closed but double, like Chidi.

9. **[WAEC-style · Understand]** In a tilapia, blood leaves the heart, passes through the gills and then flows on to the body. Why does this blood reach the body tissues at reduced pressure?
   - **A.** The tilapia heart has four chambers, so the blood mixes and slows down
   - **B.** Tilapia blood is haemolymph, which is too thick to flow fast
   - **C.** Pressure is lost as blood is forced through the narrow gill capillaries, and it is not pumped again before it reaches the body ✓
   - **D.** Each lap sends the blood into the heart two times, so it loses pressure there
   - *Explanation:* In single circulation one pump must push blood through two capillary networks in a row (gills, then body); friction in the fine gill capillaries drains much of the pressure. A is wrong: a fish heart has two chambers, not four. B is wrong: fish have a closed system carrying true blood; haemolymph belongs to open systems. D describes double circulation, which actually *restores* pressure.

10. **[WAEC-style · Understand]** Which pair states two advantages of the double circulation found in mammals?
    - **A.** Blood returning from the lungs is pumped again, so it reaches the body at high pressure; oxygenated and deoxygenated blood are kept apart ✓
    - **B.** Blood passes the heart only once, which saves energy; the lungs can be bypassed during rest
    - **C.** The heart needs fewer chambers; blood moves slowly so it has more time for exchange
    - **D.** Haemolymph bathes the organs directly; blood pressure stays low and safe
    - *Explanation:* Mammals pump the blood a second time after the lungs, so it leaves for the body at high pressure, and the four chambers with a septum stop oxygen-rich and oxygen-poor blood mixing. B describes single circulation, and no mammal bypasses its lungs. C is wrong: double circulation needs *more* chambers (four), and slow flow is a feature of capillaries, not an advantage of the double plan. D describes an open system.

11. **[JAMB-style · Remember]** Which of the following is NOT carried from place to place in mammalian blood?
    - **A.** Urea
    - **B.** Hormones
    - **C.** Heat
    - **D.** Starch ✓
    - *Explanation:* Starch is too large to cross the gut wall; it is digested to glucose first, and glucose is what the blood carries. Urea (A) travels from the liver to the kidneys, hormones (B) travel from glands to their target organs, and heat (C) is spread from busy organs such as muscles and the liver to the rest of the body.

12. **[WAEC-style · Analyse]** Dr Aisha models a *Paramecium* as a cube of side 0.12 mm and Baba Sule's goat as a cube of side 0.60 m. How many times larger is the *Paramecium*'s SA : V?
    - **A.** 5
    - **B.** 5000 ✓
    - **C.** 500
    - **D.** 0.0002
    - *Explanation:* Same units first: 0.60 m × 1000 = 600 mm. *Paramecium*: 6 ÷ 0.12 = 50 mm⁻¹. Goat: 6 ÷ 600 = 0.010 mm⁻¹. Ratio: 50 ÷ 0.010 = 5000. A (5) skips the conversion and divides 50 by 6 ÷ 0.60 = 10, mixing mm and m. C (500) uses 1 m = 100 mm, a ×10 slip. D divides the goat's ratio by the *Paramecium*'s, the comparison turned upside down. So the tiny protist has thousands of times more surface for each unit of volume, which is why diffusion alone serves it.

### Part 2 · Home Investigation & 30-Mark Written Paper (Lesson 01)

**Part A · Home Investigation:** *Dye cubes: how far does colour travel into cubes of different sizes?*
You need: a raw Irish potato or a piece of white yam, a kitchen knife and chopping board (an adult does the cutting), a 30 cm ruler, a cup of strong zobo drink or water with a few drops of food colouring, a spoon and a clock.
1. Ask an adult to cut three cubes from the peeled potato or yam, of side 10 mm, 25 mm and 40 mm. Check each side with the ruler.
2. Lower all three cubes into the cup of coloured liquid at the same moment, using a spoon, and make sure the liquid covers them.
3. After 30 minutes, lift all three out together, rinse them quickly in clean water and pat them dry.
4. Ask the adult to cut each cube in half through its centre. Measure the depth of the coloured edge on each cut face.
5. For each cube, calculate SA : V, the volume still uncoloured, and the percentage of the volume that has been coloured.
6. Wash your hands, the knife and the board, and throw the used cubes away; do not eat them.

Sample results table (dye moved 2.0 mm in from every face):

| Side L (mm) | SA (mm²) | V (mm³) | SA : V (mm⁻¹) | Uncoloured centre side (mm) | Uncoloured volume (mm³) | % volume coloured |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | 600 | 1000 | 0.60 | 6.0 | 216 | 78.4 |
| 25 | 3750 | 15 625 | 0.24 | 21.0 | 9261 | 40.7 |
| 40 | 9600 | 64 000 | 0.15 | 36.0 | 46 656 | 27.1 |

*What a good answer shows:* the dye travelled about the same depth into every cube, yet the share of the cube it reached fell sharply as the cube grew, because SA : V fell; the uncoloured centre side is found by taking the depth off *both* sides (L − 2d); percentages are worked from volumes, not lengths; a smooth curve is drawn if % coloured is plotted against SA : V. A strong answer links the result to the unit mystery: the uncoloured core is like the cells deep inside Chidi's leg, which diffusion from the skin could never supply in time, so a heart and blood vessels are needed. It also names one source of error with its direction, e.g. a cube cut larger than recorded has a true SA : V smaller than the value used, so its % coloured looks too low for the SA : V plotted.

**Part B · Written Paper (30 marks)**

**b1 · Definitions (3 marks)**
Define each term: (a) *transport system*; (b) *open circulation*; (c) *double circulation*.
*Model solution:* (a) A transport system is the set of organs and fluids that moves materials in bulk between the places where they are taken in or made and the places where they are used or removed. (1) (b) Open circulation is a system in which the blood (haemolymph) leaves the vessels and flows through a body cavity, the haemocoel, bathing the organs directly. (1) (c) Double circulation is a system in which blood passes through the heart twice during one complete trip round the body: once on the way to and from the lungs, and once on the way to and from the rest of the body. (1)
*Mark scheme note:* Equivalent wordings that keep the key ideas are credited. In (a) the idea of *bulk* movement between distant places is needed; "the circulation of blood" alone is too narrow. In (b) "blood flows slowly" alone scores zero; the mark is for blood *outside vessels* in a body cavity. In (c) "two circulations" without "through the heart twice per circuit" is not credited.

**b2 · Why Chidi Needs a Transport System (4 marks)**
Give four reasons why a large, active animal such as Chidi cannot depend on diffusion alone to supply its cells.
*Model solution:* (1) Its ratio of surface area to volume is small, so the body surface is too little to supply the whole volume. (1) (2) Most of its cells are far from the surface, and diffusion over long distances is far too slow. (1) (3) It is very active, so its cells use oxygen and food quickly and need a fast supply. (1) (4) Many substances are made in one organ and used or removed in another (for example urea is made in the liver but removed by the kidneys; hormones act far from the gland), so they must be carried. (1)
*Mark scheme note:* "It is big" and "its surface area is small" repeat the same idea and earn one mark only; the credited point is the *ratio*. "Diffusion is slow" without "over long distances" is too vague. Saying the body surface area is smaller than an *Amoeba*'s is wrong and is penalised.

**b3 · The Parts of the System and What They Carry (4 marks)**
(a) Name the pump, the channels and the medium of the mammalian transport system. (b) Name one waste that blood carries from the liver to the kidneys.
*Model solution:* (a) Pump: the heart. (1) Channels: the blood vessels (arteries, capillaries and veins). (1) Medium: blood (lymph is accepted as a second medium). (1) (b) Urea. (1)
*Mark scheme note:* Each part scores only in its own slot; "blood" written as the pump scores zero for that slot. In (b) "urine" is not accepted: urine is made *in* the kidney, so it is not carried there by the blood.

**b4 · Surface Area to Volume Ratio (5 marks)**
An agar cube has a side of 0.80 cm.
(a) Calculate its surface area in mm². (b) Calculate its volume in mm³. (c) Calculate its SA : V in mm⁻¹. (d) Express the SA : V in cm⁻¹. (e) A second cube has a side of 16 mm. State what happens to SA : V when the side is doubled, and give its new value.
*Model solution:* (a) Side = 0.80 cm × 10 = 8.0 mm; SA = 6L² = 6 × 8.0² = 384 mm². (1) (b) V = L³ = 8.0³ = 512 mm³. (1) (c) SA : V = 384 ÷ 512 = 0.75 mm⁻¹. (1) (d) 1 cm = 10 mm, so a ratio per cm is ten times the ratio per mm: 0.75 × 10 = 7.5 cm⁻¹ (check: 6 ÷ 0.80 = 7.5). (1) (e) It halves: 6 ÷ 16 = 0.375 mm⁻¹. (1)
*Mark scheme note:* Work carried forward is allowed from (a) to (c). Leaving the side as 0.80 in (a) and writing "3.84 mm²" loses the mark (no conversion). In (d), dividing by 10 (giving 0.075 cm⁻¹) is the commonest slip. Ratios given with no unit, or with mm² or mm³ as the unit, lose the mark in (c), (d) and (e).

**b5 · Practical: Reading the Cube Results (5 marks)**
In a school version of the cube bench, acid moved 2.5 mm into every face of an 18 mm cube in 8 minutes.
(a) Calculate the SA : V of the cube. (b) Find the side of the pink centre that remained. (c) Calculate the volume of the pink centre. (d) Calculate the percentage of the cube's volume that turned colourless. (e) State what the pink centre represents in a large animal.
*Model solution:* (a) SA : V = 6 ÷ 18 = 0.33 mm⁻¹. (1) (b) Pink centre side = 18 − (2 × 2.5) = 13 mm. (1) (c) Pink volume = 13³ = 2197 mm³. (1) (d) Whole cube = 18³ = 5832 mm³; colourless share = (5832 − 2197) ÷ 5832 × 100 = 62.3%. (1) (e) It represents cells deep inside the body, too far from the surface for diffusion to supply them in time. (1)
*Mark scheme note:* Subtracting 2.5 mm only once in (b) (centre 15.5 mm) loses (b) but later marks may be earned on error carried forward. Giving the *pink* share (37.7%) in (d) is not credited. "Dead cells" in (e) is not accepted; the idea is cells that diffusion cannot reach fast enough.

**b6 · Practical: Precautions and Errors (3 marks)**
(a) State two precautions that were taken in the agar-cube experiment. (b) A student lifted one cube out of the acid two minutes late. State and explain the effect on its percentage colourless.
*Model solution:* (a) Any two, in the past tense: all the cubes were lowered into the acid at the same moment and lifted out together (1); the acid fully covered every cube (1); each cube was checked with a ruler after cutting (1); the cubes were handled with forceps and the eyes were protected from the acid (1). (Maximum 2.) (b) Its % colourless was too high, because the acid had extra time to move further into that cube. (1)
*Mark scheme note:* Precautions written as commands ("cover the cubes") or in the future tense lose the marks. General rules such as "a lab coat was worn" are not specific to this experiment and are not accepted. In (b), the direction (too high) and the reason (extra time) must both be given for the mark.

**b7 · Comparing Circulatory Systems (4 marks)**
Using one table, give two differences between open and closed circulation and two differences between single and double circulation. Name an example animal in each column.
*Model solution:*

| Feature | Open | Closed |
|---|---|---|
| Where the blood is | Leaves vessels and pools in the haemocoel round the organs | Stays inside vessels all the way round (1) |
| Pressure and speed (with example) | Low pressure, slow flow; cockroach | High pressure, fast flow; earthworm or human (1) |

| Feature | Single | Double |
|---|---|---|
| Trips through the heart per full circuit | One | Two (1) |
| Route (with example) | Heart → gills → body → heart; tilapia | Heart → lungs → heart → body → heart; pigeon or human (1) |

*Mark scheme note:* Each mark needs a matched pair on one row *and* the examples must be correct for that row's mark; an insect named as closed, or a fish named as double, loses that row. "Chambers: two | four" is accepted as an alternative single-vs-double row. Answers in two separate paragraphs score at most half.

**b8 · Solving the First Clue (2 marks)**
Baba Sule says a heart that pumps blood only once per circuit, as in a fish, would serve Chidi just as well. State two advantages of Chidi's double circulation that prove him wrong.
*Model solution:* (1) Blood coming back from the lungs is pumped a second time, so it reaches Chidi's working muscles at high pressure and fast. (1) (2) The four-chambered heart with its septum keeps oxygen-rich and oxygen-poor blood apart, so the blood sent to his muscles is fully oxygenated. (1)
*Mark scheme note:* "It is faster" without a reason linked to the second pumping is not credited. "No mixing" must refer to oxygenated and deoxygenated blood; "blood does not mix with lymph" scores zero.

## Lesson 02 · The Heart Up Close: Chambers, Walls, Valves and the Goat-Heart Dissection

### Part 1 · 12 Interactive MCQs (`window.LESSON_QUIZ`)

1. **[WAEC-style · Remember]** Which layer of the heart is the thick band of muscle that contracts rhythmically all through life without tiring?
   - **A.** Pericardium
   - **B.** Endocardium
   - **C.** Myocardium ✓
   - **D.** Septum
   - *Explanation:* The myocardium is the middle layer of cardiac muscle that does the pumping and starts each beat itself. A (pericardium) is the two-layered bag around the heart, not muscle that contracts. B (endocardium) is the thin, smooth inner lining that lets blood slide past. D (septum) is the partition between the left and right halves, not one of the three layers of the wall.

2. **[NECO-style · Understand]** Dr Aisha tells Chidi that a thin film of fluid lies between the two layers of the pericardium. What is the main job of this fluid?
   - **A.** It lets the layers slide over each other so the beating heart does not rub against the sac ✓
   - **B.** It supplies the heart muscle with oxygen and glucose
   - **C.** It stops blood flowing backwards between the chambers
   - **D.** It sends out the impulse that starts each heartbeat
   - *Explanation:* The heart moves non-stop, so the slippery pericardial fluid reduces friction between the layers. B is the job of the coronary arteries, which bring oxygen and food to the heart muscle. C is the job of the valves. D is the job of the pacemaker, the sinoatrial node, which lies in the right atrium's wall.

3. **[JAMB-style · Understand]** Blood that has just arrived in the left atrium has come directly from
   - **A.** the body, through the venae cavae
   - **B.** the right ventricle, through the septum
   - **C.** the aorta, through the aortic semilunar valve
   - **D.** the lungs, through the pulmonary veins ✓
   - *Explanation:* The four pulmonary veins bring oxygen-rich blood back from the lungs into the left atrium. A describes the right atrium, which receives the venae cavae. B is impossible: the septum is a solid wall that keeps the two halves apart. C runs the wrong way: the aorta carries blood *away* from the left ventricle, and its valve stops blood coming back.

4. **[WAEC Practical · Apply]** At the dissection bench, Chidi measures the left ventricle wall twice (10.2 mm and 9.8 mm) and the right ventricle wall twice (3.1 mm and 3.3 mm). To two significant figures, how many times thicker is the left ventricle wall?
   - **A.** 6.8
   - **B.** 3.1 ✓
   - **C.** 0.32
   - **D.** 31
   - *Explanation:* Mean LV = (10.2 + 9.8) ÷ 2 = 10.0 mm. Mean RV = (3.1 + 3.3) ÷ 2 = 3.2 mm. Ratio = 10.0 ÷ 3.2 = 3.125 ≈ 3.1. A (10.0 − 3.2 = 6.8) gives the difference in mm, not "how many times". C (3.2 ÷ 10.0) divides the wrong way round. D is a ×10 slip. The answer fits the "about three times" rule.

5. **[WAEC-style · Understand]** Why is the wall of the left ventricle much thicker than the wall of the right ventricle?
   - **A.** The left ventricle holds a much larger volume of blood
   - **B.** The left ventricle receives deoxygenated blood, which is heavier
   - **C.** The left ventricle is on the right side of the body, which is the stronger side
   - **D.** The left ventricle must force blood round the whole body, whereas the right ventricle sends blood only as far as the lungs close by ✓
   - *Explanation:* Wall thickness matches the distance and resistance the blood must be pushed against; the trip from the left ventricle reaches every organ from scalp to toes. A is wrong: both ventricles pump the same volume per beat, or blood would pile up in one circuit. B is wrong twice: the left ventricle holds *oxygenated* blood, and how much oxygen the blood carries has nothing to do with how thick a wall must be. C is Baba Sule's mistake: the left ventricle is on the patient's *left*.

6. **[WAEC-style · Understand]** In the valve zoom, Dr Aisha cuts the chordae tendineae and the flaps flip backwards. What job do these cords normally do?
   - **A.** They hold the atrioventricular valve flaps so they cannot be forced back up into the atria as the ventricles squeeze ✓
   - **B.** They pull the semilunar valves open when the ventricles relax
   - **C.** They carry the electrical impulse from the atria to the ventricles
   - **D.** They divide the heart into a right half and a left half
   - *Explanation:* The cords tie the tricuspid and bicuspid flaps to the papillary muscles, so the high ventricular pressure shuts the flaps but cannot turn them inside out. B is wrong: the semilunar valves have no cords; they are pockets that fill and close on their own. C is the job of the conducting tissue (bundle of His and Purkinje fibres). D is the job of the septum.

7. **[WAEC-style · Understand]** Blood leaves the chamber that receives both venae cavae and enters the chamber that pumps into the pulmonary artery. Which valve does it pass?
   - **A.** Bicuspid (mitral) valve
   - **B.** Aortic semilunar valve
   - **C.** Tricuspid valve ✓
   - **D.** Pulmonary semilunar valve
   - *Explanation:* The venae cavae enter the right atrium and the pulmonary artery leaves the right ventricle, so the blood passes the right atrioventricular valve, the tricuspid ("tRi = Right"). A guards the *left* atrioventricular opening. B sits at the base of the aorta. D sits at the exit of the right ventricle into the pulmonary artery, one step later than this question asks.

8. **[NECO-style · Analyse]** In Chidi's front-view diagram, the chamber with the thickest wall and the aorta leaving it is drawn on the reader's right. Baba Sule says that proves it is the right ventricle. Which statement is correct?
   - **A.** Baba Sule is correct, because the chamber is on the reader's right
   - **B.** It is the left ventricle; a front-view diagram faces the reader, so the heart's left side appears on the reader's right ✓
   - **C.** It is the right atrium, because the atria have the thickest walls
   - **D.** The diagram is wrong, because the left ventricle must always be drawn on the reader's left
   - *Explanation:* A heart drawn facing you is like a person facing you: their left hand is on your right. The aorta and the thickest wall both identify the left ventricle. A is the sides trap the drawing rule warns about. C is wrong: the atria have the *thinnest* walls. D gets the rule backwards: in front view the left ventricle belongs on the reader's right, so Chidi's drawing is correct.

9. **[WAEC-style · Remember]** Which vessel is an artery even though it carries deoxygenated blood?
   - **A.** Pulmonary artery ✓
   - **B.** Aorta
   - **C.** Pulmonary vein
   - **D.** Superior vena cava
   - *Explanation:* Arteries are named by direction (away from the heart), not by oxygen; the pulmonary artery takes oxygen-poor blood from the right ventricle to the lungs. B (aorta) is an artery carrying *oxygenated* blood. C is a vein that carries oxygenated blood, the matching exception. D is a vein carrying deoxygenated blood, so it follows the usual pattern.

10. **[WAEC Practical · Analyse]** When measuring the left ventricle wall, Chidi placed his ruler where a papillary muscle joined the wall. How did this affect his reading?
    - **A.** It made the reading too low, because a papillary muscle is hollow
    - **B.** It had no effect, because the papillary muscle is part of the wall
    - **C.** It made the reading too low, because the cut there was slanting
    - **D.** It made the reading too high, because muscle that is not wall was added to the measurement ✓
    - *Explanation:* A papillary muscle juts inwards from the wall, so measuring through it adds extra tissue and the wall reads too thick; every wall should be measured clear of these bulges, at the same height. A is wrong: papillary muscles are solid. B is wrong: they project into the cavity and are not counted as wall thickness. C mixes in a different error (a slanting cut) and also gets its direction wrong: a slanting cut makes a wall read too *high*.

11. **[WAEC-style · Apply]** Baba Sule's goat heart is 7.5 cm long from base to apex. Chidi's drawing of it is 150 mm long. What is the magnification of the drawing?
    - **A.** ×0.5
    - **B.** ×2.0 ✓
    - **C.** ×20
    - **D.** 2.0 cm
    - *Explanation:* Same units: 150 mm = 15.0 cm. Magnification = drawing length ÷ actual length = 15.0 ÷ 7.5 = ×2.0. A divides actual by drawing, the wrong way round. C divides 150 by 7.5 without converting mm to cm, a ×10 slip. D gives the right number with a unit; magnification has no unit, so the mark is lost.

12. **[JAMB-style · Apply]** On a ×2.5 drawing of a goat heart, the right ventricle wall measures 11 mm thick. What is its actual thickness?
    - **A.** 27.5 mm
    - **B.** 44 mm
    - **C.** 4.4 mm ✓
    - **D.** 8.5 mm
    - *Explanation:* Actual size = drawing size ÷ magnification = 11 mm ÷ 2.5 = 4.4 mm. A multiplies instead of dividing, which would make the real wall bigger than the drawing of it. B is a ×10 slip. D subtracts the magnification (11 − 2.5), which has no meaning.

### Part 2 · Home Investigation & 30-Mark Written Paper (Lesson 02)

Baba Sule says, "The thick side of the heart is the right side, because right is strong." (a) Explain why the left ventricle has a thicker wall than the right ventricle. (b) Explain why both atria have thin walls.
*Model solution:* (a) The left ventricle must drive its blood, under high pressure, along the aorta and out to every organ from head to feet, a long journey with much resistance. (1) The right ventricle has a short trip to make, to the lungs lying beside the heart, so it needs less muscle. (1) (b) Each atrium needs only to squeeze its blood a few centimetres down into the ventricle directly beneath it. (1)
*Mark scheme note:* The two marks in (a) are for two separate ideas: the long whole-body route for the left, and the short lung route for the right. "The left ventricle works harder" with no reason earns nothing. "The left ventricle holds more blood" is wrong and is penalised.

**b4 · The Four Valves (4 marks)**
Name the four valves of the mammalian heart and state where each is found.
*Model solution:* Tricuspid valve: between the right atrium and the right ventricle. (1) Bicuspid (mitral) valve: between the left atrium and the left ventricle. (1) Pulmonary semilunar valve: at the base of the pulmonary artery, where it leaves the right ventricle. (1) Aortic semilunar valve: at the base of the aorta, where it leaves the left ventricle. (1)

(a) Describe the chordae tendineae and explain why they are needed. (b) State the function of the septum.
*Model solution:* (a) They are tough cords joining the flaps of the atrioventricular valves to the papillary muscles on the ventricle wall. (1) When the ventricles contract, they hold the flaps so they shut but cannot be blown back into the atria, so blood does not leak backwards. (1) (b) The septum separates the right and left halves of the heart, keeping oxygen-poor and oxygen-rich blood apart. (1)
*Mark scheme note:* The first mark in (a) is for structure and attachment, the second for function; "they prevent backflow" alone earns only the second. "The septum pumps blood" is wrong and scores zero.

(a) Calculate the mean thickness of each ventricle wall. (b) Calculate how many times thicker the left ventricle wall is. (c) Calculate the magnification of the drawing.

## Lesson 03 · The Double Circuit: Tracing Blood Around the Body

### Part 1 · 12 Interactive MCQs (`window.LESSON_QUIZ`)

1. **[WAEC-style · Understand]** On the route-tracer bench, blood has just left the right ventricle. Which three structures does it meet next, in order?
   - **A.** Aorta → body organs → venae cavae
   - **B.** Bicuspid valve → left ventricle → aorta
   - **C.** Pulmonary veins → lungs → left atrium
   - **D.** Pulmonary semilunar valve → pulmonary artery → lung capillaries ✓
   - *Explanation:* When the right ventricle squeezes, blood pushes open the pulmonary semilunar valve, enters the pulmonary artery and is then divided among the capillaries of the lungs. A starts from the *left* ventricle. B belongs to the left side of the heart, which this blood cannot reach until it has been to the lungs. C puts the pulmonary veins before the lungs; they carry blood *from* the lungs, so the order is reversed.

2. **[JAMB-style · Remember]** The pulmonary circulation begins in the _____ and ends in the _____.
   - **A.** left ventricle; right atrium
   - **B.** right ventricle; left atrium ✓
   - **C.** right atrium; left ventricle
   - **D.** left atrium; right ventricle
   - *Explanation:* The lung loop runs right ventricle → pulmonary semilunar valve → pulmonary artery → lung capillaries → pulmonary veins → left atrium. A is the start and end of the *systemic* loop. C and D pair an atrium with a ventricle on the opposite side, which never happens directly, because the septum keeps the halves apart.

3. **[WAEC-style · Understand]** As the okada rider's blood flows through the capillaries of his lungs, which change takes place in it?
   - **A.** It loses carbon dioxide and gains oxygen ✓
   - **B.** It gains carbon dioxide and loses oxygen
   - **C.** It gains glucose from the air in the lungs
   - **D.** It loses urea, which is breathed out
   - *Explanation:* In the lungs, carbon dioxide diffuses out of the blood into the air sacs and oxygen diffuses in, so dark oxygen-poor blood becomes bright oxygen-rich blood. B is what happens in the capillaries of body organs, not the lungs. C is wrong: air contains no glucose; glucose enters the blood in the gut. D is wrong: urea is removed by the kidneys, not breathed out.

4. **[NECO-style · Understand]** Why is the vessel that carries blood from the intestine to the liver called a *portal* vein?
   - **A.** It carries oxygenated blood, unlike other veins
   - **B.** It carries blood straight to the heart
   - **C.** It begins in the capillaries of the gut and ends in the capillaries of the liver ✓
   - **D.** It has semilunar valves at both ends
   - *Explanation:* A portal vein links two capillary beds instead of draining a capillary bed into the heart. A is wrong: gut blood has already given up much of its oxygen. B is the classic trap: the hepatic portal vein ends in the *liver*, not the heart. D is wrong: semilunar valves guard the exits of the two ventricles, not the hepatic portal vein.

5. **[WAEC-style · Analyse]** Mama Bisi eats a large plate of amala at noon. Half an hour later, which blood vessel holds blood with the highest concentration of glucose?
   - **A.** Hepatic vein
   - **B.** Hepatic portal vein ✓
   - **C.** Hepatic artery
   - **D.** Renal vein
   - *Explanation:* Starch from the amala is digested to glucose and absorbed into the gut capillaries, which drain into the hepatic portal vein, so its blood is the richest in glucose after a meal. A carries blood *after* the liver has stored the extra glucose as glycogen, so it holds less. C brings oxygen-rich blood from the aorta, with ordinary glucose levels. D drains the kidney, which has taken glucose out of its blood to use, not added any.

6. **[JAMB-style · Apply]** A drip is placed in a vein in the okada rider's forearm. Which named vein does the fluid enter before it reaches the superior vena cava?
   - **A.** Jugular vein
   - **B.** Hepatic vein
   - **C.** Inferior vena cava
   - **D.** Subclavian vein ✓
   - *Explanation:* Blood from the arm drains into the subclavian vein, which joins the superior vena cava. A drains the head and brain, not the arm. B drains the liver into the *inferior* vena cava. C collects blood from the trunk and legs and enters the right atrium separately from the superior vena cava.

7. **[WAEC-style · Understand]** How does the muscle of the heart wall get its oxygen and food?
   - **A.** From the coronary arteries, the first branches of the aorta ✓
   - **B.** By absorbing blood directly from inside the chambers through the endocardium
   - **C.** From the pulmonary artery as it leaves the heart
   - **D.** From the carotid arteries in the neck
   - *Explanation:* The coronary arteries leave the aorta just beyond the aortic valve and spread over the heart surface, feeding the muscle. B is the misconception the lesson corrects: the wall is too thick for diffusion from the chambers to reach all its cells, and half the heart holds oxygen-poor blood anyway. C carries deoxygenated blood to the lungs. D supply the head and brain.

8. **[WAEC-style · Analyse]** A clot blocks a branch of one coronary artery. What is the most likely result?
   - **A.** Brain cells die from lack of oxygen
   - **B.** The lungs can no longer exchange gases
   - **C.** The patch of heart muscle beyond the blockage is starved of oxygen and dies, causing a heart attack ✓
   - **D.** Blood flows backwards from the left ventricle into the right atrium
   - *Explanation:* A coronary artery supplies only heart muscle, so blocking it starves the muscle downstream of the clot. A describes a stroke, caused by a blocked vessel to the brain. B would follow damage to the lungs or their vessels, not a coronary blockage. D is impossible: the ventricle and the opposite atrium are not connected, and the problem is starved muscle, not backflow.

9. **[NECO-style · Analyse]** Carbon dioxide made by Chidi's brain cells must reach his lungs. Which route is correct?
   - **A.** Jugular vein → inferior vena cava → right atrium → tricuspid valve → right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lung capillaries
   - **B.** Carotid artery → superior vena cava → right atrium → tricuspid valve → right ventricle → pulmonary vein → lung capillaries
   - **C.** Jugular vein → superior vena cava → left atrium → bicuspid valve → left ventricle → pulmonary artery → lung capillaries
   - **D.** Jugular vein → superior vena cava → right atrium → tricuspid valve → right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lung capillaries ✓
   - *Explanation:* Blood leaves the brain in the jugular vein, reaches the right atrium by way of the superior vena cava, and the right ventricle sends it to the lungs in the pulmonary artery. A uses the inferior vena cava, which drains the lower body, not the head. B starts in the carotid *artery*, which brings blood *to* the brain, and uses the pulmonary vein, which carries blood *from* the lungs. C sends it to the left side, which receives only blood that has already been to the lungs.

10. **[WAEC-style · Analyse]** A drug is injected into a vein in a patient's leg. How many heart valves does it pass before it reaches the kidney?
    - **A.** 4 ✓
    - **B.** 2
    - **C.** 3
    - **D.** 8
    - *Explanation:* Leg vein → inferior vena cava → right atrium → **tricuspid** → right ventricle → **pulmonary semilunar** → pulmonary artery → lung capillaries → pulmonary veins → left atrium → **bicuspid** → left ventricle → **aortic semilunar** → aorta → renal artery → kidney: four valves. B counts only the left side, as if the drug skipped the lungs. C forgets one of the semilunar valves. D counts every valve twice.

11. **[JAMB-style · Apply]** Which organ is correctly matched with the vessel that brings blood to it and the vessel that drains blood from it?
    - **A.** Kidney: renal vein in, renal artery out
    - **B.** Gut: hepatic portal vein in, mesenteric artery out
    - **C.** Brain: carotid artery in, jugular vein out ✓

A, B and D all have the right pair of vessels but swap their directions: the renal *artery* enters the kidney, the mesenteric *artery* enters the gut (and the hepatic portal vein drains it), and the pulmonary *artery* enters the lungs.

12. **[WAEC-style · Analyse]** Baba Sule insists that the drip in the rider's arm "goes straight up the vein to the brain". Which statement best corrects him?
    - **A.** He is right, because the subclavian vein joins the carotid artery in the neck

    - *Explanation:* Every vein ends at the heart, and every body-to-body trip must cross the lungs, so the drip makes a full double loop before reaching the brain. A is wrong: no vein joins an artery directly; they are linked only through capillaries. C reverses the jugular vein, which drains blood *from* the brain. *Model solution:* (a) The pulmonary circulation is the loop that carries blood from the right ventricle to the lungs and back to the left atrium, where carbon dioxide is lost and oxygen gained. (1) (2) The right ventricle contracts, forcing the pulmonary semilunar valve open, and the blood is sent along the pulmonary artery. (1) (3) In the capillaries round the air sacs of the lungs, carbon dioxide leaves the blood and oxygen enters it. (1) (4) Now rich in oxygen, it returns along the pulmonary veins into the left atrium, and from there crosses the bicuspid (mitral) valve to fill the left ventricle. (1) (5) The left ventricle contracts strongly, forcing the aortic semilunar valve open; the aorta divides into arteries and arterioles that lead to capillaries in every organ, where the cells take oxygen and nutrients from the blood. (1) (6) The capillaries merge into venules and then veins, which empty into the superior and inferior venae cavae, and these return the blood to the right atrium. (1)

## Lesson 04 · The Beating Heart: Cardiac Cycle, Heart Sounds, Pacemaker, Pulse and Blood Pressure

### Part 1 · 12 Interactive MCQs (`window.LESSON_QUIZ`)

1. **[JAMB-style · Understand]** Chidi listens through Dr Aisha's stethoscope after his sprint. Baba Sule insists the "lub" is the heart muscle clapping as it squeezes. What really produces the "lub"?
   - **A.** The semilunar valves snapping shut as the ventricles relax
   - **B.** The tricuspid and bicuspid valves shutting as the ventricles begin to contract ✓
   - **C.** The atria contracting and blood striking the ventricle walls
   - **D.** Blood rushing into the aorta as the semilunar valves open
   - *Explanation:* When ventricular pressure climbs above atrial pressure, the two atrioventricular (AV) valves, the tricuspid and the bicuspid, are forced shut; that closure is the first sound, "lub". Option A describes the second sound, "dub", so it swaps the two sounds. Option C is Baba Sule's slip in another form: a heart sound comes from valves closing, not from a chamber squeezing. Option D names a valve opening, and valves make their sound when they close, not when they open.

2. **[WAEC-style · Apply]** At the screening tent, Dr Aisha counts 25 beats at a volunteer's wrist in 15 seconds. What are the volunteer's heart rate and the length of one cardiac cycle?
   - **A.** 100 beats min⁻¹; 1.67 s
   - **B.** 25 beats min⁻¹; 2.4 s
   - **C.** 375 beats min⁻¹; 0.16 s
   - **D.** 100 beats min⁻¹; 0.60 s ✓
   - *Explanation:* Heart rate = beats in t × 60/t = 25 × 60/15 = 25 × 4 = 100 beats min⁻¹. Cycle time T = 60 s ÷ heart rate = 60 ÷ 100 = 0.60 s. Option A has the right rate but turns the division upside down (100 ÷ 60 = 1.67). Option B forgets to scale the 15 s count up to one minute. Option C multiplies by 15 instead of by 60/15 = 4, a scaling slip that gives an impossible rate.

3. **[WAEC-style · Remember]** Which sequence shows the correct path of the electrical impulse that starts each heartbeat?
   - **A.** Sinoatrial node → atrioventricular node → bundle of His → Purkinje fibres ✓
   - **B.** Atrioventricular node → sinoatrial node → Purkinje fibres → bundle of His
   - **C.** Sinoatrial node → bundle of His → atrioventricular node → Purkinje fibres
   - **D.** Medulla oblongata → sinoatrial node → atrioventricular node → bundle of His
   - *Explanation:* The SAN in the right atrium wall fires first and the wave crosses both atria; the AVN holds it for a moment, then the bundle of His carries it down the septum and the Purkinje fibres spread it through the ventricle walls. Option B starts at the wrong node and reverses the last two steps. Option C places the bundle of His before the AVN, but the bundle can only receive the impulse after the AVN has passed it on. Option D is Baba Sule's myth: heart muscle is myogenic, and the brain only changes the rate; it does not start each beat.

4. **[WAEC-style · Analyse]** Chidi's heart rate after his sprint was 132, 116, 96, 80, 72 and 68 beats min⁻¹ at 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 minutes. Between which two readings did his heart rate fall by the largest amount?
   - **A.** 0 and 1 minute
   - **B.** 3 and 4 minutes
   - **C.** 1 and 2 minutes ✓
   - **D.** 4 and 5 minutes
   - *Explanation:* The falls are 132 − 116 = 16, 116 − 96 = 20, 96 − 80 = 16, 80 − 72 = 8 and 72 − 68 = 4 beats min⁻¹, so the biggest drop (20) is between 1 and 2 minutes. Option A is the tempting guess "the first minute always falls most", but it fell by 16, not 20. Options B and D are the smallest drops, where the curve is flattening out as it nears the resting rate, which is why the points are joined with a smooth curve, not a straight line.

5. **[NECO-style · Understand]** While the ventricles are contracting and blood is being driven into the aorta and pulmonary artery, which statement about the valves is correct?
   - **A.** The AV valves are shut and the semilunar valves are open. ✓
   - **B.** The AV valves are open and the semilunar valves are shut.
   - **C.** The AV valves and the semilunar valves are both open.
   - **D.** The AV valves and the semilunar valves are both shut.
   - *Explanation:* To push blood out into the arteries, the semilunar valves must be open; at the same moment the AV valves must be shut, or blood would be forced back up into the atria. Option B is the state while the heart is relaxed and filling, the reverse of this stage. Option C would let blood escape backwards into the atria. Option D can only last an instant at the very start of contraction, before ventricular pressure passes arterial pressure; while blood is actually flowing out, the semilunar valves are open.

6. **[WAEC-style · Apply]** Dr Aisha's own resting heart rate is 64 beats min⁻¹ and her stroke volume is 75 cm³. What is her cardiac output?
   - **A.** 4800 dm³ min⁻¹
   - **B.** 0.48 dm³ min⁻¹
   - **C.** 4.80 dm³ min⁻¹ ✓
   - **D.** 1.17 dm³ min⁻¹
   - *Explanation:* Cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume = 64 × 75 cm³ = 4800 cm³ min⁻¹; then 4800 ÷ 1000 = 4.80 dm³ min⁻¹. Option A keeps the cm³ number but writes the dm³ unit (forgot to divide by 1000). Option B is a ÷10 slip in the conversion (dividing by 10 000). Option D divides the stroke volume by the rate (75 ÷ 64) instead of multiplying.

7. **[JAMB-style · Remember]** Where in the heart is the natural pacemaker (sinoatrial node) found?
   - **A.** In the wall of the left atrium
   - **B.** In the septum between the two ventricles
   - **C.** In the medulla oblongata of the brain
   - **D.** In the right atrium's wall ✓
   - *Explanation:* The SAN is a small patch of special muscle set into the right atrium's wall, close to where the venae cavae enter. Option A puts it on the wrong side. Option B is where the bundle of His runs, not where the beat begins. Option C is the cardiac centre, which only adjusts the rate through nerves; it is not part of the heart.

   - **C.** 220%
   - **D.** 84%
   - *Explanation:* % change = (new − old)/old × 100 = (154 − 70)/70 × 100 = 84/70 × 100 = 120%. Option A divides the rise by the new value (84/154) instead of the old one. Option C gives the new rate as a percentage of the old one (154/70 × 100), forgetting to subtract the starting value first. Option D stops at the rise in beats (84) and never divides by the old value.

9. **[WAEC Practical · Analyse]** In the pulse-rate practical, a learner waited about 20 seconds after the step-ups before starting the first count. How did this affect the peak heart rate she recorded?

    - *Explanation:* The cardiac centre in the medulla slows the SAN by sending impulses down the vagus nerve; this is how Chidi's rate glided back to 68.     - **D.** 70% ✓

### Part 2 · Home Investigation & 30-Mark Written Paper (Lesson 04)

**Part A · Home Investigation:** *Does posture or a short climb change your pulse?*

You will measure your own pulse (or a family member's) lying down, sitting, standing and after climbing stairs, then see how quickly it settles.

*You need:* a phone or wall clock with a seconds hand (or the phone stopwatch), a mat or bed to lie on, a flight of stairs or a sturdy low step (a block or the bottom step outside the house), a pencil and your notebook.

*Safety:* Anyone with asthma, sickle cell disease or a known heart condition should only take the resting readings and must not do the climb. Climb at a steady pace, not a race, hold the rail, and stop at once if you feel dizzy or short of breath. Do not do the climb straight after a heavy meal.

*Steps:*
1. Lie still for 3 minutes. Place your index and middle fingers (not the thumb) on the thumb side of the inside of the wrist and count the beats for 15 s. Repeat twice more and take the mean.
2. Sit up and wait 1 minute; take three 15 s counts again. Then stand still for 1 minute and repeat.
3. Climb up and down the stairs (or step up and down the low step) steadily for 1 minute.
4. Count for 15 s at once, then again at 1, 2, 3 and 4 minutes, sitting quietly between counts.
5. Multiply each count by 4 to get beats per minute. Draw a ruled table, then plot heart rate (y) against time after the climb (x) and join the points with a smooth curve.

*Sample results table:*

| Condition | Mean beats in 15 s | Heart rate (beats min⁻¹) |
|---|---|---|
| Lying | 16 | 64 |
| Sitting | 17 | 68 |
| Standing | 19 | 76 |
| 0 min after climb | 30 | 120 |
| 1 min after | 25 | 100 |
| 2 min after | 21 | 84 |
| 3 min after | 19 | 76 |
| 4 min after | 18 | 72 |

*What a good answer shows:* the rate is lowest lying down and rises a little on sitting and standing, because on standing blood tends to pool in the legs and the heart beats faster to keep enough blood reaching the brain; the climb raises the rate sharply because working leg muscles need more oxygen and glucose and produce more carbon dioxide, and adrenaline and sympathetic nerves speed up the SAN; recovery is fast at first and then slows, so the points lie on a curve, not a straight line; the learner states the recovery time (where the curve meets the resting rate); the learner notes at least one source of error with its direction (for example, a delay before the first count makes the peak too low; one miscounted beat becomes an error of 4 beats min⁻¹ after multiplying); results sit in a ruled table with units in the column headings.

**Part B · Written Paper (30 marks)**

**b1 · Definitions (3 marks)**
Define each of the following: (a) pulse; (b) blood pressure; (c) cardiac output.
*Model solution:* (a) A pulse is the regular throb felt in an artery as its wall stretches and then springs back each time the left ventricle forces blood into the aorta (1). (b) Blood pressure is the force with which blood pushes against the walls of the vessels it flows through, measured in mmHg (1). (c) Cardiac output is the volume of blood pumped out by one ventricle in one minute; it equals heart rate × stroke volume (1). Equivalent wordings that keep the key ideas are credited.
*Mark scheme note:* For (a), "the beating of the heart" scores zero: the pulse is felt in an artery, not in the heart. For (c), "the blood in the heart" or a volume with no time ("per minute") is not credited.

**b2 · The cardiac cycle (5 marks)**
At a resting rate of about 75 beats per minute, one heartbeat lasts about 0.8 s. Describe the stages of one cardiac cycle, stating what happens to the valves at each stage.
*Model solution:* (1) While all four chambers are relaxed (about 0.4 s), blood from the venae cavae and pulmonary veins fills the atria and flows on through the open AV valves into the ventricles; the semilunar valves are closed. (2) The two atria then contract together (about 0.1 s) and push the last of their blood into the ventricles. (3) Next the ventricles contract (about 0.3 s); as their pressure rises above that in the atria, the tricuspid and bicuspid valves close. (4) When ventricular pressure passes the pressure in the arteries, the semilunar valves open and blood is driven into the aorta and pulmonary artery. (5) The ventricles then relax; their pressure falls below that in the arteries and the semilunar valves close, stopping blood from flowing back. One mark for each numbered point.
*Mark scheme note:* The stages must be in the right order. Candidates who say the AV valves open during ventricular contraction, or that the atria and ventricles contract at the same time, lose that point. Timings help but no extra mark is given for timings alone.

**b3 · Heart sounds (3 marks)**
Through a stethoscope Chidi hears "lub-dub, lub-dub".
(a) State the cause of the "lub". (1 mark)
(b) State the cause of the "dub". (1 mark)
(c) Dr Aisha hears an extra swishing sound in another patient's chest. Suggest what this indicates. (1 mark)
*Model solution:* (a) The closing of the AV valves (tricuspid and bicuspid) as the ventricles begin to contract (1). (b) The closing of the semilunar valves at the bases of the aorta and pulmonary artery as the ventricles begin to relax (1). (c) A murmur, which suggests a faulty valve that is leaking or narrowed, so blood is flowing the wrong way or through too small an opening (1).
*Mark scheme note:* Swapping the two sounds scores zero for both (a) and (b). "The heart contracting" or "the ventricles squeezing" is not credited: the sounds come from valves closing.

**b4 · Control of the heartbeat (4 marks)**
(a) Describe how an impulse starting in the heart makes the whole heart contract in the correct order. (3 marks)
(b) Explain why the impulse is delayed at the atrioventricular node. (1 mark)
*Model solution:* (a) The sinoatrial node (pacemaker), set in the right atrium's wall, fires an impulse that spreads across both atria, so they contract (1). The impulse reaches the atrioventricular node and, after a short pause, travels along the bundle of His running down the septum (1). The Purkinje fibres carry it through the ventricle walls, so both ventricles contract from the apex upwards, squeezing blood up towards the arteries (1). (b) The delay gives the atria time to finish emptying into the ventricles before the ventricles start to contract (1).
*Mark scheme note:* Candidates who write "the brain sends the impulse" lose the first mark; the beat is myogenic. In (b), "to rest the heart" is not credited.

**b5 · Calculation: Baba Sule's heart (6 marks)**
Dr Aisha checks Baba Sule at rest. She counts 22 beats in 15 s. His stroke volume is 65 cm³.
(a) Calculate his heart rate. (1 mark)
(b) Calculate the length of one cardiac cycle, to 2 decimal places. (1 mark)
(c) Calculate his cardiac output in dm³ min⁻¹. (2 marks)
(d) After carrying a goat carcass to his stall, his heart rate is 121 beats min⁻¹. Calculate the percentage rise in his heart rate. (2 marks)
*Model solution:* (a) Heart rate = 22 × 60/15 = 22 × 4 = 88 beats min⁻¹ (1). (b) T = 60 s ÷ 88 = 0.6818… ≈ 0.68 s (1). (c) Cardiac output = 88 × 65 cm³ = 5720 cm³ min⁻¹ (1); 5720 ÷ 1000 = 5.72 dm³ min⁻¹ (1). (d) Rise = 121 − 88 = 33 beats min⁻¹; % rise = 33/88 × 100 (1) = 37.5% (1).
*Mark scheme note:* In (c), the first mark is for the product with its cm³ unit and the second for the correct conversion; 5720 dm³ scores only the first mark. In (d), dividing by 121 instead of 88 (giving 27.3%) loses both marks. Answers without units lose the answer mark in (a)–(c).

**b6 · Practical: a recovery curve (5 marks)**
Chidi repeated the pulse practical after a training session. His resting rate was 72 beats min⁻¹. His heart rates (beats min⁻¹) after exercise were:

| Time after exercise (min) | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Heart rate (beats min⁻¹) | 140 | 124 | 104 | 88 | 78 | 74 | 72 |

(a) State his recovery time. (1 mark)
(b) Explain why these points should be joined with a smooth curve and not a straight line. (1 mark)
(c) State **two** sources of error in this practical, giving the direction of each. (2 marks)
(d) Suggest why a trained athlete would recover faster than an untrained learner. (1 mark)
*Model solution:* (a) 6 minutes: the time at which the rate returns to the resting value of 72 (1). (b) The rate does not fall by the same amount each minute (falls of 16, 20, 16, 10, 4 and 2): the fall slows as the rate nears the resting value, so the trend is curved (1). (c) Any two, each with its direction (1 each): a delay before the first count let the heart slow, so the peak reading was too low · counting the beat at the instant the watch started as "one" added a beat, so that rate was 4 beats min⁻¹ too high · pressing too hard on the artery blocked some beats, so the rate was too low · counting with the thumb picked up the thumb's own pulse, so the count could be too high. (d) An athlete's heart is stronger and pumps a larger stroke volume, so it supplies the muscles' needs and clears the extra carbon dioxide with fewer beats and settles back sooner (1).
*Mark scheme note:* An error with no direction ("human error", "the watch was wrong") scores zero. In (b), "because it looks nicer" is not credited; the answer must refer to the uneven fall.

**b7 · Precautions (2 marks)**
State **two** precautions that were taken to obtain a reliable resting pulse rate in the practical.
*Model solution:* Any two (1 each): the pulse was felt with the index and middle fingers, not the thumb · the subject sat quietly for five minutes before the resting count · each resting count was repeated three times and the mean was taken · the stopwatch was started on a beat counted as zero.
*Mark scheme note:* Precautions must be written in the past tense. "Use two fingers" (an instruction) loses the mark. "I was careful" is too vague to score.

**b8 · Baba Sule's claim (2 marks)**
Baba Sule says, "The brain sends every single heartbeat." Explain why he is wrong, and state what the brain actually does to the heart.
*Model solution:* Heart muscle is myogenic: each beat starts in the heart itself, at the sinoatrial node, so the heart would keep beating even if its nerves were cut (1). The cardiac centre in the medulla oblongata only changes the rate: sympathetic nerves (with the hormone adrenaline) speed it up, and impulses down the vagus nerve slow it down (1).
*Mark scheme note:* The second mark needs both directions, one route that speeds the heart and one that slows it. "The brain controls the heart" with no detail scores zero.

    - *Explanation:* The lymphatic system has no pump; lymph is squeezed along by skeletal muscles and breathing movements, and valves stop it flowing back. ### Part 2 · Home Investigation & 30-Mark Written Paper (Lesson 05)

**Part A · Home Investigation:** *Which way do the valves in your veins let blood go?*

You will use the veins on the back of your hand and forearm to show that vein valves let blood flow only towards the heart, and you will time how fast a vein refills.

*You need:* your own arm (or a family member's), a phone stopwatch, a ballpoint pen that writes on skin (or a small piece of masking tape), a chair, your notebook and pencil.

*Safety:* Press gently: you only need enough pressure to flatten the vein. Stop if anything hurts or tingles. Do not tie anything tightly round the arm. Wash off any pen marks afterwards.

*Steps:*
1. Sit down and let one arm hang straight down beside the chair for 1 minute, so the veins on the back of the hand and the forearm stand out. Look along a straight vein for small knots or bulges; mark each one lightly with the pen. These are often the sites of valves.
2. Press one fingertip firmly on the vein just below a bulge (the hand side). Keep pressing, and with a second finger stroke the blood in the vein away from the first finger towards the elbow (towards the heart), then lift the second finger only. Note whether the emptied stretch of vein stays flat.
3. Now lift the first finger and start the stopwatch. Time how long the flat stretch takes to fill again. Repeat three times and take the mean.
4. Repeat Step 2, but this time try to stroke the blood the other way, from the elbow side towards the hand (away from the heart). Note what you feel and see.
5. Repeat Steps 2–3 with the arm raised above the shoulder for 1 minute first. Record the refill time.

*Sample results table:*

| Test | Observation | Refill time (s, mean of 3) | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stroke towards the elbow, first finger held | Stretch of vein stays flat | n/a | A valve stops blood falling back into the emptied stretch |
| First finger lifted (arm hanging) | Vein fills from the hand side | 1.5 | Blood flows towards the heart |
| Stroke towards the hand | Blood will not move past the bulge; vein swells behind the finger | n/a | Valve shuts against blood moving away from the heart |
| First finger lifted (arm raised) | Vein fills slowly or stays flat | 4.0 | Blood must now flow against gravity, so refilling is slower |

*What a good answer shows:* the learner concludes that the valves are pocket valves that open only towards the heart and shut when blood tries to flow back, which is why the stroked stretch stays empty and why blood cannot be pushed towards the hand; the bulges are explained as valve sites; the slower refill with the arm raised is linked to blood returning against gravity, and then to Mama Bisi's legs: when she stands still for hours there is no muscle pump, and if her valves leak, blood pools in her lower legs; the learner suggests at least one practical remedy (raising the feet, walking about to work the calf muscles); the results are in a ruled table with units and means.

**Part B · Written Paper (30 marks)**

**b1 · Definitions (3 marks)**
Define each of the following: (a) tissue fluid; (b) lymph; (c) oedema.
*Model solution:* (a) Tissue fluid is the watery liquid, pressed out of the blood in capillaries, that surrounds and bathes body cells; it carries dissolved food and oxygen to them and wastes away from them, but holds no red cells and very little protein (1). (b) Lymph is the surplus tissue fluid that has drained into the lymph capillaries and is carried in the lymphatic vessels back towards the blood (1). (c) Oedema is the swelling of a body part caused by tissue fluid building up between the cells faster than it is removed (1). Equivalent wordings that keep the key ideas are credited.
*Mark scheme note:* "Tissue fluid is blood outside the vessels" scores zero: it has no red cells. For (b), the answer must say the fluid is inside lymph vessels. For (c), "swelling" alone, without the cause, is not credited.

**b2 · Arteries and veins (5 marks)**
Baba Sule says, "An artery is just a vein with red blood in it." In a table, give **five** ways in which an artery differs from a vein.
*Model solution:*

| Feature | Artery | Vein |
|---|---|---|
| Direction of flow | Away from the heart | Towards the heart (1) |
| Wall | Thick, with much muscle and elastic tissue | Thinner, with less muscle and elastic tissue (1) |
| Lumen | Narrow | Wide (1) |
| Valves | None along its length (only at the heart exits) | Pocket valves along its length (1) |
| Pressure and pulse | High pressure, a pulse is felt | Low pressure, no pulse (1) |

Accept also: blood usually oxygenated (except the pulmonary artery) | usually deoxygenated (except the pulmonary veins); fast flow in surges | slow, steady flow; mostly deep | many near the surface; cut vessel spurts bright red blood | cut vessel gives a steady dark red flow. Any five, 1 mark each.
*Mark scheme note:* A row with only one column filled, or "thick | thin" with no feature named, scores zero. "Arteries carry oxygenated blood, veins carry deoxygenated blood" without naming the pulmonary exceptions is not credited.

**b3 · Capillary adaptations (3 marks)**
State **three** ways in which capillaries are suited to the exchange of materials with body cells, explaining each.
*Model solution:* Any three (1 each, feature and reason together): the wall is only one cell thick, so the diffusion path between blood and cells is very short · there are huge numbers of capillaries, giving a very large total surface area for exchange · blood flows very slowly through them, giving time for exchange · they are so narrow that red cells pass in single file close to the wall, which shortens the path for oxygen · they reach close to every living cell, so no cell is far from a supply.
*Mark scheme note:* A feature without its reason scores zero ("they are thin" alone). Do not credit "capillaries have valves" or "capillaries have thick walls to withstand pressure".

**b4 · Formation and return of tissue fluid (4 marks)**
Describe how tissue fluid is formed at a capillary bed and how it returns to the blood.
*Model solution:* At the arterial end, the blood pressure is greater than the osmotic pull of the plasma proteins, so water and small dissolved substances such as glucose, amino acids, oxygen and salts are forced out between the wall cells (1). Red cells and most plasma proteins are too large to pass and stay in the blood (1). At the venous end, the blood pressure has fallen below the protein pull, so most of the fluid, now carrying carbon dioxide and wastes from the cells, is drawn back into the capillary by osmosis (1). The fluid left over seeps into lymph capillaries, which are closed at one end, and is returned to the blood as lymph (1).
*Mark scheme note:* Each mark point is separate: a candidate who only says "pressure forces fluid out and osmosis brings it back" scores 2 at most. "Red cells leave the capillary" loses the second mark.

**b5 · Calculation: net filtration pressure (6 marks)**
In a capillary of the gut wall, Pc falls evenly from 32 mmHg at the arterial end to 14 mmHg at the venous end. The osmotic pressure of the plasma proteins (πc) is 26 mmHg all along. Take NFP = Pc − πc.
(a) Calculate NFP at the arterial end and state the direction of fluid movement. (1 mark)
(b) Calculate NFP at the venous end and state the direction of fluid movement. (1 mark)
(c) Calculate what fraction of the way along the capillary NFP becomes zero. (2 marks)
(d) In someone whose vein valves leak, like Mama Bisi, the venous-end pressure rises to 28 mmHg. Calculate the new venous-end NFP and explain the effect on the tissues. (2 marks)
*Model solution:* (a) 32 − 26 = +6 mmHg; fluid moves out of the capillary (1). (b) 14 − 26 = −12 mmHg; fluid moves into the capillary (1). (c) NFP is zero when Pc = 26 mmHg, a fall of 32 − 26 = 6 mmHg out of a total fall of 32 − 14 = 18 mmHg (1); 6/18 = 1/3, so one third (0.33) of the way along (1). (d) 28 − 26 = +2 mmHg (1). NFP is now positive at both ends, so fluid leaves along the whole capillary and none is reabsorbed; the extra fluid builds up faster than lymph can drain it, so the tissue swells (oedema) (1).
*Mark scheme note:* A value without its direction loses the mark in (a) and (b). In (c), the first mark is for the two pressure falls, the second for the fraction; "18 out of 32" is a common slip and scores zero. In (d), the second mark needs the link to no reabsorption, not just "the leg swells".

**b6 · Practical: measuring vessels (4 marks)**

| Vessel | Wall thickness (divisions) | Lumen width (divisions) |
|---|---|---|
| Artery | 32 | 96 |
| Vein | 14 | 152 |

(b) Artery 0.80/2.4 = 0.33; vein 0.35/3.8 = 0.092; the artery has a far thicker wall for its size, to withstand and smooth out the high, pulsing pressure from the ventricle, while the vein's wide lumen and thin wall suit slow, low-pressure flow back to the heart (1). (c) The lumen of the vein read too low, because the collapsed walls had moved closer together (1).
*Mark scheme note:* In (a), each mark needs both readings for that vessel, with mm units. In (b), the ratios alone without an explanation score zero. In (c), "the reading was wrong" with no direction scores zero.

**b7 · The lymphatic system (3 marks)**
(a) Trace the path of lymph from a lymph capillary until it rejoins the blood. (1 mark)
(b) Explain how lymph keeps moving although the lymphatic system has no pump. (1 mark)
(c) State **one** function of the lymphatic system other than returning tissue fluid to the blood. (1 mark)
*Model solution:* (a) Lymph capillary → larger lymph vessels with valves, passing through lymph nodes → thoracic duct (or right lymphatic duct) → subclavian vein near the neck (1). *Mark scheme note:* Precautions must be in the past tense; "calibrate the graticule" (an instruction) loses the mark. "The microscope was handled carefully" is too vague to score.

1. **[WAEC-style · Remember]** In the UCH teaching lab, Dr Aisha leaves a tube of the rider's blood standing until a jelly-like lump forms at the bottom. She then pours off the clear, straw-coloured liquid above it. Compared with plasma, which substance is now missing from this liquid?

Cells per mm³ of diluted blood = 460 ÷ 0.020 = 23 000 (1). Cells per mm³ of whole blood = 23 000 × 200 = 4 600 000 = 4.6 × 10⁶ (1). (b) This is close to, and a little below, the typical value of about 5 million per mm³ (1).
*Mark scheme note:* Each mark is for a separate step: the volume of one square, the total volume of five squares (with the total count), the diluted count, and undoing the dilution. Candidates who divide by 200 (getting about 115) lose the last mark of (a). A final answer without a unit ("per mm³") loses the last mark of (a).

**b7 · Practical: precautions and errors (4 marks)**
(a) State **two** precautions that were taken when filling and counting the haemocytometer. (2 marks)
(b) For **two** sources of error, state whether each made the count too high or too low, and why. (2 marks)
*Model solution:* (a) Any two, in the past tense: the chamber was filled from the edge of the cover slip in one smooth flow so that no air bubbles were trapped (1); the cells were left to settle for 2 minutes before counting (1); cells touching the top and left lines were counted but those on the bottom and right lines were not (1); the count was repeated with a second filling and the mean was taken (1). (b) Any two: counting cells on all four boundary lines counted some cells twice, so the count was too high (1); drawing too much diluting fluid into the pipette made the blood weaker than 1 in 200, so the count was too low (1); counting before the cells had settled missed cells that were out of focus, so the count was too low (1); an overfilled chamber was deeper than 0.1 mm and held more cells, so the count was too high (1).
*Mark scheme note:* Precautions written as instructions ("fill the chamber slowly") lose the mark: they must be in the past tense. In (b), an error earns its mark only with both a direction and a reason; "human error" or "the count was wrong" scores zero.

**b8 · Cell size and the rider's blood loss (3 marks)**
(a) On a micrograph at ×3000, a red cell measures 21 mm across. Calculate its actual diameter in micrometres. (2 marks)
(b) The rider's wound has sealed. Explain why he still needs a blood transfusion. (1 mark)
*Model solution:* (a) Actual size = image size ÷ magnification = 21 mm ÷ 3000 = 0.007 mm (1). 0.007 mm × 1000 = 7.0 µm (1). (b) The clot only stops further loss; it does not replace the red cells (and plasma) he has already lost, so his blood can no longer carry enough oxygen to his tissues (1).
*Mark scheme note:* In (a) the second mark is for the conversion to µm; "0.007 µm" scores only the first mark. In (b) "because the clot is weak" or "because of germs" scores zero.

Part B total: 3 + 4 + 5 + 4 + 2 + 5 + 4 + 3 = 30 marks.

## Lesson 07 · Blood Groups, Rhesus, Transfusion and Disorders: Closing the Case

### Part 1 · 12 Interactive MCQs (`window.LESSON_QUIZ`)

1. **[JAMB-style · Remember]** A person's plasma carries both anti-a and anti-b antibodies. Which ABO group does this person belong to?
   - **A.** AB
   - **B.** O ✓
   - **C.** A
   - **D.** B
   - *Explanation:* Plasma holds the antibodies for whichever antigens the person's own red cells lack. Group O cells carry neither antigen A nor antigen B, so the plasma holds both anti-a and anti-b. Group AB (A) is the reverse: both antigens, no antibodies. Group A (C) has only anti-b, and group B (D) has only anti-a.

2. **[WAEC Practical · Apply]** On the typing tile, a donor's blood clumps in the anti-A well and in the anti-B well, but stays smooth in the anti-D well. What is the donor's blood group?
   - **A.** O, Rh+
   - **B.** AB, Rh+
   - **C.** A, Rh−
   - **D.** AB, Rh− ✓
   - *Explanation:* Clumping in a serum shows the matching antigen is on the cells. Clumping with anti-A and anti-B means antigens A and B are both present: group AB. No clumping with anti-D means no Rhesus antigen: Rh−. Option A reads clumping as "absent", which reverses the rule. Option B gets the ABO group right but misreads the smooth anti-D well. Option C ignores the clumping in the anti-B well.

3. **[WAEC-style · Apply]** At Adeoyo hospital, a woman of group O, Rh+ is bleeding heavily after childbirth. The cool box holds O+, O−, A−, B+, B− and AB−. Which list contains **all** the bags that are compatible with her for both ABO and Rhesus?
   - **A.** O− only
   - **B.** O+, O−, A− and B−
   - **C.** O+ and O− ✓
   - **D.** AB− only
   - *Explanation:* Her plasma carries anti-a and anti-b, so any bag whose cells carry antigen A or B (A−, B+, B−, AB−) would clump. That leaves the two O bags. She is Rh+, so her cells already carry the D antigen and Rh+ blood will not sensitise her: O+ is safe, and O− is also safe. Option A is safe but incomplete, because it leaves out O+. Option B wrongly includes A− and B−, which carry antigens her antibodies attack. Option D is Baba Sule's "AB suits everybody" error: AB cells carry both antigens.

4. **[NECO-style · Understand]** Why is the AB− bag unsafe for the rider, who is group B, Rh−?
   - **A.** AB cells carry antigen A, and the anti-a in the rider's plasma would clump them. ✓
   - **B.** AB plasma contains anti-a and anti-b, which would attack the rider's cells.
   - **C.** The AB− bag is Rhesus positive.
   - **D.** AB blood clots faster than group B blood.
   - *Explanation:* The rule is to check the donor's antigens against the patient's antibodies. The rider is group B, so his plasma holds anti-a; AB cells carry antigen A (as well as B), so they would be clumped. Having antigen B as well does not help. Option B is false: group AB plasma has no anti-a or anti-b at all. Option C misreads the label: the minus sign means Rh−. Option D confuses clotting with clumping; blood group does not change clotting speed.

5. **[WAEC-style · Understand]** Hadiza writes that "clotting and agglutination are the same thing". Which statement correctly tells them apart?
   - **A.** They are the same process, seen at a wound and in a test tube.
   - **B.** Agglutination is red cells sticking together when an antigen comes into contact with the antibody that fits it; clotting is soluble fibrinogen being turned into fibrin threads at a wound. ✓
   - **C.** Agglutination needs thromboplastin and calcium ions; clotting needs anti-A and anti-B serum.
   - **D.** Clotting happens when the wrong blood group is transfused; agglutination seals a cut.
   - *Explanation:* The two are different reactions with different triggers. Agglutination is an antigen–antibody reaction that sticks red cells together; clotting is the thromboplastin → thrombin → fibrin chain that seals a wound. Option A ignores this difference. Option C swaps what each process needs. Option D swaps the two outcomes.

6. **[JAMB-style · Analyse]** In which family is the **second** baby most at risk of haemolytic disease of the newborn?
   - **A.** Rh+ mother; first baby Rh−; second baby Rh−
   - **B.** Rh− mother; first baby Rh−; second baby Rh+
   - **C.** Rh+ mother; first baby Rh+; second baby Rh+
   - **D.** Rh− mother; first baby Rh+, with no anti-D injection given after the birth; second baby Rh+ ✓
   - *Explanation:* The danger needs an Rh− mother who has already been sensitised. At the first Rh+ birth, some baby cells entered her blood; with no anti-D injection she made her own anti-D, which can pass across the placenta and break down the red cells of a second Rh+ baby. In A and C the mother is Rh+, so she never makes anti-D. In B the first baby was Rh−, so there was no Rhesus antigen to sensitise her before the second pregnancy.

7. **[WAEC-style · Understand]** How does an anti-D injection given to an Rh− mother soon after delivering an Rh+ baby protect her next Rh+ baby?
   - **A.** It gives the next baby the Rhesus antigen so it is not attacked.
   - **B.** It turns the mother's blood Rh+ permanently.
   - **C.** It destroys the baby's Rh+ red cells that entered her blood before her own immune system can learn to make anti-D. ✓
   - **D.** It thickens the placenta so that no antibodies can cross.
   - *Explanation:* The injected antibodies clear the stray Rh+ cells quickly, so her body never builds up its own long-lasting anti-D; with none of her own anti-D, the next Rh+ baby is safe. The next baby already has the antigen (A), which is the problem, not the cure. A mother's Rhesus group is inherited and cannot be changed (B). The injection has no effect on the placenta (D).

8. **[NECO-style · Apply]** Dr Aisha uses 140/90 mmHg as her warning line for high blood pressure. Which reading is above the line for **both** numbers?
   - **A.** 152/96 mmHg ✓
   - **B.** 150/86 mmHg
   - **C.** 132/94 mmHg
   - **D.** 138/88 mmHg
   - *Explanation:* The systolic number (when the ventricles contract) is compared with 140 and the diastolic number (when they relax) with 90. For A, 152 ≥ 140 and 96 ≥ 90, so both are high. In B the systolic is high but 86 is below 90. In C the diastolic is high but 132 is below 140. In D both numbers are below the line. For the keyed reading, the pulse pressure is 152 − 96 = 56 mmHg, wider than the usual 120 − 80 = 40 mmHg.

9. **[NECO-style · Apply]** Which disorder is correctly matched with a way to prevent or control it?
   - **A.** Haemophilia: eating iron-rich food such as ugu and beans
   - **B.** Varicose veins: an anti-D injection after childbirth
   - **C.** Thrombosis: genotype testing before marriage
   - **D.** Sickle cell anaemia: genotype testing and counselling before marriage ✓
   - *Explanation:* Sickle cell anaemia is inherited, so couples who know their genotypes (for example, two AS carriers) can make an informed choice with a counsellor. Iron-rich food (A) helps iron-deficiency anaemia, not haemophilia, which is an inherited clotting failure managed by avoiding injury and giving clotting factor. Anti-D (B) prevents Rhesus disease of the newborn; varicose veins are helped by not standing still for hours and by exercise. Thrombosis (C) is a clot inside a vessel; it is reduced by moving about on long journeys and by anticoagulant drugs, not by genotype tests.

10. **[WAEC Practical · Analyse]** Tobi typed a group A, Rh− sample but used one stirrer for all three wells, going from the anti-A well to the anti-B well. Slight clumping then appeared in the anti-B well. What is the best explanation?
    - **A.** The sample really carries antigen B, so it is group AB.
    - **B.** Anti-A serum and group A cells were carried into the anti-B well on the stirrer, giving a false positive that could make him misread the sample as AB. ✓
    - **C.** Anti-B serum always clumps a little after 2 minutes.
    - **D.** The stirrer carried antigen D into the well, so the sample is Rh+.
    - *Explanation:* The stirrer brought group A cells and anti-A serum from the first well, so they reacted in the anti-B well. The clumping did not come from the anti-B serum, so it is a false positive. That is why a fresh stirrer is used for every well. Option A trusts a contaminated result. Option C is false: anti-B does not clump cells without antigen B. Option D is wrong because the Rhesus result is read only in the anti-D well, and anti-D serum was not involved here.

11. **[JAMB-style · Remember]** A clot that forms inside an unbroken blood vessel and blocks it causes the condition called
    - **A.** atherosclerosis
    - **B.** haemophilia
    - **C.** thrombosis ✓
    - **D.** leukaemia
    - *Explanation:* Thrombosis is clotting inside a vessel, for example in a leg vein after long hours without moving. Atherosclerosis (A) is fatty deposits narrowing and stiffening arteries; it can lead to a clot but is not the clot itself. Haemophilia (B) is the opposite problem: blood fails to clot. Leukaemia (D) is a cancer of white cells.

12. **[WAEC-style · Analyse]** Dr Aisha's whiteboard holds four conclusions from health day. Which one is correct?
    - **A.** Long hours of standing and faulty leg-vein valves raised the pressure at the venous end of her ankle capillaries, so more tissue fluid formed than could drain away, and her ankles swelled. ✓
    - **B.** The rider should receive the O+ bag, because O is the universal donor.
    - **C.** Chidi's pulse of 132 after the sprint shows that his pacemaker is damaged.
    - **D.** The rider's wound sealed because his red cells agglutinated at the cut.
    - *Explanation:* Raised pressure at the venous end of the capillaries weakens the return of fluid, so tissue fluid builds up as oedema; this is Case 2 solved (her high blood pressure is a separate risk, not the cause of the swelling). Option B ignores Rhesus: the rider is Rh−, so Rh+ cells could sensitise him, and B− or O− must be used. Option C mistakes a normal, healthy response to exercise for a fault; his pulse returned to resting within minutes. Option D confuses clumping with clotting; fibrin sealed the wound.

### Part 2 · Home Investigation & 30-Mark Written Paper (Lesson 07)

**Part A · Home Investigation:** *The paper cross-match and the family salt check*

You will build a paper model that shows why some donor–patient pairs clump, and then look at one everyday habit that affects blood pressure in your home. You will not use any blood or needles.

*You need:* thin card or an old exercise-book cover, scissors, a pencil and ruler, a teaspoon, and a notebook.

*Safety and respect:* Do not prick anyone's finger or handle anyone's blood. Asking relatives about their blood group, genotype or blood pressure is optional: ask politely, accept "I don't know" or "I'd rather not say", and keep their answers private.

*Steps:*
1. **The paper cells.** Cut out 8 card circles as red cells. Draw a small triangle on the edge of two of them (antigen A), a small square on two (antigen B), both shapes on two (group AB) and nothing on the last two (group O). Label each with its group.
2. **The paper antibodies.** Cut out Y-shaped cards. On the arms of some, cut a triangle-shaped notch (anti-a); on others, a square notch (anti-b). A group A patient gets anti-b cards, group B gets anti-a, group O gets both, group AB gets none.
3. Draw a 4 × 4 grid with donors down the side and patients across the top. For each square, test whether any of the patient's antibody notches fits a shape on the donor's cell. If one fits, write "clumps"; if none fits, write "safe".
4. Add a Rhesus row: mark a dot on cells that are Rh+, and decide which of them an Rh− patient should not receive.
5. **The salt check.** For one day, ask whoever cooks to let you note how many level teaspoons of salt and how many seasoning cubes go into the family's meals. Divide by the number of people who ate.
6. Write a short heart-health plan for your home with three changes, one of which uses your salt result.

*Sample results table:*

| Donor ↓ / Patient → | A | B | AB | O |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | safe | clumps | safe | clumps |
| B | clumps | safe | safe | clumps |
| AB | clumps | clumps | safe | clumps |
| O | safe | safe | safe | safe |

| Salt check (one day, 5 people) | Amount |
|---|---|
| Level teaspoons of salt | 3 |
| Seasoning cubes | 4 |
| Salt per person | 3 ÷ 5 = 0.6 teaspoon, plus the salt in the cubes |

*What a good answer shows:* the grid matches the ABO rules (O gives to all four groups; AB receives from all four; A and B each receive from their own group and O); the student states the rule in words, that the donor's antigens are checked against the patient's antibodies; the Rhesus row keeps Rh+ cells away from Rh− patients; the salt result is honest, notes that seasoning cubes also contain salt, and compares the amount with the World Health Organization's advice for adults of less than 5 g of salt a day, just under one level teaspoon; the three-change plan is practical for a Nigerian home (for example, fewer cubes, more vegetables and fruit, a daily walk, a pressure check for adults at a health day); and one limit of the paper model is stated, for example that real antibodies are dissolved in plasma and real cells carry many antigen copies.

**b2 · Agglutination (3 marks)**
(a) State what is meant by agglutination. (1 mark)
(b) Explain why giving group A blood to a group B patient is dangerous. (2 marks)
*Model solution:* (a) Agglutination is the clumping together of red cells when the antigen on them meets the antibody that matches it (1). Equivalent wordings that keep the key ideas are credited. (b) The donor's red cells carry antigen A, and the patient's plasma contains anti-a, so the donor cells are clumped (1). The clumps can block small vessels such as capillaries and the burst cells can damage the kidneys, which may kill the patient (1).
*Mark scheme note:* Do not credit "the blood clots" for (a): clumping is not clotting. In (b) the first mark needs both the donor's antigen and the patient's antibody; naming only one does not earn it.

**b3 · Choosing the rider's bag (5 marks)**
The injured okada rider is group B, Rh−. The cool box holds O+, O−, A−, B+, B− and AB−. State which bags are safe for him and explain your choice.
*Model solution:* A− and AB− are rejected, because their cells carry antigen A, which the anti-a in his plasma would clump (1). O+ and B+ are rejected, because he is Rh− and Rh+ cells could make him produce anti-D, so a later transfusion could cause a reaction (1). B− is the first choice, as an exact match for both ABO and Rhesus (1). O− is the alternative if B− runs out (1), because its cells carry no A, B or D antigen for his antibodies to react with (1).
*Mark scheme note:* Each mark is for a separate decision; a candidate who simply lists "B− and O−" without reasons earns only the two marks for naming them. Choosing AB− because "AB receives from everybody" confuses the universal recipient with a universal donor and scores zero for that line.

**b4 · Haemolytic disease of the newborn (5 marks)**
(a) Explain how an Rh− mother's second Rh+ baby can be harmed. (4 marks)
(b) State how this is prevented. (1 mark)
*Model solution:* (a) At the birth of her first Rh+ baby, some of the baby's red cells, which carry the Rhesus (D) antigen, enter the mother's blood (1). Her immune system responds by making anti-D antibodies (1). In a later pregnancy with an Rh+ baby, her anti-D crosses the placenta into the baby's blood (1). The anti-D destroys the baby's red cells, so the baby becomes anaemic and may die (1). (b) The mother is given an anti-D injection soon after each Rh+ delivery (1).
*Mark scheme note:* Reversing the Rhesus groups (an Rh+ mother with an Rh− baby) scores zero for the whole of (a). The first baby is not usually harmed; candidates who say it is lose the first mark. "Anti-D is given to the baby" does not earn (b).

**b5 · Reading a typing tile (3 marks)**

|---|---|---|---|

*Mark scheme note:* Each mark needs both the ABO group and the Rhesus group. Q is often given as "AB" by candidates who read "no clumping" as "both antigens present"; that scores zero.

**b6 · Practical: precautions and errors (4 marks)**
(a) State **two** precautions that were taken during the blood-typing practical. (2 marks)
(b) For **two** sources of error, state the effect on the result and why. (2 marks)
*Model solution:* (a) Any two, in the past tense: a separate clean stirrer was used for each well (1); the wells were labelled before any serum was added (1); the lancet was sterile, used once and dropped into a sharps bin (1); gloves were worn and the tile was disinfected after use (1); each well was read only after the full 2 minutes (1). (b) Any two: reading before 2 minutes missed weak clumping, so a positive well was recorded as negative, a false negative (1); reusing one stirrer carried cells and serum between wells, giving clumping that did not belong there, a false positive (1); a drop drying at the edge of a well looked grainy and was mistaken for clumping, a false positive (1).
*Mark scheme note:* Precautions must be in the past tense; "use a new stirrer" loses the mark. An error needs both the effect (false positive or false negative) and the reason. "Use clean apparatus" with no detail is not credited.

**b7 · Circulatory disorders (3 marks)**
For each disorder, state its cause and **one** way to prevent or control it: (a) atherosclerosis, (b) sickle cell anaemia, (c) varicose veins.
*Model solution:* (a) Fatty deposits build up in artery walls, narrowing and hardening them; prevented by eating less saturated fat, not smoking and keeping active (1). (b) An inherited abnormal haemoglobin (HbS) makes red cells bend into sickle shapes that jam in capillaries; reduced by genotype testing and counselling before marriage (1). (c) The valves in leg veins fail, so blood pools and the veins swell and twist; reduced by avoiding standing still for many hours and by regular exercise (1).
*Mark scheme note:* Each mark needs both the cause and a matching prevention. "Eat a good diet" alone is too vague. Do not credit "sickle cell is caught from an infected person": it is inherited.

**b8 · Mama Bisi's follow-up (3 marks)**

(a) Is her new reading still above the warning line? Give a reason. (1 mark)

(c) Any one: regular exercise such as a daily walk; losing excess weight; not smoking; little or no alcohol; managing stress; taking the drugs her doctor prescribes and having her pressure checked regularly (1).
