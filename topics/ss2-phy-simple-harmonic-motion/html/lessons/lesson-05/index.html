Precautions (past tense):

  • I measured the length to the centre of the bob.
  • I ensured the angle of swing was small.
  • I started and stopped timing at the rest position.
  • I repeated each timing and used the mean.
  • I avoided parallax on the metre rule.
  • I ensured the bob swung in one vertical plane.
  • I ensured the cork was firmly clamped so the point of suspension did not move.

Sources of error (with effect): length to the top of the bob → every l too small by r, slope unchanged but the line misses the origin · counting from "one" → only 19 oscillations timed, T too small by 19/20, g too large by (20/19)² ≈ 1.11 · wide angle → T slightly too large, g slightly too small · reaction time (random) · conical swing → shorter period, g too large.

Model answers unlocks after you try
  1. Squaring T = 2π√(l/g) gives T² = (4π²/g) l, a straight line through the origin; T against l is a curve.
  2. The slope equals 4π²/g, so g = 4π² ÷ slope.
  3. Large ΔT² and Δl make the small reading uncertainty a small percentage of each, so the slope is more accurate.
  4. Every point moves left by r: the line keeps its slope but cuts the T² axis above the origin.
  5. The graph averages all the readings and shows a constant error as an intercept instead of hiding it in the answer.

3Make sense · 15 min

From a formula to a straight line

Why does a graph of T² against l give g, and what changes when you can't reach the ceiling?

From formula to straight line: T² = (4π²/g) l has the form y = mx with y = T², x = l and slope 4π²/g, so g = 4π²/slope. The graph averages many readings and shows a constant error (an intercept) that a single reading would hide.

Ceiling variant: if the ceiling is out of reach, measure the height y of the bob's centre above the floor; then l = H − y and T² = (4π²/g)(H − y). The T²–y line slopes downwards (slope −4π²/g), and it cuts the horizontal (y) axis, where T² = 0, at y = H, the ceiling height.

Key equations

l = thread + r · T = t̄/n · T² = (4π²/g) l · g = 4π²/s · T² = (4π²/g)(H − y)

4Apply · Worked examples · 10 min

Worked examples, step by step

Try each on paper first, then check every step.

EXAMPLE 1

g from the slope

The best-fit line of a T²–l graph passes through (0.400 m, 1.613 s²) and (1.000 m, 4.032 s²). Find g.

  1. Slope from two points on the line: s = (4.032 − 1.613) ÷ (1.000 − 0.400)
  2. Evaluate: s = 2.419 ÷ 0.600 = 4.03 s² m⁻¹
  3. Use g = 4π²/s: g = 39.48 ÷ 4.03 = 9.79 m s⁻²
g ≈ 9.79 m s⁻²
EXAMPLE 2

g from one reading

A thread is 0.780 m from the cork to the top of a bob of radius 2.0 cm. Twenty oscillations take 35.90 s. Find g.

  1. Effective length (add r): l = 0.780 + 0.020 = 0.800 m
  2. Period, then square it: T = 35.90 ÷ 20 = 1.795 s, so T² = 1.795² = 3.222 s²
  3. Substitute in g = 4π²l/T²: g = 39.48 × 0.800 ÷ 3.222
  4. Evaluate: g = 31.58 ÷ 3.222 = 9.80 m s⁻²
g ≈ 9.80 m s⁻²
EXAMPLE 3

The cost of a slip

In Example 2, Tunde forgets the radius and uses l = 0.780 m. Find his g and the percentage error.

  1. His value: g = 39.48 × 0.780 ÷ 3.222 = 9.56 m s⁻²
  2. Error (unrounded values): 9.802 − 9.557 = 0.245 m s⁻²
  3. Percentage error: 0.245 ÷ 9.80 × 100 = 2.5 % (too low), the same as r/l = 0.020 ÷ 0.800 = 0.025
g ≈ 9.56 m s⁻², about 2.5 % too low.
EXAMPLE 4

The ceiling variant

With the bob's centre 1.80 m above the floor, T² = 4.028 s²; at 2.20 m, T² = 2.417 s². Find g and the ceiling height H.

  1. Slope: s = (2.417 − 4.028) ÷ (2.20 − 1.80) = −1.611 ÷ 0.40 = −4.03 s² m⁻¹
  2. g from the size of the slope: g = 39.48 ÷ 4.03 = 9.80 m s⁻²
  3. Where T² = 0: from y = 1.80 m, Δy = 4.028 ÷ 4.03 = 1.00 m
  4. Ceiling height: H = 1.80 + 1.00 = 2.80 m
g ≈ 9.80 m s⁻², H ≈ 2.80 m

Assignment: Part A and Part B

Type your attempt first to unlock each model solution.

Part A · Home investigation: measure g in your own house

A stone, a thread and a phone stopwatch: find g from a T²–l graph.

You need: a stone or padlock, about 1.2 m of strong thread, a door frame or nail to hang it from, a tape measure, a phone stopwatch and a sheet of graph paper.

  1. Hang the stone so that the length from the support to the middle of the stone is 0.40 m (measure to the top of the stone, then add half its height).
  2. Mark the rest position. Pull the stone aside by a small angle (no more than about 10 cm at this length) and release it so it swings in one plane.
  3. Start timing as it passes the mark, counting "zero"; time 20 oscillations. Repeat and take the mean.
  4. Repeat for lengths of 0.70 m and 1.00 m.
  5. Find T and T² for each length. Plot T² (vertical) against l (horizontal), draw the best straight line through the origin, find its slope and calculate g = 4π²/slope.

Slope from two points read from the drawn line, e.g. (0.20, 0.81) and (1.00, 4.04): slope = 3.23 ÷ 0.80 = 4.04 s² m⁻¹, so g = 39.48 ÷ 4.04 = 9.8 m s⁻².

A good answer shows a complete table with consistent decimal places, T² against l close to a straight line through the origin, a slope from a large triangle on the line (not from one table row), and g within about ±0.3 m s⁻² of 9.8 m s⁻². A strong answer gives two or more precautions in the past tense ("I measured the length to the middle of the stone", "I ensured the stone swung in one plane") and one source of error with its effect (the stone is not a point mass, so its middle was estimated and each l may be out by a centimetre; the support moved slightly, adding to the effective length).

b1 · Composite table

4 marks

Copy the table and add rows for: the effective length l = L + r (3 d.p.); the mean time t̄ (2 d.p.); the period T = t̄/20 (3 d.p.); and T² (3 d.p.).

Model solution unlocks after you try

r = 3.0 ÷ 2 = 1.5 cm = 0.015 m. l: 0.300, 0.450, 0.600, 0.750, 0.900 m (1) · t̄: 22.00, 26.94, 31.10, 34.78, 38.10 s (1) · T: 1.100, 1.347, 1.555, 1.739, 1.905 s (1) · T²: 1.210, 1.814, 2.418, 3.024, 3.629 s² (1).

Mark scheme note: One mark per correct row; all five values must be correct to the stated decimal places. Using L instead of l (no radius added), or adding the diameter, loses the l row; error carried forward is allowed in later parts. Inconsistent decimal places within a row lose that mark.

b2 · The graph

6 marks

Plot T² (vertical axis) against l (horizontal axis), starting both axes from zero. Draw the line of best fit.

What the examiner looks for unlocks after you try

Both axes labelled with quantity and unit, T²/s² vertical and l/m horizontal (1); a sensible linear scale using at least half the graph paper on each axis, e.g. 2 cm to 0.1 m and 2 cm to 0.5 s², not an awkward scale such as 3 cm to 0.1 (1); all five points correctly plotted, within half a small square (2: 1 mark for three or four correct); a single thin straight line of best fit passing through, or very close to, the origin with the points evenly spread about it (1); a title or clear origin and an overall neat graph with fine points (1).

Mark scheme note: Axes the wrong way round (l vertical) lose the axis mark and make the later slope the inverse; the examiner then allows only error carried forward. Joining the dots dot-to-dot, or a thick line, loses the line mark. A scale that cannot be read easily (multiples of 3 or 7) loses the scale mark.

b3 · The slope

4 marks

Determine the slope s of your graph, showing the triangle you used.

Model solution unlocks after you try

A large right-angled triangle drawn on the line, using points read from the line (not from the table), e.g. (0.100 m, 0.403 s²) and (0.900 m, 3.628 s²) (1); ΔT² = 3.628 − 0.403 = 3.225 s² (1); Δl = 0.900 − 0.100 = 0.800 m (1); s = 3.225 ÷ 0.800 = 4.03 s² m⁻¹ (accept 3.98–4.08) (1).

Mark scheme note: A triangle smaller than half the length of the line loses the first mark. Using two table points that are not on the drawn line loses the first mark. Δl/ΔT² (0.248) is the inverse slope and loses the last mark. The unit s² m⁻¹ is required for the last mark.

b4 · The value of g

3 marks

(a) Show that the slope of the graph is 4π²/g. (b) Use your slope to determine g.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) T = 2π√(l/g), so T² = 4π²l/g = (4π²/g) l, which is y = mx with slope 4π²/g (1). (b) g = 4π²/s (1) = 39.48 ÷ 4.03 = 9.79 m s⁻² (accept 9.68–9.92 m s⁻² on error carried forward) (1).

Mark scheme note: In (b) the unit m s⁻² is required. g = s/4π² or g = 4π² × s loses the last two marks.

b5 · The intercept

2 marks

(a) State where your line cuts the T² axis. (b) Explain what this shows about the way the lengths were measured.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) At (or very near) the origin, T² ≈ 0 when l = 0 (1). (b) It shows that l was correctly measured to the centre of the bob (the radius was added), so no constant error was present in the lengths (1).

Mark scheme note: In (b), "the graph is correct" earns nothing; the link between a zero intercept and correct effective lengths is needed.

b6 · Precautions

3 marks

State three precautions that should have been taken to ensure accurate results.

Model solution unlocks after you try

Any three (1 each), in the past tense: I ensured the bob swung through a small angle (about 10° or less) · I started and stopped the stopwatch as the bob passed its rest position · I measured the length to the centre of the bob · I ensured the bob swung in one vertical plane and not in a circle · I ensured the cork was firmly clamped so the point of suspension did not move · I repeated each timing and used the mean · I avoided parallax when reading the metre rule · I avoided draughts by closing the windows.

Mark scheme note: Precautions written as commands ("ensure the angle is small") or in the future tense score zero. "I repeated the experiment" without saying what was repeated, and "I was careful", are not credited. Sources of error ("reaction time") offered as precautions score zero.

b7 · Errors and their effects

5 marks

(a) Why is the T² against l graph preferred to working out g from a single reading? (b) A second candidate measured every length to the top of the bob. State and explain the effect on (i) his graph and (ii) his value of g. (c) A third candidate started her stopwatch while counting "one". State and explain the effect on her value of g.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) The graph averages many readings, so random errors partly cancel, and it exposes any constant error as an intercept instead of hiding it in the answer (1). (b)(i) Every l is too small by r, so every point shifts left by 0.015 m and the line cuts the T² axis above the origin (1). (ii) The slope is unchanged, so g from the slope is unaffected (1). (c) Only 19 oscillations were really timed but the time was divided by 20 (1), so T was too small and g = 4π²l/T² came out too large (by about 11 %) (1).

Mark scheme note: In (b)(ii) "g too small" scores zero, though it would be true for g calculated from a single reading. In (c) both the 19/20 reason and the direction "too large" are required for the two marks.

b8 · The ceiling variant

3 marks

In another laboratory the support was out of reach, so a candidate measured the height y of the bob's centre above the floor. When y = 1.20 m, T² = 5.646 s²; when y = 1.80 m, T² = 3.226 s². (a) Calculate the slope of T² against y. (b) Hence find g. (c) Find the height H of the point of suspension above the floor.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) slope = (3.226 − 5.646) ÷ (1.80 − 1.20) = −2.420 ÷ 0.60 = −4.03 s² m⁻¹ (1). (b) g = 39.48 ÷ 4.033 = 9.79 m s⁻² (1). (c) The line cuts the horizontal axis (T² = 0) at y = H: from y = 1.20 m the extra height is 5.646 ÷ 4.033 = 1.40 m, so H = 1.20 + 1.40 = 2.60 m (1).

Mark scheme note: A negative value of g loses (b). In (c) error carried forward is allowed from (a).
