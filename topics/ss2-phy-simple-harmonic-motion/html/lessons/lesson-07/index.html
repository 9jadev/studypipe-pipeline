Practical analysis questions

Answer these five questions, then compare with the model answers.

  1. Why did only the equal-length cone swing widely?
  2. Why does the forcing frequency f rise with speed?
  3. At what forcing frequency was A largest, and how does that compare with f₀?
  4. What did the healthy damper do to the peak?
  5. Why did the extra load move the peak to a lower speed?
Model answers unlocks after you try
  1. Every cone was pushed at the driver's frequency through the cord, but a pendulum's natural frequency depends on its length (T = 2π√(l/g)). Only the 0.60 m cone had a natural frequency equal to the driving frequency, so only it resonated.
  2. The ridges are a fixed 2.5 m apart. The faster the okada goes, the less time it takes to reach the next ridge, so more ridges are met each second: f = v/d grows in step with v.
  3. A was largest at f = 2.00 Hz (18 km/h), which equals the okada's natural frequency f₀ = 2.00 Hz: that is resonance.
  4. It made the peak much lower (about 2.0 cm instead of 10.0 cm) and broader, at about the same frequency, so the bounce never reached the lift-off threshold.
  5. More mass on the same spring lowers f₀ (f₀ ∝ 1/√m), from 2.00 Hz to 1.79 Hz. Resonance needs v = d f₀, so the dangerous speed falls to about 16 km/h.

3Make sense · 15 min

Free, damped, forced — and resonant

Now we put names and formulae on what the cones and the okada showed you.

Three kinds of vibration. Free — the system vibrates at its own natural frequency f₀, with no outside push (and, ideally, no losses). Damped — the amplitude dies away because energy is removed each cycle. Forced — an outside periodic push makes the system vibrate at the driving frequency, not its own.

Resonance: when the driving frequency equals the natural frequency, each push arrives in step with the motion, energy builds up, and the amplitude becomes greatest. Damping lowers and widens the resonance peak.

Ridge forcing: a vehicle at speed v meets ridges spaced d apart at a rate f = v/d per second. Resonance happens when v/d = f₀, so the dangerous speed is v = d f₀. Remember to change km/h to m s⁻¹ (÷ 3.6) first.

Key equations

f = v/d · v_res = d f₀ · f₀ = (1/2π)√(k/m) · ω²A ≥ g

4Apply · Worked examples · 10 min

Worked examples, step by step

Try each on paper first, then check every step of the working.

EXAMPLE 1

The dangerous speed

Ridges are 2.5 m apart and the loaded okada's natural frequency is 2.0 Hz. Find the speed at which resonance occurs, in m s⁻¹ and km/h.

  1. Resonance condition: resonance when the forcing frequency equals f₀, and the forcing frequency is f = v/d, so v = d f₀.
  2. Substitute: v = 2.5 × 2.0 = 5.0 m s⁻¹
  3. Conversion factor: 1 m s⁻¹ = 3.6 km h⁻¹
  4. Speed in km/h: v = 5.0 × 3.6 = 18 km h⁻¹
v = 5.0 m s⁻¹ = 18 km h⁻¹

Story numbers are a teaching model, not real measurements.

EXAMPLE 2

Two extra crates

With 250 kg on the same spring (k = 3.16 × 10⁴ N m⁻¹), find the new natural frequency and the dangerous speed.

  1. Natural frequency: f₀ = (1/2π)√(k/m) = (1/2π)√(31600 ÷ 250) = (1/2π)√126.4
  2. Evaluate: f₀ = 11.24 ÷ 6.283 = 1.79 Hz
  3. Resonant speed: v = d f₀ = 2.5 × 1.79 = 4.47 m s⁻¹
  4. In km/h: v = 4.47 × 3.6 = 16 km h⁻¹
f₀ ≈ 1.79 Hz · v ≈ 16 km h⁻¹
EXAMPLE 3

Barton's pendulums

The driver of a Barton's set is 0.60 m long. Find its frequency and say which paper cone resonates. (g = 9.8 m s⁻²)

  1. Period: T = 2π√(0.60 ÷ 9.8) = 2π × 0.2474 = 1.55 s
  2. Frequency: f = 1/T = 1 ÷ 1.55 = 0.64 Hz
  3. Which cone: the cone with the same natural frequency — the same length, 0.60 m — resonates.
f ≈ 0.64 Hz · the 0.60 m cone
EXAMPLE 4

A keke on speed bumps

A keke's suspension has natural frequency 1.5 Hz. Speed bumps are 4.0 m apart. At what speed would it resonate?

  1. Resonant speed: v = d f₀ = 4.0 × 1.5 = 6.0 m s⁻¹
  2. In km/h: v = 6.0 × 3.6 = 21.6 km h⁻¹
  3. Round sensibly: about 22 km/h.
v ≈ 22 km h⁻¹

Clinic 1 · Resonance in words

4 marks

(a) What is meant by resonance? (b) Give one example where resonance is useful and one where it is harmful. (c) State one way the harmful example can be prevented.

Marking guide unlocks after you try

(a) Resonance is when a system is driven by a periodic force whose frequency equals the system's natural frequency, so that it vibrates with the greatest amplitude. (2: one for the frequency match, one for maximum amplitude) (b) Useful: tuning a radio, a guitar body, a talking drum, pushing a swing in time. Harmful: a footbridge swaying under marching feet, Dayo's okada on the ridges, a washing machine walking at one spin speed. (1) (c) Matching prevention, e.g. soldiers break step; replace the worn shock absorber (more damping); avoid the dangerous speed; change the mass or stiffness so f₀ moves away from the driving frequency. (1)

Marking note: "a big vibration" alone scores zero in (a). In (c) the method must fit the example given.

(a) Calculate the natural frequency. (b) Find the speed, in km/h, at which the bus resonates on the strips. (c) Passengers and goods add 400 kg. Find the new resonant speed in km/h and comment.

Marking guide unlocks after you try

Assignment: Part A and Part B

Type your attempt first to unlock each model solution.

Part A · Home investigation: the swing that grows

Push a swing in step, twice as often, and at random. Which builds the biggest swing?

You need: a playground or compound swing, a friend to sit on it (a younger child only with an adult present), and a phone stopwatch. Keep the swing low and gentle; stop at once if anyone is uneasy.

  1. Pull the swing back a little and let it go; time 10 oscillations to find its natural period.
  2. Give a gentle push once per swing, each time it reaches you and starts to move away. Count 10 pushes and note how high it goes.
  3. Let it settle. Now push twice as often (each time it passes you, coming or going), for 10 pushes.
  4. Let it settle. Now push at a random rhythm for 10 pushes.
  5. Describe the amplitude after each set of pushes.

A good answer gives the natural period (for example 2.5 s, f₀ ≈ 0.4 Hz) and explains that in-step pushes build the biggest swing because each push adds energy at the right moment (resonance: driving frequency = natural frequency), while pushes at other rhythms sometimes act against the motion and take energy out. A strong answer links this to the ridges pushing Dayo's okada once per bounce at 18 km/h, notes that friction and air resistance (damping) stopped the swing growing for ever, and gives a safety point about keeping the amplitude small.

Part B · Written paper (30 marks)

1. Kinds of vibration

3 marks

Define: (a) free vibration; (b) damped vibration; (c) forced vibration.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) Vibration of a system at its own natural frequency, with no outside periodic force acting, after it is displaced and released (ideally with constant amplitude). (1) (b) Vibration whose amplitude decreases with time because energy is removed by resistive forces. (1) (c) Vibration of a system caused by an outside periodic force; the system vibrates at the frequency of that driving force. (1)

Mark scheme note: In (c) "at the driving frequency" is needed; "a vibration that is forced" scores zero.

2. Resonance

3 marks

(a) Define resonance. (b) Give one example where resonance is useful and one where it is harmful.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) Resonance occurs when a system is driven by a periodic force whose frequency equals its natural frequency, so that it vibrates with the greatest amplitude. (1) (b) Useful (any one): tuning a radio · the hollow body of a guitar or talking drum amplifying the sound · pushing a swing in time (1). Harmful (any one): a bridge set swaying by marching feet or wind · a vehicle shaking violently at one speed (Dayo's okada) · machinery vibrating dangerously on its mounting (1).

Mark scheme note: In (a) both "frequency equals natural frequency" and "greatest amplitude" are needed. "A big vibration" alone scores zero.

3. Barton's pendulums

4 marks

(a) Describe how Barton's pendulums are set up. (b) Describe what is seen when the driver is set swinging. (c) Explain the observation. (d) State the effect of putting a small weight inside each paper cone.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) A heavy driver pendulum and several light paper-cone pendulums of different lengths hang from the same taut cord; one cone has the same length as the driver. (1) (b) All the cones begin to swing, but the one equal in length to the driver swings with by far the largest amplitude. (1) (c) The driver forces every cone at its own frequency through the cord; only the equal-length cone has a natural frequency equal to the driving frequency, so it resonates. (1) (d) Heavier cones are less affected by air resistance (relatively less damped), so the resonance becomes sharper: the matching cone swings even more widely compared with the others. (1)

Mark scheme note: In (c) the frequency-match idea is essential; "it is the same length" without linking length to natural frequency earns no mark.

4. The dangerous speed

4 marks

A vehicle moves at speed v over ridges spaced d apart. (a) Show that it meets the ridges at a frequency f = v/d. (b) Hence write the speed at which it resonates. (c) Calculate this speed for Dayo's okada (d = 2.5 m, f₀ = 2.0 Hz) in m s⁻¹ and km/h.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) The time between ridges is the distance between them divided by the speed, d/v; the number met per second is the reciprocal, f = v/d. (1) (b) Resonance when f = f₀, so v = d f₀. (1) (c) v = 2.5 × 2.0 = 5.0 m s⁻¹ (1); 5.0 × 3.6 = 18 km/h. (1)

Mark scheme note: In (c) the conversion factor 3.6 must be applied correctly; 5.0 km/h loses the last mark.

7. Synoptic: the whole case

3 marks

Zainab timed 20 bounces of the parked, loaded okada in 10.0 s; the sprung load is 200 kg. (Take g = 9.8 m s⁻².) (a) Find the period and hence the spring constant. (b) Find the static sag. (c) Explain, using the lift-off rule, why the sag tells you the amplitude at which crates begin to leave the carrier.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) T = 10.0 ÷ 20 = 0.50 s; k = 4π²m/T² = 39.48 × 200 ÷ 0.25 = 3.16 × 10⁴ N m⁻¹. (1) (b) sag = mg/k = 200 × 9.8 ÷ 31600 = 0.062 m = 6.2 cm. (1) (c) Lift-off needs ω²A ≥ g, so A ≥ g/ω² = g × m/k = mg/k: exactly the static sag, 6.2 cm. (1)

Mark scheme note: In (a) accept 3.2 × 10⁴ N m⁻¹. In (c) the step ω² = k/m must be used; the numerical coincidence alone is not credited.

8. Advice to Dayo

2 marks

Using your answers, give Dayo and Hajia Ramatu two pieces of advice, each with a physics reason.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(1) Replace the leaking rear shock absorber: a healthy damper turns bounce energy into heat and keeps the resonant amplitude near 2 cm, well below the 6.2 cm lift-off threshold. (1) (2) Until it is replaced, cross the ridge stretch slowly (about 10 km/h or below), well away from the resonant speeds of 18 km/h (usual load) and 16 km/h (extra crates), so the ridges do not push in step with the bounce. Lift-off note
Away from resonance the threshold is g/ω² with the driving ω = 2πv/d (5.0 cm at 20 km/h, 2.2 cm at 30 km/h); at resonance the driving ω equals ω₀ and the threshold equals the static sag, 6.2 cm (7.8 cm for 250 kg).
Model
The bench uses the base-excitation response A = Y√(1 + (2ζr)²)/√((1 − r²)² + (2ζr)²), r = f/f₀, Y = 1.0 cm, ζ = 0.05 (leaking) or 0.30 (healthy), k chosen so that f₀ = 2.00 Hz at 200 kg (quoted 3.16 × 10⁴ N m⁻¹).
