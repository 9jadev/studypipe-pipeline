Precautions (past tense): I started and stopped the stopwatch as the bob passed the rest mark, where it moved fastest; I began counting at “zero”; I timed 20 oscillations and repeated the timing; I displaced the bob only slightly.

Sources of error: reaction time at the start and stop (shared out over 20 cycles); miscounting the oscillations; air resistance slowly reducing the amplitude (it does not noticeably change T).

Practical analysis questions

Answer these five questions, then compare with the model answers.

  1. Why time 20 oscillations rather than 1?
  2. Why start counting at zero?
  3. Why start at the rest mark rather than at an end?
  4. The amplitude shrank during timing; did T change?
  5. Which rig had the higher frequency, and how do you know from T?
Model answers unlocks after you try
  1. The reaction error at each press is about the same however long you time, so dividing by 20 makes it about twenty times smaller as a fraction of T.
  2. Counting “one” as you start means only 19 oscillations are really timed when you reach 20, so T comes out too small.
  3. The bob moves fastest at the rest mark, so the instant it passes is sharp; at the ends it slows, stops and lingers, so the turning instant is hard to judge.
  4. No, not noticeably: the period of a small swing does not depend on its amplitude.
  5. The spring (and the okada even more): it has the shorter period, and since f = 1/T, a shorter T means more oscillations per second.

3Make sense · 15 min

Periodic motion and its measures

Now connect what you timed to the definitions and equations WAEC, NECO and JAMB expect.

Definitions

  • Periodic motion repeats itself in equal intervals of time.
  • Oscillation (vibration) is periodic to-and-fro motion about a fixed rest (equilibrium) position.
  • One complete oscillation is the motion from a point back to the same point, moving in the same direction (for a pendulum: A → O → B → O → A).
  • Amplitude A is the greatest displacement from the rest position (m).
  • Period T is the time for one complete oscillation (s).
  • Frequency f is the number of complete oscillations per second (Hz = s⁻¹).
  • Angular frequency ω is 2π times the frequency (rad s⁻¹).
T = t/n · f = 1/T · ω = 2πf = 2π/T · s = 4A s = distance covered in one complete oscillation

Amplitude is measured from the middle to one end — not from end to end. A bob swinging between marks 12 cm apart has A = 6 cm.

4Apply · Worked examples · 10 min

Worked examples, step by step

Try each on paper first, then check the working.

EXAMPLE 1

The okada's rhythm

Zainab counts 20 bounces of the loaded, parked okada in 10.0 s. Find T, f and ω.

  1. Period: T = t/n = 10.0 ÷ 20 = 0.50 s
  2. Frequency: f = 1/T = 1 ÷ 0.50 = 2.0 Hz
  3. Angular frequency: ω = 2πf = 2 × 3.142 × 2.0 = 12.6 rad s⁻¹
T = 0.50 s · f = 2.0 Hz · ω = 12.6 rad s⁻¹

Story numbers are a teaching model, not real measurements.

EXAMPLE 2

The compound swing

A child on a compound swing makes 15 complete oscillations in 42.0 s. Find the period, frequency and angular frequency.

  1. Period: T = 42.0 ÷ 15 = 2.80 s
  2. Frequency: f = 1 ÷ 2.80 = 0.357 Hz
  3. Angular frequency: ω = 2π ÷ 2.80 = 6.283 ÷ 2.80 = 2.24 rad s⁻¹
T = 2.80 s · f = 0.357 Hz · ω = 2.24 rad s⁻¹
EXAMPLE 3

Why time 20 swings?

A student's reaction error is 0.2 s. A pendulum's true period is 1.80 s. Compare the percentage error from timing one oscillation with that from timing 20.

  1. One oscillation: error = (0.2 ÷ 1.80) × 100 = 11.1 %
  2. Twenty oscillations: t = 20 × 1.80 = 36.0 s, then error = (0.2 ÷ 36.0) × 100 = 0.56 %
  3. Compare: ratio = 11.1 ÷ 0.56 = 20 — the error is shared out twenty times.
about 11 % · about 0.6 %
EXAMPLE 4

Amplitude and distance

A bob swings between two points 12.0 cm apart with frequency 0.50 Hz. Find the amplitude, the period and the distance the bob travels in 10 s.

  1. Amplitude: A = 12.0 ÷ 2 = 6.0 cm
  2. Period: T = 1/f = 1 ÷ 0.50 = 2.0 s, so N = 10 ÷ 2.0 = 5 complete oscillations.
  3. Distance: 4A = 4 × 6.0 = 24 cm per oscillation, so s = 5 × 24 = 120 cm = 1.20 m.
A = 6.0 cm · T = 2.0 s · s = 1.20 m

      5Assignment · 40 min home

      Assignment: timing things that repeat

      Part A is a home investigation; Part B is a 30-mark written paper. Type your attempt first to unlock each model solution.

      Part A · Home investigation: a stone, a thread and a phone stopwatch

      Hang a small stone (or padlock) on a thread so the length from the hook to the middle of the stone is 1.00 m, with a tape mark under its rest position. Pull it aside about 10 cm, release, and when the swing is steady start the stopwatch as it passes the mark, counting “zero”. Time 20 complete oscillations, twice. Halve the length to 0.50 m and repeat. Also time just one oscillation three times at 1.00 m. Find T and f for each length, say what changed, and compare the single-oscillation timings with the 20-oscillation timings. Keep the swing small and hang it where nobody will walk into it.

      Practical task
      Sample answer unlocks after you try

      Sample results: at 1.00 m, t₁ = 40.3 s and t₂ = 40.0 s, mean 40.2 s, so T = 40.2 ÷ 20 = 2.01 s and f = 1 ÷ 2.01 = 0.498 Hz. At 0.50 m, mean 28.4 s, so T = 28.4 ÷ 20 = 1.42 s and f = 1 ÷ 1.42 = 0.704 Hz. Single-oscillation timings at 1.00 m: 1.8 s, 2.3 s, 2.0 s.

      The shorter thread gave the shorter period and the higher frequency; only the length was changed, so it was a fair test. The three single timings scatter by several tenths of a second while the two 20-oscillation timings agree within about 0.3 s: the reaction-time error is shared out when many cycles are timed, just as Zainab timed 20 bounces of the okada rather than one. A source of error: the stone swung slightly in an ellipse, or a draught from the window nudged it.

      Part B · Written paper (30 marks)

      b1. Define: (a) periodic motion; (b) oscillation; (c) frequency of an oscillation.

      3 marks
      Solution unlocks after you try

      (a) Periodic motion is motion that repeats itself in equal intervals of time. (1)
      (b) An oscillation (vibration) is a periodic to-and-fro motion about a fixed rest (equilibrium) position. (1)
      (c) Frequency is the number of complete oscillations made in one second; its unit is the hertz (Hz = s⁻¹). (1)

      Mark scheme: (a) needs equal time intervals; (b) needs both to-and-fro and the fixed rest position — “moving up and down” alone scores zero; (c) needs “complete” oscillations per second.

      b2. A pendulum bob swings between end-points A and B through its rest position O. (a) Define the amplitude and show it on a sketch of A, O and B. (b) Define the period. (c) Write the relation between angular frequency ω and period T, with the unit of ω.

      3 marks
      Solution unlocks after you try

      (a) Amplitude is the greatest displacement of the bob from its rest position; on the sketch it is OA (or OB), not AB. (1)
      (b) The period is the time taken for one complete oscillation (A → O → B → O → A). (1)
      (c) ω = 2π/T (= 2πf), in radians per second (rad s⁻¹). (1)

      Mark scheme: marking AB as the amplitude loses (a); in (c) both the relation and the unit are needed.

      b3. When finding the period of a pendulum, explain why a careful student (a) times 20 oscillations, not one; (b) starts the stopwatch as the bob passes the rest position; (c) counts “zero”, not “one”, at the start. (d) State what would go wrong with T if the student counted half-swings as complete oscillations.

      4 marks
      Solution unlocks after you try

      (a) The reaction-time error on pressing the stopwatch is about the same however long the timing is, so dividing by 20 makes it twenty times smaller as a fraction of T. (1)
      (b) The bob moves fastest at the rest position, so the instant it passes the mark is sharp; at the ends it lingers. (1)
      (c) Counting “one” at the start means only 19 oscillations are really timed when the count reaches 20, so T comes out too small. (1)
      (d) Each count would be only half an oscillation, so the T obtained would be half the true period. (1)

      Mark scheme: “to be accurate” without the reaction-time idea earns nothing; “because the bob stops there” in (b) is the misconception reversed and scores zero.

      b4. (a) A body makes n complete oscillations in time t. Show that its frequency is f = n/t and that f = 1/T. (b) Show that the distance covered in one complete oscillation is 4A, and hence write an expression for the distance covered in time t.

      4 marks
      Solution unlocks after you try

      (a) One oscillation takes T = t/n, so the number of oscillations in one second is n ÷ t: f = n/t. (1) Since T = t/n, n/t = 1/T, so f = 1/T. (1)
      (b) In one oscillation the body goes O → end (A), back to O (A), to the other end (A) and back to O (A): total 4A. (1) In time t there are t/T oscillations, so distance = (t/T) × 4A = 4Aft. (1)

      b5. A generator sits on rubber mounts. When it is switched off, Mallam Sule pushes it down and lets go: its frame bobs between the 12.0 cm and 18.0 cm marks on a ruler and makes 45 complete oscillations in 27.0 s. Calculate (a) the period; (b) the frequency; (c) the angular frequency; (d) the amplitude; (e) the total distance the frame moves in the first 6.0 s, assuming the amplitude stays the same.

      5 marks
      Solution unlocks after you try

      (a) T = t/n = 27.0 ÷ 45 = 0.600 s (1)
      (b) f = 1/T = 1 ÷ 0.600 = 1.67 Hz (1)
      (c) ω = 2π/T = 6.283 ÷ 0.600 = 10.5 rad s⁻¹ (1)
      (d) End to end = 18.0 − 12.0 = 6.0 cm, so A = 6.0 ÷ 2 = 3.0 cm (1)
      (e) N = 6.0 ÷ 0.600 = 10 oscillations; s = 10 × 12.0 = 120 cm (1.20 m) (1)

      Mark scheme: error carried forward allowed from (a); using 18.0 cm or 6.0 cm as A loses (d); units required in every part.

      b6. Zainab timed a pendulum three ways: n = 10, t = 17.4 s; n = 20, t = 35.0 s; n = 30, t = 52.4 s. (a) Calculate T for each row to 3 s.f. (b) Find the mean period. (c) Using the 20-oscillation value, calculate f and ω. (d) Assuming a reaction error of 0.2 s on each timing, calculate the percentage error in T for the 10- and 30-oscillation timings, and say which timing is most reliable.

      6 marks
      Solution unlocks after you try

      (a) 17.4 ÷ 10 = 1.74 s; 35.0 ÷ 20 = 1.75 s; 52.4 ÷ 30 = 1.75 s (1.747 s) (1)
      (b) T = (1.74 + 1.75 + 1.747) ÷ 3 = 1.75 s (1)
      (c) f = 1 ÷ 1.75 = 0.571 Hz (1); ω = 2π ÷ 1.75 = 3.59 rad s⁻¹ (1)
      (d) 10 oscillations: (0.2 ÷ 17.4) × 100 = 1.1 %; 30 oscillations: (0.2 ÷ 52.4) × 100 = 0.38 % (1). The 30-oscillation timing is most reliable, because the same reaction error is the smallest fraction of the time measured. (1)

      b7. (a) State two precautions Zainab took when timing the pendulum. (b) State one source of error that a precaution cannot remove completely.

      3 marks
      Solution unlocks after you try

      (a) Any two, in the past tense: I started and stopped the stopwatch as the bob passed the rest mark · I counted “zero” at the start · I timed 20 oscillations and repeated the timing · I displaced the bob only slightly · I ensured the bob swung in one plane. (2)
      (b) Reaction time in starting and stopping the stopwatch (it can only be reduced, by timing many oscillations), or air resistance slowly reducing the amplitude. (1)

      Mark scheme: precautions written as commands or in the future tense score zero; a precaution offered as an error scores zero.

      b8. Tunde timed one bounce of the parked okada and got 0.7 s. Zainab timed 20 bounces in 10.0 s. (a) Find Zainab's period. (b) Explain why Tunde's value should not be trusted.

      2 marks
      Solution unlocks after you try

      (a) T = 10.0 ÷ 20 = 0.50 s (1)
      (b) Tunde's reading includes a reaction error of a few tenths of a second, a large fraction (about 40 %) of a period of only 0.5 s; Zainab's error is shared over 20 bounces, so hers is far more reliable. (1)

      TTeacher notes

      For the teacher

      • Timetable: 0–5 Do Now · 5–15 Wonder · 15–40 Investigate · 40–45 results discussion · 45–50 movement break (“human pendulum”: sway arms to a clap, then double the clap rate) · 50–65 Make Sense · 65–75 Apply · 75–80 Reflect.
      • Competences: L1 predict, then test timing methods · L2 farm-to-market trade; local oscillators such as the swing and the talking drum · L4 tables, units, significant figures · L5 the virtual stopwatch and bench.
      • Bench: “Time n” is the ideal stopwatch (starts at the rest mark counting zero); “Start / Stop” is hand timing with a modelled reaction delay of 0.1–0.3 s per press. Use “Fast ×4” if class time is short.
      • Support: give the counting script “0, 1, 2 …” and let weaker students record t only. Stretch: estimate the frequency of a ceiling fan's blades passing a point.
      • Story model: the okada numbers (200 kg, T = 0.50 s, f = 2.0 Hz) are a constructed teaching model, used consistently in all seven lessons.
