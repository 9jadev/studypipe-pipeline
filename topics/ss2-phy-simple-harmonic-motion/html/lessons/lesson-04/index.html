The Simple Pendulum

Does a heavier bob or a bigger swing change the timing?

  • 01Identify the restoring force on a pendulum bob (mg sin θ) and explain why the motion is SHM only for small angles.
  • 02Show that a = −(g/l)x, so T = 2π√(l/g).
  • 03Show by fair tests that T does not depend on bob mass or (small) amplitude, but depends on l and g.
  • 04Solve ratio problems (T ∝ √l, T ∝ 1/√g), including the seconds pendulum and the Moon.
  • 05Contrast the pendulum (mass has no effect) with the spring (T ∝ √m).

Model answers unlocks after you try
  1. Only the length and g changed T. Bob mass (50 g and 100 g both 28.44 s) and small angles (5° and 10°: 28.40 s and 28.44 s) made no real difference.
  2. At 30° sin θ is noticeably smaller than θ (about 4.5 % smaller), so the restoring force grows less than in proportion to the displacement and the bob takes a little longer: 28.88 s against 28.40 s at 5°, about 1.7 % longer.

3Make sense · 15 min

Why only length and gravity count

Now we connect the bench to the formula WAEC, NECO and JAMB expect — and see exactly why WAEC says “small angle”.

Forces on the bob: the weight mg splits into mg cos θ along the thread (balanced by the tension) and mg sin θ along the arc, pulling the bob back towards the lowest point. That component is the restoring force.

The small-angle step: for small θ (in radians), sin θ ≈ θ ≈ x/l, so the restoring force is F ≈ −mg(x/l) and a = F/m = −(g/l)x. The mass cancels. This matches a = −ω²x with ω² = g/l, so T = 2π√(l/g). For large angles sin θ falls noticeably below θ, the force is no longer proportional to x, and the period grows.

The equations
F = −mg sin θ
a ≈ −(g/l)x
ω² = g/l
T = 2π√(l/g)
T₂/T₁ = √(l₂/l₁)
l = gT²/4π²

4Apply · Worked examples · 10 min

Worked examples, step by step

Try each on paper first, then check the working. Watch the square roots — most lost marks come from leaving one out.

EXAMPLE 1

The period of a 0.90 m pendulum

Find the period of a simple pendulum 0.90 m long (g = 9.8 m s⁻²).

  1. Write the formula and substitute: T = 2π√(0.90 ÷ 9.8)
  2. Divide inside the root: T = 2π√0.0918 = 2 × 3.142 × 0.303
  3. Multiply: T = 1.90 s
T ≈ 1.90 s
EXAMPLE 2

A ratio: lengthening the thread

A pendulum 0.40 m long has period 1.27 s. The thread is lengthened to 0.90 m. Find the new period.

  1. Use T ∝ √l: T₂/T₁ = √(0.90 ÷ 0.40)
  2. Evaluate the root: T₂/T₁ = √2.25 = 1.5
  3. Multiply by the old period: T₂ = 1.5 × 1.27 = 1.90 s
T₂ ≈ 1.90 s (agrees with Example 1)
EXAMPLE 3

The same pendulum on the Moon

A pendulum has period 2.0 s on Earth. Find its period on the Moon, where g is one-sixth of Earth's.

  1. Use T ∝ 1/√g: T_M/T_E = √(g_E/g_M) = √6
  2. Evaluate the root: √6 = 2.45
  3. Multiply: T_M = 2.0 × 2.45 = 4.9 s
T ≈ 4.9 s
EXAMPLE 4

The seconds pendulum

A “seconds pendulum” ticks once per second, so its period is 2.0 s. Find its length (g = 9.8 m s⁻²).

  1. Rearrange T = 2π√(l/g): l = gT²/4π²
  2. Substitute: l = 9.8 × 2.0² ÷ 39.5 = 39.2 ÷ 39.5
  3. Divide: l = 0.99 m
l ≈ 0.99 m

Part A · Home investigation: key against padlock

Does mass change the swing?

You need: two pieces of thread about 80 cm long, a light key, a heavy padlock, a broom handle or rod laid across two chairs, tape, a tape measure and a phone stopwatch.

  1. Tie the key to one thread and the padlock to the other. Hang both from the rod so that each length, from the rod to the middle of the object, is the same (say 60 cm). Keep them about 30 cm apart.
  2. Pull both aside by the same small angle (about 10°, roughly 10 cm sideways) and release them together. Watch for 20 swings: do they stay in step?
  3. Time 20 oscillations of each separately, twice.
  4. Now shorten the key's thread to 30 cm and release both together again. Watch which one gets ahead.

A good answer shows that the key and the padlock at the same length stay in step and have the same period within timing error (about 1.56 s), so the mass of the bob does not change T, which disproves Tunde's “heavier is steadier”; halving the length shortens the period by a factor of about √2 (1.56 ÷ 1.10 ≈ 1.4), so length does change it. A strong answer explains why mass cancels (bigger weight but also bigger inertia), notes that the angle was kept small and the same for a fair test, and names a source of error: the lengths were measured to the top of the objects rather than their centres, or a draught pushed the light key more than the padlock.

Part B · Written paper (30 marks)

1. Definitions

3 marks

Define: (a) a simple pendulum; (b) a seconds pendulum; (c) the effective length of a simple pendulum.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) A small heavy bob hung from a fixed point by a light, inextensible thread, free to swing to and fro in a vertical plane. (1) (b) A simple pendulum that takes exactly 2 s for one complete oscillation (each swing from one end to the other takes 1 s). (1) (c) The distance from the point of suspension to the centre of mass (centre) of the bob. (1)

Mark scheme note: In (a) “light thread” and “heavy (small) bob” are both needed. In (b) “period 1 s” is a common error and scores zero. In (c) “the length of the thread” alone is not accepted.

2. What Sets the Period?

3 marks

(a) Name the two quantities on which the period of a simple pendulum depends. (b) Name two quantities that do not affect it (for small swings). (c) Why must the swing be small?

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) The length l and the acceleration due to gravity g. (1) (b) The mass of the bob and the amplitude (angle) of a small swing; also accept the material or colour of the bob. (1) (c) Only for small angles is sin θ ≈ θ, so that the restoring force is proportional to displacement and the motion is SHM. (1)

Mark scheme note: Both answers are needed in (a) and in (b). “Small angles are safer” scores zero in (c).

3. Deriving T = 2π√(l/g) (extension)

5 marks

A bob of mass m on a thread of length l is displaced through a small angle θ, its displacement along the arc being x. (a) Draw and label the forces on the bob. (b) Write the restoring force. (c) Using the small-angle approximation, show that a = −(g/l)x. (d) Hence show that the motion is SHM and that T = 2π√(l/g). (e) Explain why the mass of the bob does not appear.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) Weight mg vertically down and tension along the thread towards the support; mg resolved into mg cos θ along the thread and mg sin θ along the arc. (1) (b) F = −mg sin θ (towards the lowest point). (1) (c) For small θ in radians, sin θ ≈ θ = x/l, so F ≈ −mg(x/l) and a = F/m = −(g/l)x. (1) (d) g/l is a positive constant, so a ∝ −x: SHM with ω² = g/l; T = 2π/ω = 2π√(l/g). (1) (e) m appears in both the restoring force and in F = ma, so it cancels. (1)

Mark scheme note: The small-angle step (sin θ ≈ θ = x/l) must be stated for (c). A formula quoted without derivation earns only (d) if the comparison with a = −ω²x is shown.

4. A Long Pendulum

4 marks

A pendulum in a school hall is 1.21 m long. (Take g = 9.8 m s⁻².) Calculate: (a) its period; (b) the time for 50 oscillations; (c) the length needed for a period of 1.10 s; (d) the frequency of the 1.10 s pendulum.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) T = 2π√(1.21 ÷ 9.8) = 2π × 0.3514 = 2.21 s (1) (b) t = 50 × 2.208 = 110 s (1) (c) l = 9.8 × 1.21 ÷ 39.48 = 0.300 m (1) (d) f = 1 ÷ 1.10 = 0.909 Hz (1)

Mark scheme note: Error carried forward into (b). In (c) forgetting to square T gives 0.273 m and loses the mark. Units required.

5. Earth and Moon

5 marks

A clock pendulum has a period of 2.40 s on Earth (g = 9.8 m s⁻²). (a) Calculate its length. (b) Calculate its period on the Moon, where g is one-sixth of Earth's. (c) What length would give a period of 2.40 s on the Moon? (d) Explain why a pendulum clock taken to the Moon without adjustment would show the wrong time, and say whether it would gain or lose.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) l = 9.8 × 5.76 ÷ 39.48 = 1.43 m (1) (b) T_M = 2.40 × 2.449 = 5.88 s (1) (c) For the same T, l ∝ g, so l_M = 1.43 ÷ 6 = 0.238 m (0.24 m). (1) (d) Each swing takes longer, so the clock ticks fewer times per real hour (1): it loses time (runs slow). (1)

Mark scheme note: In (b) multiplying by 6 (14.4 s) loses the mark. In (c) “l × 6” is the inverse error. In (d) “gains” scores zero for the last mark.

7. Pendulum or Spring?

3 marks

For each change, say whether the period of (i) a simple pendulum and (ii) a mass on a spring increases, decreases or stays the same: (a) the mass of the bob or load is doubled; (b) the system is taken to the Moon; (c) the amplitude is halved (still small).

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) Pendulum: same; spring: increases (×√2). (1) (b) Pendulum: increases (×√6); spring: same. (1) (c) Both stay the same. (1)

Mark scheme note: Each part needs both systems right for its mark.

8. The Bridge: The 6.2 cm Pendulum

2 marks

Mrs Okafor's short pendulum keeps time with the okada's bounce (T = 0.50 s). (Take g = 9.8 m s⁻².) (a) Calculate its length. (b) Show that it equals the okada's static sag, mg/k, with m = 200 kg and k = 3.16 × 10⁴ N m⁻¹.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) l = 9.8 × 0.25 ÷ 39.48 = 0.062 m (6.2 cm). (1) (b) mg/k = 200 × 9.8 ÷ 31600 = 0.062 m: the same, because the two periods are equal when l/g = m/k. (1)

Mark scheme note: In (b) the numerical check alone earns the mark; the l/g = m/k reason is credit-worthy but not required. Story numbers are a teaching model, not real measurements.
