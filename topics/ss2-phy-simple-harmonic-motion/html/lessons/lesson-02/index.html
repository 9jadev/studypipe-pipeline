Practical analysis questions

Answer these five questions, then compare with the model answers.

  1. Why does equal added mass give equal added extension?
  2. What does the slope of the F–x graph give?
  3. Why is T², not T, plotted against m?
  4. How does k follow from the T²–m slope?
  5. What would a stiffer spring do to the T²–m line?
Model answers unlocks after you try
  1. Each 50 g adds the same weight (0.49 N), and within the elastic limit the extension is proportional to the force (Hooke's law), so each step adds the same 2.0 cm.
  2. Its slope, ΔF ÷ Δx, is the force per metre of extension: the spring constant k (24.5 N m⁻¹ here).
  3. T = 2π√(m/k) is not a straight line against m, but squaring gives T² = (4π²/k) m, which is a straight line through the origin.
  4. The slope of T² against m is 4π²/k, so k = 4π² ÷ slope = 39.48 ÷ 1.61 = 24.5 N m⁻¹.
  5. A larger k makes 4π²/k smaller, so the line would still pass through the origin but with a smaller slope (shorter periods).

3Make sense · 15 min

The pull back to the middle: Hooke, F = −kx and a = −ω²x

Now we put names and equations on what the bench showed.

Hooke's law: provided the elastic limit is not exceeded, the extension of a spring is directly proportional to the stretching force: F = kx. The spring constant k (N m⁻¹) is the force needed for each metre of extension.

Restoring force and the SHM condition. Displace the mass from equilibrium and the spring pushes or pulls it back with F = −kx: proportional to the displacement and opposite to it. Newton's second law gives a = F/m = −(k/m)x. Any motion with a = −ω²x (ω² a constant) is simple harmonic: the acceleration is proportional to the displacement from a fixed point and always directed towards that point.

The equations

F = kx · F = −kx
a = −(k/m)x · a = −ω²x so ω² = k/m
T = 2π√(m/k) f = (1/2π)√(k/m)
T² = (4π²/k) m slope of T² against m = 4π²/k

4Apply · Worked examples · 10 min

Worked examples, step by step

Try each problem on paper first, then check the working. Take g = 9.8 m s⁻².

EXAMPLE 1

Finding k, then T

A 0.30 kg load stretches a spring by 6.0 cm. Find k and the period when the load bounces.

  1. Stretching force: F = mg = 0.30 × 9.8 = 2.94 N, and x = 6.0 cm = 0.060 m.
  2. Spring constant: k = F ÷ x = 2.94 ÷ 0.060 = 49 N m⁻¹.
  3. Substitute into the period: T = 2π√(0.30 ÷ 49) = 2π√0.00612.
  4. Evaluate: T = 2 × 3.142 × 0.0782 = 0.49 s.
k = 49 N m⁻¹ · T = 0.49 s
EXAMPLE 2

The SHM condition

For the spring in Example 1, find the acceleration of the load when it is 2.0 cm below equilibrium, and the angular frequency.

  1. Use a = −(k/m)x: k ÷ m = 49 ÷ 0.30 = 163.
  2. Acceleration: a = −163 × 0.020 = −3.3 m s⁻²; the minus sign means it is directed upwards, towards equilibrium.
  3. Angular frequency: ω = √163 = 12.8 rad s⁻¹.
a = 3.3 m s⁻² towards equilibrium · ω = 12.8 rad s⁻¹
EXAMPLE 3

The okada, 200 kg then 250 kg

The okada's rear spring has k = 3.16 × 10⁴ N m⁻¹. Find the bounce period with 200 kg and with 250 kg on it.

  1. 200 kg: T₁ = 2π√(200 ÷ 31600) = 2π√0.00633.
  2. Evaluate: T₁ = 2 × 3.142 × 0.0796 = 0.50 s, so f = 2.0 Hz.
  3. 250 kg: T₂ = 2π√(250 ÷ 31600) = 2π√0.00791.
  4. Evaluate: T₂ = 2 × 3.142 × 0.0889 = 0.56 s, so f = 1.79 Hz.
T₁ = 0.50 s · T₂ = 0.56 s

The heavier load bounces more slowly, not less.

Story numbers are a teaching model, not real measurements.

EXAMPLE 4

A ratio problem

A load of 0.20 kg on a spring has period 0.60 s. Find the period with 0.45 kg on the same spring.

  1. Ratio rule: T ∝ √m, so T₂ ÷ T₁ = √(m₂ ÷ m₁).
  2. Substitute: T₂ ÷ T₁ = √(0.45 ÷ 0.20) = √2.25 = 1.5.
  3. New period: T₂ = 1.5 × 0.60 = 0.90 s.
T₂ = 0.90 s

Part A · Home investigation: does a spring stretch evenly?

Keys on a spring (or a rubber band)

You need: a spring from an old pen or a strong rubber band, a hook or nail, a light plastic bag or cup with a string handle, six identical keys (or coins), a 30 cm ruler and tape.

  1. Hang the spring or band from the hook and tape the ruler upright beside it. Hang the empty cup and note where its bottom edge is on the ruler.
  2. Add one key at a time, up to six. After each, wait for the cup to stop and read the position with your eye level with the edge.
  3. Work out the extension (stretch beyond the empty-cup reading) for each number of keys.
  4. Remove all the keys and check whether the cup returns to its first reading.
  5. Plot extension (vertical) against number of keys (horizontal).

For the spring, each extra key adds roughly the same extension (about 0.9 cm), so the graph is a straight line through the origin: extension ∝ load, which is Hooke's law. It returned to 8.0 cm, so the elastic limit was not passed. A rubber band usually gives a curve and may not return exactly, so it obeys Hooke's law poorly. This even pull-back is why a spring gives SHM, like the okada's rear spring. One error with its effect: reading the ruler from above made each reading slightly too small (parallax); a swinging cup made readings hard.

Part B · Written paper (30 marks)

1. Definitions

3 marks

Define: (a) the spring constant of a spring; (b) the elastic limit; (c) restoring force.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) The force needed to produce unit extension (k = F/x); unit N m⁻¹. (1) (b) The greatest load a spring can carry and still return to its original length when the load is removed. (1) (c) A force on a displaced body that acts back towards its equilibrium position. (1)

Mark scheme note: "the stiffness of the spring" alone scores zero; "the point where it breaks" is the breaking point, not the elastic limit; in (c) the direction towards equilibrium is essential.

2. The SHM condition

3 marks

(a) State the condition for a body to move with simple harmonic motion. (b) Write it as an equation and explain the minus sign. (c) Give two examples, other than a loaded spring, of systems that move with SHM.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) The acceleration is directly proportional to the displacement from a fixed (equilibrium) point and always directed towards that point. (1) (b) a = −ω²x; the minus sign shows the acceleration is always opposite in direction to the displacement. (1) (c) Any two: a small-angle simple pendulum · a weighted test-tube bobbing in a liquid · the prong of a struck tuning fork · a plucked string. (1)

Mark scheme note: both "proportional" and "towards the fixed point" are needed in (a); "repeats its motion" scores zero; a bouncing ball is not accepted in (c).

3. Deriving the spring period

4 marks

A mass m hangs on a light spring of spring constant k and is pulled down a further distance x below equilibrium. (a) Write the restoring force. (b) Use Newton's second law to find the acceleration. (c) Hence show the motion is SHM and that T = 2π√(m/k).

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) The extra stretch x gives an extra upward spring force kx, so F = −kx. (1) (b) a = F/m = −(k/m)x. (1) (c) k/m is a positive constant, so a ∝ x and opposite to it: this is a = −ω²x with ω² = k/m, so the motion is SHM. (1) Then ω = √(k/m) and T = 2π/ω = 2π√(m/k). (1)

Mark scheme note: the minus sign must appear in (a) or (b); writing the formula from memory without the comparison earns only the last mark.

4. From extension to period

4 marks

A 0.20 kg mass stretches a spring by 4.0 cm. A 0.50 kg mass is then hung on the same spring and set bouncing (g = 10 m s⁻²). Calculate (a) k; (b) the period with 0.50 kg; (c) its frequency; (d) the size of its acceleration 2.0 cm from equilibrium.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) k = 2.0 ÷ 0.040 = 50 N m⁻¹ (1) (b) T = 2π√(0.50 ÷ 50) = 0.628 s (1) (c) f = 1 ÷ 0.628 = 1.59 Hz (1) (d) a = 100 × 0.020 = 2.0 m s⁻², towards equilibrium. (1)

Mark scheme note: error carried forward from (a); using 0.20 kg in (b) gives 0.40 s and loses the mark; x left in cm loses that mark; units required.

5. The okada's two loads

5 marks

The okada's rear spring has k = 3.16 × 10⁴ N m⁻¹ (g = 9.8 m s⁻²). (a) Calculate the static sag with 200 kg. (b) Calculate the bounce period with 200 kg and with 250 kg. (c) Find the ratio of the periods and show it equals √(250/200). (d) How long do 20 bounces take with 250 kg?

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) x = 1960 ÷ 31600 = 0.062 m (6.2 cm). (1) (b) T₁ = 2π × 0.0796 = 0.50 s (1); T₂ = 2π × 0.0889 = 0.56 s. (1) (c) T₂/T₁ = 0.559 ÷ 0.500 = 1.12 and √1.25 = 1.12, the same, because T ∝ √m. (1) (d) t = 20 × 0.559 = 11.2 s. (1)

Mark scheme note: using 200 kg as the force in (a) loses the mark; the √(m₂/m₁) statement must be shown in (c); accept 0.559 s or 0.56 s. Story numbers are a teaching model, not real measurements.

6. Practical: k from a T²–m graph

6 marks

Zainab timed 20 oscillations for five loads: m / kg 0.100, 0.200, 0.300, 0.400, 0.500 and t / s 7.02, 9.94, 12.16, 14.06, 15.72. (a) Add rows for T (3 d.p.) and T² (3 d.p.). (b) State what goes on each axis and the shape of graph expected. (c) Using (0.100 kg, 0.123 s²) and (0.500 kg, 0.617 s²) from the best-fit line, find the slope. (d) Deduce k.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) T = t/20: 0.351, 0.497, 0.608, 0.703, 0.786 s (1); T²: 0.123, 0.247, 0.370, 0.494, 0.618 s². (1) (b) T² vertical, m horizontal; a straight line through the origin. (1) (c) slope = 0.494 ÷ 0.400 = 1.235 s² kg⁻¹ (2) (d) k = 39.48 ÷ 1.235 = 32 N m⁻¹. (1)

Mark scheme note: accept T² within ±0.002 s²; an inverted slope (Δm/ΔT²) loses both marks in (c); "k = slope" scores zero; accept 31.5–32.5 N m⁻¹ on error carried forward.

7. Practical: precautions and errors

3 marks

(a) State two precautions taken in the spiral-spring experiment. (b) The spring itself has some mass. Does this make the measured periods slightly too long or too short? Why?

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) Any two (1 each): I read the pointer with my eye level with it to avoid parallax · I ensured the spring was not loaded beyond its elastic limit · I pulled the load down only slightly so it bounced vertically without swinging · I started timing as the load passed the equilibrium position and repeated each timing. 8. The bridge: "heavier means steadier"

2 marks

Dayo says that loading two extra crates makes the okada "steadier, so it won't bounce". Using T = 2π√(m/k), give two reasons why this is wrong.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(1) The spring still provides a restoring force proportional to displacement, so the loaded okada still oscillates in SHM; extra mass does not remove the bounce. (1) (2) Extra mass makes the period longer (0.50 s → 0.56 s) and the frequency lower (2.0 Hz → 1.79 Hz): the bounce becomes slower, not smaller. (1)

Mark scheme note: "heavy things still bounce" without a reason linked to the restoring force or to T ∝ √m scores zero.
