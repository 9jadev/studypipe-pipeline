3Make sense · 15 min

Energy sloshes; damping drains it

Now we connect the bars and the curves to the formulas WAEC, NECO and JAMB expect.

Energy interchange: in SHM without losses, the total energy stays fixed while it changes form — all potential at the ends, all kinetic at the centre. For a spring, E = ½kA²; at displacement x, PE = ½kx² and KE = ½k(A² − x²). Since k = mω², E = ½mω²A². The total energy is proportional to A²: double the amplitude, four times the energy.

Damping: a resistive force (air, a liquid, friction) takes energy out of the oscillation every cycle, so the amplitude dies away. Light damping: many shrinking oscillations; the period is almost unchanged. Heavy damping: a slow creep back to equilibrium with no oscillation. Critical damping: the quickest return to rest without overshooting — what a good shock absorber and a moving-coil meter's needle aim for.

The equations
E = ½kA² = ½mω²A²
PE = ½kx² · KE = ½k(A² − x²)
KE = PE at x = ±A/√2
E = mgh = ½mv_max²

4Apply · Worked examples · 10 min

Worked examples, step by step

Try each on paper first, then check the working. Keep amplitudes in metres.

EXAMPLE 1

Energy at a point

A 0.50 kg mass on a spring of k = 50 N m⁻¹ oscillates with A = 0.10 m. Find the total energy, and PE, KE and speed at x = 0.060 m.

  1. Total energy: E = ½kA² = ½ × 50 × 0.10² = 0.25 J
  2. Potential energy at x: PE = ½kx² = ½ × 50 × 0.060² = 0.090 J
  3. Kinetic energy: KE = E − PE = 0.25 − 0.090 = 0.16 J
  4. Speed: v = √(2KE/m) = √(2 × 0.16 ÷ 0.50) = √0.64 = 0.80 m s⁻¹ (check: ω = 10 rad s⁻¹, v = 10√(0.010 − 0.0036) = 0.80)
E = 0.25 J · PE = 0.090 J · KE = 0.16 J · v = 0.80 m s⁻¹
EXAMPLE 2

Where is KE = PE?

For the same oscillator, where is the kinetic energy equal to the potential energy?

  1. Each is half the total: ½kx² = ½ × ½kA²
  2. Simplify: x² = A²/2, so x = A/√2
  3. Substitute: x = 0.10 ÷ 1.414 = 0.071 m
x = ±0.071 m (about 71 % of the way out, not halfway)
EXAMPLE 3

Pendulum energy

A 0.10 kg bob is released from 5.0 cm above its lowest point. Find its total energy and its greatest speed (g = 9.8 m s⁻²).

  1. Total energy: E = mgh = 0.10 × 9.8 × 0.050 = 0.049 J
  2. All KE at the lowest point: ½mv² = 0.049 J
  3. Greatest speed: v = √(2gh) = √(2 × 9.8 × 0.050) = √0.98 = 0.99 m s⁻¹
E = 0.049 J · v_max ≈ 0.99 m s⁻¹
EXAMPLE 4

The okada's bounce energy

For the okada's rear (k = 3.16 × 10⁴ N m⁻¹), compare the energy in a gentle 2.0 cm bounce with that in a 6.2 cm lift-off bounce.

  1. Gentle bounce: E₁ = ½ × 31600 × 0.020² = 6.3 J
  2. Lift-off bounce: E₂ = ½ × 31600 × 0.062² = 61 J
  3. Ratio: E₂/E₁ = (6.2/2.0)² = 9.6
about 6.3 J against 61 J — nearly ten times as much energy must build up for lift-off

Story numbers are a teaching model, not real measurements.

Part A · Home investigation: whose shock absorbers are tired?

Bounce test on two parked vehicles

You need: two parked vehicles (cars, a keke or motorcycles) with the owners' permission, a friend to watch, and a notebook. Each vehicle must be switched off, in gear or on its stand, with nobody in it or behind it.

  1. Press down firmly on one corner (or the back of a motorcycle seat) and let go quickly.
  2. Your friend counts how many times that corner rises and falls before it settles, and estimates the height of the first rebound.
  3. Repeat three times for the same corner, then do the same on the second vehicle.
  4. Ask each owner how old the shock absorbers are, if they know.

Part B · Written paper (30 marks)

1. Definitions

3 marks

Define: (a) damping; (b) critical damping; (c) a free oscillation.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) Damping is the gradual loss of energy from an oscillating system, caused by resistive forces such as friction or air or liquid resistance, so that the amplitude decreases. (1) (b) Critical damping is the amount of damping that returns a displaced system to its equilibrium position in the shortest possible time without oscillating. (1) (c) A free oscillation is one in which the system vibrates at its own natural frequency with no outside periodic force and no loss of energy, so its amplitude stays constant. (1)

Mark scheme note: In (a) both “energy loss” (or a resistive force) and “amplitude decreases” are expected. In (b) “without oscillating” and “shortest time” are both needed. In (c) “natural frequency” or “constant amplitude” must appear.

2. Energy Round a Swing

3 marks

A pendulum bob swings from end-point A through its lowest point O to the other end B. Describe the energy at A, at O, and halfway in energy between them, assuming no losses.

Model solution unlocks after you try

At A (and B) the bob is momentarily at rest: all its energy is potential, PE is greatest and KE is zero. (1) At O it moves fastest: all its energy is kinetic, KE is greatest and PE is least (zero, taking O as the reference level). (1) In between, the energy is shared, with KE + PE always equal to the same total; they are equal at x = A/√2. (1)

Mark scheme note: “Total energy is constant” must appear in some form for the third mark.

3. Deriving the Energy Expressions

4 marks

For a mass m on a spring of constant k oscillating with amplitude A: (a) using v = ω√(A² − x²) and ω² = k/m, show that KE = ½k(A² − x²); (b) show that KE + PE = ½kA², a constant; (c) show that KE = PE where x = A/√2.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) KE = ½mv² = ½mω²(A² − x²) (1); since mω² = k, KE = ½k(A² − x²). (1) (b) PE = ½kx², so KE + PE = ½kA² − ½kx² + ½kx² = ½kA², which does not depend on x. (1) (c) KE = PE gives ½k(A² − x²) = ½kx², so A² = 2x² and x = A/√2. (1)

Mark scheme note: The substitution mω² = k must be shown in (a). In (c) “x = A/2” scores zero.

4. Energy at a Point

5 marks

A 0.80 kg mass on a spring of spring constant 20 N m⁻¹ oscillates with amplitude 0.15 m. Calculate: (a) the angular frequency; (b) the total energy; (c) the maximum speed; (d) the potential and kinetic energies at x = 0.090 m; (e) the speed at x = 0.090 m.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) ω = √(k/m) = √(20 ÷ 0.80) = √25 = 5.0 rad s⁻¹ (1) (b) E = ½kA² = ½ × 20 × 0.0225 = 0.225 J (1) (c) v_max = ωA = 5.0 × 0.15 = 0.75 m s⁻¹ (check: √(2 × 0.225 ÷ 0.80) = 0.75) (1) (d) PE = ½ × 20 × 0.0081 = 0.081 J; KE = 0.225 − 0.081 = 0.144 J (1) (e) v = √(2 × 0.144 ÷ 0.80) = √0.36 = 0.60 m s⁻¹ (1)

Mark scheme note: Error carried forward allowed throughout. Both energies are needed for (d). Units required.

5. A Swinging Bob

4 marks

A 0.20 kg bob is pulled aside until it is 4.0 cm above its lowest point, then released (g = 10 m s⁻²). (a) Calculate its total energy. (b) Calculate its greatest speed. (c) Air resistance removes 20 % of its energy by the time it reaches the other side. How high does it rise there? (d) Where did the lost energy go?

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) E = mgh = 0.20 × 10 × 0.040 = 0.080 J (1) (b) v = √(2gh) = √(2 × 10 × 0.040) = √0.80 = 0.89 m s⁻¹ (1) (c) Energy left = 0.80 × 0.080 = 0.064 J; h = E/mg = 0.064 ÷ 2.0 = 0.032 m = 3.2 cm. (1) (d) It was transferred to the surrounding air as heat (internal energy) and a little sound, by the work done against air resistance. (1)

Mark scheme note: In (c) giving 0.8 cm (the 20 % lost, not the height reached) or 4.8 cm loses the mark. In (d) “it was destroyed” scores zero.

6. Practical: Damping in Water

6 marks

A 0.30 kg load on a spring (k = 23.5 N m⁻¹) carried a card vane dipping into a beaker of water. Zainab recorded the amplitude at the end of each cycle: cycle 0 → 6.00 cm, 1 → 4.50, 2 → 3.38, 3 → 2.53, 4 → 1.90, 5 → 1.42 cm. Ten oscillations took 7.10 s with the vane in air and 7.14 s in water.

(a) Show that the amplitude falls by the same ratio each cycle, and find the ratio. (b) What fraction of the energy remains after each cycle? (c) Calculate the energy at cycle 0 and at cycle 5. (d) What do the two timings show about light damping and the period? (e) Describe the shape of a graph of amplitude against cycle number.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) 4.50 ÷ 6.00 = 0.75, 3.38 ÷ 4.50 = 0.75, 2.53 ÷ 3.38 = 0.75 (and so on): a constant ratio of 0.75. (1) (b) E ∝ A², so 0.75² = 0.56 (56 %) remains each cycle and about 44 % is lost. (1) (c) E₀ = ½ × 23.5 × 0.0600² = 0.042 J (1); E₅ = ½ × 23.5 × 0.0142² = 0.0024 J (1) (d) The periods are 0.710 s and 0.714 s: almost the same, so light damping hardly changes the period. (1) (e) A curve that falls steeply at first and then more gently, approaching zero but never quite reaching it (an exponential-type decay), not a straight line. (1)

Mark scheme note: In (a) at least three ratios must be shown. In (b) giving 0.75 (forgetting E ∝ A²) scores zero. In (c) amplitudes in centimetres lose the mark. In (e) “a straight line downwards” is not accepted.

7. The Shock Absorber

3 marks

(a) Explain how an oil-filled shock absorber removes energy from a vehicle's bounce. (b) Why are shock absorbers designed to be close to critical damping rather than heavily damped? (c) Give one other device designed to be critically damped.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) As the suspension moves, a piston is forced through oil; the oil is pushed through small holes, and the work done against this resistance turns the bounce energy into heat in the oil. (1) (b) Heavy damping would make the suspension return too slowly after each bump, so it would not be ready for the next one and the ride would be harsh; critical damping returns it quickly without bouncing. (1) (c) The pointer of a moving-coil meter (a door closer is also accepted). (1)

Mark scheme note: In (a) the conversion to heat must be stated. In (c) any correct example is accepted.

8. The Bridge: Mallam Sule's Verdict

2 marks

For the okada's rear spring (k = 3.16 × 10⁴ N m⁻¹), (a) calculate the energy in a 2.0 cm bounce and in a 6.2 cm bounce, and (b) explain why a working shock absorber stops the bounce reaching 6.2 cm.

Model solution unlocks after you try

(a) E₁ = ½ × 31600 × 0.020² = 6.3 J; E₂ = ½ × 31600 × 0.062² = 61 J (nearly ten times as much). (1) (b) The working damper turns a large share of each cycle's energy into heat in its oil, so the road can never pile up the 61 J needed; the leaking one removes very little, so energy can build up when the pushes arrive in step. (1)

Mark scheme note: Both energies are needed for (a). In (b) the energy-removal (heat) idea is essential.
